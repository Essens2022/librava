Wood Burning for Beginners: How to Start Pyrography Safely

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have admired a hand-burned sign or coaster and wondered if you could make one, you almost certainly can. To start wood burning, get a simple burner with a writing tip, practice on bare, untreated pale wood such as basswood, work slowly at low heat in a well-ventilated room, and let heat and time make your marks instead of pressure.

Pyrography (the proper name for wood burning) is patient, quiet and very forgiving once you learn a few habits. This guide covers the tools, the safety rules that matter most, your first practice marks, simple shading and an easy first project.

What you need to start wood burning

The starter kit is small. You need a burner, a stand to rest it in, a few pieces of suitable wood, some fine sandpaper, a pencil and something to transfer designs. Everything else can wait.

Burners come in two families:

FeatureSolid point burnerWire nib burner
PriceLowerHigher
Heat-up timeA few minutesSeconds
Temperature controlNone or basic dialFine dial on a control box
Fine detailHarderEasier

A solid point burner looks like a thick soldering iron with screw-in brass tips. It is affordable and sturdy, which makes it a sensible first tool, though it heats and cools slowly. A wire nib burner has a slim pen and a thin wire tip that responds in seconds, giving finer lines and smoother shading for more money.

For tips, start with three: a writing tip for outlines and lettering, a shader with a flat face for broad areas of tone, and a ball tip for even lines, curves and dots. Those will carry you through your first several months.

Is wood burning safe for beginners

Yes, when you follow a few firm rules. The two real risks are fumes and burns, and both are easy to manage.

For fumes, work in a well-ventilated room with a window open. Use a small fan or fume extractor to pull smoke away from your face, without blowing it toward anyone else, and do not lean directly over the smoke. For long sessions, or if you have asthma or a lung condition, consider a well-fitting respirator rated for fine particles and fumes, and take regular breaks.

For burns and fire:

  • Tips can reach several hundred degrees and stay hot for minutes after unplugging.
  • Always rest the pen in its stand, never on the table.
  • Never leave a plugged-in burner unattended.
  • Unplug before changing tips, and use pliers as the maker instructs.
  • Work on a heat-proof surface, keep the cord clear, and tie back long hair and loose sleeves.
  • Keep a bowl of water or a small fire extinguisher nearby for stray embers.
  • Check cords and plugs for damage, and use equipment made for your country's voltage.

Pyrography is a craft for adults. Older children can try it only with close adult supervision and child-appropriate tools.

If you burn yourself. Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do not use ice, butter or creams. Get medical help for large, deep or blistering burns, burns on the face, hands or joints, or any burn on a child: call 911 (US and Canada), 999 or NHS 111 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand). If smoke ever leaves you wheezing or short of breath, stop and talk to your doctor before your next session.

Best wood for wood burning

Choose bare, untreated natural wood sold for crafts, with a pale, even grain. Pale wood shows your marks clearly, and even grain burns at the same rate across the piece.

WoodHow it burnsBest for
Basswood (lime or linden in the UK)Very even, smoothPractice, detail, shading
BirchEven, pale backgroundPlaques, coasters, rounds
PoplarFair, can be slightly fuzzySigns, budget practice
MapleCrisp, needs more heatSpoons, fine lines
BeechGood, some grain resistanceSpoons, coasters
PineUneven, resin smokesRustic signs only

Some woods and their smoke can irritate skin and lungs or trigger allergies, including cedar, walnut and many exotic hardwoods. Skip them while you learn, and stop working if any wood bothers your skin, eyes or breathing.

Prepare each piece by sanding it lightly with about 220 grit sandpaper until it feels smooth, then wipe off the dust. Wear a dust mask while sanding and clean up afterward. Make sure the wood is dry, since damp wood burns unevenly.

Materials you must never burn

This rule matters more than any technique. Many materials release toxic fumes from glues, resins, preservatives and finishes when heated. Never burn:

  • Treated, painted, stained, varnished or sealed wood
  • Pressure-treated lumber (timber)
  • MDF, particleboard (chipboard) or laminate
  • Construction plywood
  • Reclaimed or salvaged wood when you do not know its history
  • Plastics or synthetic materials of any kind
  • Wood with ink, marker or printed paper on it

If you are not sure a piece is untreated, do not burn it. A shiny surface, a chemical smell or an unusual color are all warning signs.

If you later try leather, burn only vegetable-tanned leather. Chrome-tanned leather can release harmful fumes, so it is never suitable.

How to burn your first lines

Make a practice board from a sanded scrap of basswood or birch. Before every session, spend about five minutes on scrap so the tip reaches a steady temperature and your hand settles in.

  1. Hold the pen like a pencil but further back, at the grip designed to stay cool. Hold it lightly, at roughly 45 degrees for a writing tip.
  2. Anchor the side of your hand or little finger on the wood for control.
  3. Set the temperature low. Temperature dials are not standard between machines, so test on scrap and turn up slowly.
  4. Rest the tip with about the weight of writing gently in pencil. Do not press.
  5. Start moving just before the tip touches the wood, like a small plane landing, and keep moving as you lift off. This avoids dark blobs at each end.
  6. Keep your speed steady through the whole stroke.

To make a line darker, slow down or raise the heat one small step. To make it lighter, speed up or turn the heat down. Change one thing at a time so you know what made the difference.

Pressure makes dents, not dark lines. Pressing harder digs the tip in, bends wire nibs and leaves a blotchy trench. Two or three gentle passes give a smoother, more even tone than one slow, hot pass. If your work is smoking heavily, the tip is almost certainly too hot.

Practice drills for straight lines, curves and dots

Fill your practice board with these drills over a few sessions. They build the control that every project depends on.

  • Straight lines: draw light pencil guides, then pull the pen toward you, moving from your elbow for anything longer than an inch or two. Short overlapping strokes are often neater than one long sweep.
  • Curves: use a ball tip or writing tip and turn the board as you go. Practice gentle arcs, then S shapes, then circles in four quarter turns.
  • Dots: touch straight down, hold, and lift straight up. A quick tap makes a small pale dot, and a slow count of two makes a large dark one.
  • Even tone: burn a row of lines and check that each stays the same shade from end to end.

Solid point burners cool slightly on long strokes, so pause for a few seconds between lines to let the tip recover. Clean the tip gently now and then as carbon builds up, because a dirty tip burns unevenly.

Write the tip and setting on each scrap in pencil. Within a week you will have a reference board that shows exactly how your burner behaves on that wood.

Simple wood burning shading techniques

Shading is what turns a flat outline into something with depth. On wood, tone builds up in layers, so always work from light to dark. You can add darkness but you cannot easily take it away.

  • Shader tip: rest the flat face on the wood and move in small, steady circles or strokes to lay down smooth areas of tone.
  • Layering: burn a light, even base, then go back over the areas you want darker.
  • Stippling: build tone with dots. Close dots look dark, spaced dots look light.
  • Hatching: parallel lines close together for shadow. Cross hatching adds a second layer at an angle for deeper tone.

A great exercise is a value scale. Draw a strip of nine small boxes and shade them from the palest gold to the darkest brown you can get without scorching. It teaches you how many tones your burner can make, and it is the best warm-up for real projects.

Easy wood burning projects for beginners

Small projects let you finish something in an evening while your skills grow. Good first choices:

  • Leaf coasters on birch rounds, traced from a pressed leaf.
  • Bookmarks or gift tags on thin wood, with a simple border and initials.
  • A decorated wooden spoon with a design on the handle only.
  • A house name or welcome sign, once you are comfortable with lettering.

For a first leaf coaster, sand the round, place a real leaf on it and trace around it lightly in pencil, or use graphite transfer paper. Burn the outline with a writing tip, add the veins with lighter strokes, then shade one side of each leaf half to suggest light. Avoid heat transfer of printed images while you learn, because the toner releases fumes of its own.

Add any finish only after burning is complete and the wood has cooled. For spoons, boards and anything that touches food, use only finishes labeled food safe once cured, and keep designs away from cutting surfaces. Rags soaked in oil finishes such as linseed oil can heat up and catch fire on their own, so lay them flat outdoors to dry or keep them in a sealed metal container with water before disposal.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best wood burning tool for a beginner?

A basic solid point burner is a sensible first tool because it is affordable, sturdy and simple. If you know you want fine detail and smooth shading, a wire nib burner with a temperature dial gives more control and heats in seconds. Either way, start with a writing tip, a shader and a ball tip, and test every setting on scrap first.

What temperature should I use for wood burning?

There is no single right number, because dials are not standard between machines and tips. Start low and turn up slowly on a scrap of the same wood until you get a light golden line at a comfortable speed. Lower heat gives cleaner lines and more control, while very high heat causes blotches, pale scorched halos and heavy smoke.

Can I wood burn on plywood or pallet wood?

Avoid both. Construction plywood contains glues that can release harmful fumes, and pallet wood or other reclaimed wood may be treated with chemicals you cannot see. Use bare, untreated craft wood such as basswood, birch, poplar, maple or beech instead. If you do not know a piece of wood's history, the safe choice is not to burn it.

Do I need a mask for wood burning?

Good ventilation comes first: an open window and a small fan or extractor pulling smoke away from your face. For long sessions, or if you have asthma or another lung condition, a well-fitting respirator rated for fine particles and fumes adds protection. Wear a dust mask while sanding. Check with your doctor if smoke bothers your breathing.

How do you fix a mistake in wood burning?

Stop and let the tip rest before you try anything. Light mistakes can often be sanded out gently with fine sandpaper, working only on the mark. Small dark spots can sometimes be scraped carefully with a sharp blade held flat. If a mark will not lift, try turning it into part of the design, such as a leaf, a shadow or a decorative dot.

Can you sell wood burned items?

Yes, many hobbyists sell signs, coasters and gifts at craft fairs and online. Sell only original designs or ones you have permission to use, and never copy other people's artwork, characters or logos for sale. Price to cover your wood, finish and time, and use food safe finishes on anything meant for kitchen use.

Your first step today is to set up a safe corner by an open window, sand one scrap of pale, untreated wood and burn a row of slow, light lines at a low setting. Fifteen minutes of practice marks will teach you more about your burner than any manual, and your first coaster will follow soon after.
