Healthy Eating for Seniors: A Simple Guide to Eating Well After 60

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have noticed your appetite shrinking, food tasting a little flat, or meals slipping into tea and toast, you are far from alone. The short answer to healthy eating after 60: include a protein food at every meal, eat calcium-rich foods across the day, get enough fiber and fluids, flavor food with herbs instead of extra salt, and keep moving as much as you are able.

This guide explains what changes as we get older and what to do about it, with a simple plate, a protein chart, tips for small appetites, easier chewing, food and medicine checks, a sample day of meals and the warning signs that need a doctor.

What changes with food after 60

Several small changes tend to arrive together, and each one nudges how and what we eat.

  • Appetite often gets smaller, so every bite needs to count a little more.
  • Taste and smell can fade, which makes food seem bland and tempts us to add salt.
  • Muscle naturally declines with age. Eating enough protein and staying active, including strength activities as you are able, helps keep muscles working.
  • Bones need calcium and vitamin D, and older skin makes less vitamin D from sunlight.
  • Thirst can weaken, so it is easy to drink too little without noticing.
  • Digestion can slow down, and constipation becomes more common.
  • Teeth, gums or dentures can make tough or chewy foods hard work.
  • Medicines can affect appetite and interact with some foods.

Living alone or caring for a partner changes things too. Cooking for one can feel like too much effort, and bereavement or low mood can quietly take away the wish to eat. Shared meals, lunch clubs and eating with a friend now and then help more than you might expect.

A healthy plate for older adults

You do not need to count anything. Picture your plate in rough sections:

  • About half vegetables, with some fruit across the day.
  • About a quarter a protein food: fish, eggs, chicken, meat, beans, lentils or tofu.
  • About a quarter starchy food, ideally whole grain: oats, whole grain bread, brown rice or potatoes.
  • A dairy food or calcium-rich alternative somewhere in the meal.
  • A drink alongside.

If your appetite is small, shift the balance toward the protein part. A modest plate with a good helping of fish or eggs and a few vegetables does more for you than a large plate that is mostly pasta.

Think about the day, or even the week, as a whole. A light lunch of soup and bread can balance a fuller dinner with plenty of vegetables. Nothing needs to be perfect at every meal.

How much protein do seniors need

Many older adults need at least as much protein as younger adults, and often a little more, yet tend to eat less of it, especially at breakfast and lunch. Protein helps keep muscles strong, supports healing and keeps the immune system working.

The simplest habit is a protein food at every meal, and in some snacks, rather than saving it all for dinner. Studies suggest that spreading protein through the day may help the body use it well.

MealEasy protein ideas
BreakfastEggs, yogurt, milk on oats, cottage cheese, sardines, baked beans
LunchLentil or chicken soup, tuna, cheese, hummus, egg sandwiches
DinnerFish, chicken, lean meat, tofu, beans, lentils
SnacksCheese, yogurt, a glass of milk, peanut butter, a boiled egg

Protein powders are not needed for most people; ordinary food does the job. If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor before eating more protein.

Calcium and vitamin D for strong bones

Calcium builds and maintains bone, and vitamin D helps your body absorb it. A calcium-rich food at each meal is a good aim for most people over 60.

  • Calcium: pasteurized milk, yogurt and cheese, calcium-fortified plant drinks, calcium-set tofu, canned sardines and salmon with the soft bones mashed in, and leafy greens such as kale and broccoli.
  • Vitamin D: sunlight on the skin in the warmer months, oily fish such as salmon, sardines, trout and mackerel, eggs, and fortified spreads, milks and cereals.

Sunlight is weak in autumn and winter and at higher latitudes, and older skin makes less vitamin D. Many countries advise older adults to consider a daily vitamin D supplement, especially in the colder months or if they rarely go outside. Ask your doctor or pharmacist (chemist) whether it is right for you, and check any other supplement with a pharmacist first.

Aim for fish twice a week, with one portion oily. Canned fish counts and is usually cheaper. A quick idea: mash canned sardines with a squeeze of lemon and black pepper and pile onto whole grain toast for a breakfast or lunch with protein, calcium and vitamin D in one.

Fiber, fluids and keeping digestion regular

Fiber keeps digestion moving and helps you feel satisfied. Good sources are oats, whole grain bread, brown rice, barley, beans, lentils, vegetables and fruit with the skin on where you can manage it.

  • Whole grain toast instead of white
  • Oats instead of sugary cereal
  • A handful of canned beans or lentils stirred into soups and stews
  • An extra vegetable on the side at dinner
  • Potatoes in their skins, if you can chew them comfortably

Add fiber gradually over a few weeks to avoid wind and bloating, and drink more as you eat more of it. Gentle activity, such as a daily walk, helps too.

Thirst fades with age, so drink regularly rather than waiting to feel thirsty. About 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day is a common everyday guide, and water, milk, tea, coffee and soup all count. Drink more in hot weather or when unwell. Dark urine, headaches, dizziness and confusion can be signs you need more. If your doctor has told you to limit fluids, for example with heart failure or kidney disease, follow their advice.

Tips for small appetites and weight loss

If you have a poor appetite, are underweight or are losing weight without trying, the usual "eat lighter" advice may not suit you. The priority becomes getting enough energy and protein, and the easiest way is to make small portions more nourishing. This is often called a food first approach. Unless your doctor has advised otherwise, try:

  • Grated cheese stirred into mashed potato, soups, scrambled eggs and sauces
  • Porridge, soups and sauces made with whole milk instead of water
  • An extra egg in omelets, or a beaten egg mixed into mash
  • A spoonful of plain yogurt on soups, curries and baked potatoes
  • Canned beans or red lentils added to stews and soups
  • Peanut butter on toast or crackers
  • A little butter or olive oil on vegetables

Small meals and snacks every 2 to 3 hours often work better than three large meals. Keep easy snacks within reach, and avoid filling up on tea or water just before eating. The book Healthy Eating After 60 Cookbook has recipes sized for one or two, with a booster tip on many of them.

Easier chewing and safer swallowing

Sore gums, loose dentures or dental work can turn a steak into a chore. Choose moist, tender foods with sauce or gravy: slow-cooked meats, ground (minced) meat, flaked fish, eggs, beans, risotto, mashed potatoes and soft-cooked vegetables. Cut meat into small pieces.

Sit upright to eat and stay upright for a while afterward. Take small bites and small sips, chew well and do not talk with food in your mouth or rush. If dentures are loose or sore, see a dentist.

See a doctor if you cough or choke when eating or drinking, feel food sticking, have a wet or gurgly voice after swallowing or get repeated chest infections. They may refer you to a speech and language therapist. Soft, easy-to-chew meals are for comfort, not a substitute for a professional's instructions if you have a diagnosed swallowing problem.

Flavor, salt and food and medicine checks

When taste fades, salt creeps up without us noticing. Most of it comes from bread, processed meats, cheese, ready meals, stock cubes and sauces rather than the shaker. Flavor with herbs, spices, garlic, onion, lemon, vinegar, mustard and black pepper instead, and brown or roast food well for deeper taste. Salt substitutes can be high in potassium and are unsuitable for some people with kidney disease or on certain heart or blood pressure medicines, so ask a pharmacist first.

Never stop or change a medicine because of a food without advice. A good habit: when you collect a prescription, ask whether any foods or drinks should be avoided or timed differently.

When to see a doctor about eating

This guide is general information, not medical advice. If you have kidney disease, heart failure, diabetes, swallowing problems or food allergies, or follow a special diet, check with your doctor, pharmacist or a registered dietitian before changing how you eat. See a doctor for unplanned weight loss, loss of appetite lasting more than a couple of weeks, clothes or rings becoming loose, a lasting change in bowel habits, blood in your stools or ongoing tummy pain. Call emergency services (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia, 111 in New Zealand) for choking that blocks breathing, signs of stroke such as face drooping, arm weakness or speech difficulty, chest pain, sudden confusion, vomiting blood or black, tarry stools.

Food safety matters more with age too, because the risk of serious food poisoning rises. Avoid unpasteurized milk and cheese, eat soft mold-ripened cheeses such as brie only when cooked until steaming hot, skip pâté, heat deli meats until steaming hot and reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C) only once. Vitamin B12 absorption can fall with age and with some medicines, and a doctor can check your levels.

Frequently asked questions

What is the healthiest diet for someone over 60?

A balanced, varied diet built on vegetables and fruit, a protein food at every meal, whole grains, calcium-rich foods and plenty of fluids suits most people over 60. Flavor food with herbs rather than salt, include fish twice a week and stay as active as you can. People with medical conditions should follow advice from their doctor or a registered dietitian.

What should seniors eat for breakfast?

A breakfast with protein is a good start, because many older adults eat little protein in the morning. Try savory oatmeal (porridge) made with milk and topped with an egg, scrambled eggs on whole grain toast, cottage cheese and tomato on toast, sardines on toast, or baked beans with a little cheese. Add a drink and some fruit across the morning.

How can older adults increase their appetite?

Eat small meals and snacks every 2 to 3 hours rather than three large ones, keep easy snacks within reach, and avoid filling up on drinks just before meals. Make food more appealing with herbs, lemon and color, and eat with others when you can. If appetite stays low for more than a couple of weeks or weight is dropping, see a doctor.

How much water should a 70 year old drink a day?

A common everyday guide is about 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day, and water, milk, tea, coffee and soup all count. Drink regularly through the day, because thirst often weakens with age, and more in hot weather or when unwell. If your doctor has asked you to limit fluids, for example with heart failure or kidney disease, follow their advice.

Should seniors take vitamin D?

Many countries advise older adults to consider a daily vitamin D supplement, especially in autumn and winter or if they rarely go outside, because older skin makes less vitamin D from sunlight. Food sources include oily fish, eggs and fortified foods. Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether a supplement suits you, and check any supplement for interactions with your medicines.

What are easy meals for seniors living alone?

Batch cook soups, stews, curries and pies, then freeze them in single labeled portions for tired evenings. Quick options include cheese on toast with tomato, a tuna melt, an omelet, beans on toast or lentil soup with bread. Keep frozen vegetables, canned fish and eggs in stock, and cook recipes for two, eating one portion now and saving one for tomorrow.

Start with tomorrow's breakfast and lunch: add one protein food to each, such as an egg, a pot of yogurt, a slice of cheese or a spoonful of beans, and put a glass of water beside your plate. Small habits like these, repeated most days, do far more than any big overhaul.
