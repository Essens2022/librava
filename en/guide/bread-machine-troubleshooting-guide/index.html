Bread Machine Troubleshooting: Fix Dense, Sunken or Gummy Loaves

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You followed the recipe, pressed start, waited three hours and turned out a brick, a crater or a loaf that is still damp in the middle. The short answer: almost every bread machine problem comes down to too much or too little of one thing, usually flour, liquid, yeast, salt or warmth, so measure carefully, use fresh yeast and liquids at about 80°F (27°C), and change only one thing on your next bake.

A bread machine runs the same steps a baker would: knead, rise, bake. Because you cannot feel the dough as you go, small measuring slips show up in the finished loaf. The good news is that each fault has a signature, and once you can read it, the fix is usually a tablespoon here or a quarter teaspoon there.

Change one thing at a time. If you cut the yeast and the water and switch flour all at once, you will never know which change fixed it.

Why is my bread machine bread so dense

A short, heavy loaf is the most common complaint, and it usually has one of five causes.

  1. Too much flour. Scooping the cup straight into the bag packs flour down and can add about a quarter cup to every cup. That is enough to turn a tall loaf into a brick.
  2. Old yeast. Yeast is alive, and tired yeast is the top reason for a short loaf. Keep an opened packet sealed in the refrigerator or freezer and use it within about 4 months.
  3. Cold ingredients. Milk, eggs and butter straight from the refrigerator slow the yeast. Take them out 30 minutes ahead.
  4. Liquid too hot. Above about 120°F (49°C), you can kill the yeast.
  5. The wrong flour. All-purpose (plain) flour has less protein than bread flour (strong white bread flour), so it builds less gluten and gives a lower, softer loaf. Whole wheat (wholemeal) flour is naturally denser too.

For whole wheat loaves that stay stubbornly short, use the Whole Wheat setting, which gives a longer rest and rise, and consider adding 1 to 2 tablespoons of wheat gluten powder per loaf.

Why did my bread machine loaf collapse

A sunken or cratered top means the dough rose too far, too fast, and the gluten could not hold it up when baking began. It is usually a sign of too much rather than too little.

  • Too much yeast. More yeast does not make a better loaf. Measure with proper measuring spoons, leveled, not tableware.
  • Too much liquid. Cut the water or milk by 1 tablespoon on your next bake.
  • Too little salt, or salt forgotten. Salt slows yeast and strengthens the dough, so check it went in.
  • A hot, humid kitchen. In summer, use cooler water, about 75°F (24°C), and the yeast will still be lively.

A mushroom top that spills over the pan is the same problem taken further, often because the loaf size setting does not match the amount of dough. If your pan is small, make the 1.5 lb (680 g) version rather than the 2 lb (900 g) one.

Why is my bread gummy in the middle

A gummy, doughy center usually has three possible causes, and the first is the most common.

  • Slicing too soon. A loaf is still cooking inside when it comes out of the pan. Cutting it hot squashes the crumb and makes it gummy. Cool it on a wire rack for at least 1 hour, and 2 hours for gluten-free bread.
  • Too much liquid, especially with wet add-ins such as fruit, pumpkin or cheese. Cut the liquid by 1 tablespoon.
  • Underbaking. Most loaves are done at 190 to 200°F (88 to 93°C) in the center. If an instant-read thermometer shows less, put the pan back on Bake only for about 10 minutes.

A darker crust setting also gives a slightly longer or hotter bake, which can help a loaf that is regularly underdone in your machine.

How to measure flour for a bread machine

Bread machines are less forgiving than baking by hand, because you cannot feel the dough and adjust it. An extra 2 tablespoons of flour or water can be the difference between a tall loaf and a flat one. The most reliable fix for most problems is a digital kitchen scale.

  • Weigh it. Set the pan on the scale, zero it, add an ingredient, zero again. A spooned and leveled cup of bread flour weighs about 130 g, and whole wheat flour about 120 g.
  • Or spoon and level. Stir the flour to loosen it, spoon it into a dry measuring cup until it heaps over, then sweep the top flat with the back of a knife.
  • Read liquids at eye level in a clear measuring jug.
Try this once: weigh the flour for a loaf, then measure the same amount by scooping straight from the bag. The difference often explains months of dense bread in a single glance.

Check the dough 5 to 10 minutes into kneading

This one habit prevents most failed loaves. Lift the lid about 5 to 10 minutes after kneading starts and look at the dough. It should be one smooth, soft ball that sticks briefly to the side of the pan and then pulls away.

What you seeWhat to do
Dry, lumpy, ragged dough, flour in the cornersAdd water 1 teaspoon at a time and scrape the corners with a soft spatula
Wet dough smearing across the bottomAdd flour 1 tablespoon at a time
Smooth, round, slightly tacky ballClose the lid and leave it alone

Close the lid quickly, and keep it shut once rising and baking begin, since opening it lets heat escape and can cause a sunken top or pale crust. Flour brands and the weather both change how much water dough needs, which is why even a trusted recipe sometimes needs a small nudge.

Get the yeast, temperature and order right

Yeast must stay dry and away from salt until mixing starts. Most manuals ask for this order:

  1. Liquids first: water, milk, eggs, butter or oil, honey.
  2. Sugar, salt and powdered milk (milk powder) in the corners.
  3. Flour poured over so it covers the liquid completely.
  4. Yeast in a small well on top of the flour.

Some machines with a yeast dispenser want the reverse order, so follow your manual. Use instant yeast (also sold as bread machine yeast or fast-action dried yeast), which works on every cycle. Active dry yeast works on standard cycles if you use about 25 percent more, but not on the Quick or Rapid cycle.

Aim for liquids at about 80°F (27°C), which feels barely warm on the inside of your wrist. Slightly warmer water helps in a cold winter kitchen, and cooler water in summer.

Never use the delay timer with fresh milk, buttermilk, yogurt, eggs, cheese, meat or other perishable ingredients. They sit at room temperature for hours before the cycle starts, long enough for harmful bacteria to grow. For a delayed loaf, use water and powdered milk instead.

Choose the right setting and crust

A recipe on the wrong cycle can fail even when every gram is right. Names vary by machine, so check your manual, but in general:

  • Basic or White: everyday white and mostly white loaves, about 3 to 3 1/2 hours.
  • Whole Wheat: half or more whole wheat flour, with a longer rest and rise.
  • French: lean doughs with little fat or sugar, for a crisper crust.
  • Sweet: sugar-rich or buttery doughs, baked more gently so the crust does not burn.
  • Quick or Rapid: a shorter rise that needs instant yeast.
  • Gluten-free: one mix, one rise and a longer bake, with no knock-down.

Always match the loaf size setting to the amount of dough you used. If loaves are always darker or paler than you like, change the crust setting before changing the recipe.

Start with a simple test loaf

When you are not sure whether the problem is the machine, the ingredients or the recipe, bake a plain, reliable loaf to reset. A classic 1.5 lb white sandwich loaf in brief: about 270 ml water, 2 tablespoons oil, 2 tablespoons sugar, 2 tablespoons powdered milk, 1 1/4 teaspoons salt, 420 g (3 1/4 cups) bread flour and 1 1/2 teaspoons instant yeast, on the Basic setting with a medium crust.

If that loaf comes out well, your machine is fine and the problem lies in the other recipe. If it fails too, check the yeast date first, then your measuring, then the paddle.

A large hole in the bottom of the loaf is normal for most machines, since the paddle bakes in. Ease it out with a wooden or plastic tool once the loaf has cooled a little. If loaves stick, grease the paddle shaft lightly and let the pan rest 5 minutes before turning out.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my bread machine bread so heavy?

Heavy bread usually means too much flour, old yeast or ingredients that were too cold. Weigh your flour or spoon and level it rather than scooping, use yeast opened within the last few months and stored cold, and bring milk, eggs and butter to room temperature. Using bread flour instead of all-purpose (plain) flour also gives a taller, lighter loaf.

Why does my bread machine bread sink in the middle?

A sunken middle means the dough over-rose and then fell when baking began. The usual causes are too much yeast, too much liquid, too little salt or a very warm, humid kitchen. On your next bake, measure yeast and salt exactly with leveled spoons, cut the liquid by 1 tablespoon and use cooler water in hot weather. Change one thing at a time.

Can I use all-purpose flour in a bread machine?

Yes, for most white loaves, but expect a slightly lower, softer loaf, because all-purpose (plain) flour has less protein and forms less gluten than bread flour (strong white bread flour). Hold back about 1 tablespoon of liquid per cup of flour, check the dough during kneading and adjust. For tall sandwich loaves, bread flour gives the most reliable result.

How long should bread cool before slicing?

Let a bread machine loaf cool on a wire rack for at least 1 hour before slicing, and about 2 hours for gluten-free bread. The inside is still setting as it cools, and cutting it hot squashes the crumb and makes it gummy. Turn the loaf out of the pan as soon as the cycle ends so the crust does not go soft and damp.

What temperature should water be for a bread machine?

About 80°F (27°C) suits most bread machines. It feels only just warm on the inside of your wrist. Very cold water slows the yeast and gives a short loaf, while water above about 120°F (49°C) can kill it. In a hot summer kitchen, cooler water around 75°F (24°C) helps stop the loaf rising too fast and collapsing.

Why is there flour on the sides of my bread machine loaf?

Dry flour on the sides or in the corners means the paddle did not reach it, usually because the dough was a little stiff. About 5 minutes into kneading, lift the lid and scrape the corners down with a soft rubber spatula. If the dough looks dry and lumpy, add water 1 teaspoon at a time until it forms a smooth ball.

Your first step today is to weigh your flour, or spoon and level it, and check the date on your yeast before the next loaf goes in. Then lift the lid 5 to 10 minutes into kneading and look at the dough ball.
