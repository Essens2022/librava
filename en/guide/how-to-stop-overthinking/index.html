How to Stop Overthinking: Simple Ways to Quiet a Racing Mind

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your mind replays yesterday's conversation, rehearses tomorrow's disaster and keeps you awake asking what someone really meant, you are not broken, you are overthinking. To stop overthinking, you cannot force thoughts away, but you can move your attention somewhere else with a quick physical action, get worries out of your head onto paper, and give your mind a set time and a clear ending for the thinking it really needs to do.

Overthinking feels productive, which is why it is so sticky. This guide gives you practical tools for the moment a loop starts, for the worries that keep coming back, for decisions you cannot make and for those long nights when your brain will not switch off.

What overthinking is and why your brain does it

Overthinking is thinking that goes in circles instead of moving forward. Useful thinking ends in a decision, a plan, an answer or simple acceptance. Overthinking keeps lapping the same ground, and each lap leaves you more tired and less sure.

It comes in two main flavors:

  • Rumination looks backward. You replay the email, the meeting or the awkward joke and ask why you said that.
  • Worry looks forward. You rehearse the bad version of the future: the test result, the delayed flight, the conversation nobody joins.

Your brain loops because it dislikes uncertainty and treats thinking as a way to stay safe. If it can just find the answer, it reasons, nothing bad will happen. The trouble is that many questions have no answer yet, so the search never ends.

The costs add up: lost hours, poor sleep, delayed decisions, lower mood, tight shoulders and a clenched jaw. Analyzing why you are like this is just a new loop on a new topic. The way out is usually through action, attention and writing.

How to break a thought loop in the moment

Tell someone not to think of a pink elephant and that is all they see. Pushing thoughts away makes them louder. Instead, give your attention somewhere else to go. Think of it as changing the channel rather than smashing the television.

The stop and step

  1. Stop: when you notice a loop, say stop firmly in your head, or picture a red traffic light.
  2. Step: immediately do one small physical action. Stand up, walk to the window, fill a glass of water or stretch your arms overhead.

The physical step is what breaks the momentum. Saying stop on its own often fails because the loop restarts a second later.

Other quick loop breakers

If the loop isTry this
Mild, and you are at homeStop and step
Panicky, heart racingName 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste
Stuck in the same chair for agesChange the scene: step outside, open a window, move rooms
About a task you are avoidingDo a two-minute piece of it right now

The two-minute action is powerful because the thinking often takes far longer than the task. Looping about replying to a friend? Open the message and type the first line. Wondering if you locked the door? Check once, then leave it.

Get worries out of your head and onto paper

Your mind is a wonderful place to have ideas and a terrible place to store them. Once a worry is on paper, your brain can relax its grip, and fears that felt huge at 2 a.m. often look manageable in plain words.

Brain dump

Set a timer for 10 minutes and write down everything on your mind, without sorting or judging. Then mark each item with a check (I can act on this), a circle (out of my control) or a star (important, think about later). The feeling of a million things usually turns out to be fifteen or twenty.

Scheduled worry time

  • Choose 15 to 20 minutes at the same time each day, ideally early evening and not just before bed.
  • Pick a spot that is not your bed or favorite chair, like the kitchen table.
  • During the day, jot each worry down in a few words and tell it, not now, we will deal with this at 6.
  • At worry time, go through the list and write down any actions.
  • When the timer rings, close the notebook and do something pleasant or active.

Many worries fade before their slot arrives. You also learn that you can postpone a worry, which takes away much of its power.

Use the worry tree to decide what to do

The worry tree sorts any worry into one of two branches, each with its own response.

  1. What exactly am I worried about? Write one clear sentence. Work is too vague. My report may have errors is clear.
  2. Can I do anything about it? Be honest.
  3. If no, it is outside your control, like the weather or other people's opinions. Let it go for now and shift your attention with a loop breaker.
  4. If yes, what exactly can I do? Write a specific action.
  5. Can I do it now? If yes, do it. If not, decide when, and put it in your calendar.
  6. Then let it go. You have done all you can for now.

For example, the car is making a strange noise. Can I act? Yes. Action: book it into the auto shop (garage). Now? No, they are closed until Monday. So it goes in the calendar for Monday at 9 a.m., and your brain no longer needs to remind you every hour.

Thinking about it more is not an action. A real action is something you can see, like a call, a message, a booking or a list. If your only plan is to think harder, the worry belongs on the no branch for now.

Question your thoughts before you believe them

Thoughts are guesses, not reports. She is annoyed with me feels like information, but it might be built from a short reply and a tired face. A quick routine that fits on a sticky note:

  1. Name it: I am having the thought that... This puts a little space between you and the thought.
  2. Fact or story: what would a camera have recorded? Everything else is the story you added.
  3. Most likely: ask the worst realistic outcome, the best outcome and then the most likely, which is usually in the dull middle. If the worst did happen, what two or three steps would you take?
  4. Friend test: what would you say to a friend who told you this? Say that to yourself.
  5. Kinder words: swap always, never and ruined for precise language. I always mess up becomes I made a mistake on this one.

Picture a client email that just says Got it. The first thought is they are unhappy. The camera saw two words confirming receipt. Most likely, they replied from their phone between meetings. That shift, from dread to neutral, takes about ninety seconds.

If you find yourself questioning the same worry fifty times a day, looking for total certainty, stop questioning. Answer it once, properly, then treat later visits as background noise.

How to stop replaying conversations

Replaying is an old social safety check that does not know when to stop. Each replay tends to zoom in on the worst moment and rebuild the memory with an anxious slant. Instead of fighting it, give it one proper review and a clear ending:

  1. Write only what was actually said.
  2. Ask three questions. Did I say anything that could genuinely have hurt someone? Is there something I would do differently next time? Is there anything I need to do now?
  3. Choose a repair or a release. If nothing needs fixing, write a closing line such as I have reviewed this. It was an ordinary conversation. I am done with it.
  4. When the memory pops up again, answer it with already reviewed, nothing to do, and turn your attention to something in front of you.

If you really did say something sharp, a short repair ends the replay faster than any amount of thinking. For example: I've been thinking about what I said yesterday about your idea. It came out more harshly than I meant. I'm sorry. Keep it brief, skip the long explanation, and do not keep asking if they are upset. Repeated checking keeps the moment alive for both of you.

Decide faster with less stress

Overthinkers often try to find the one perfect option and be fully certain before choosing. Neither is usually available, so the search goes on.

  • Good enough choices: write three to five must-haves first, look at options one at a time, choose the first that meets them all, and stop looking.
  • Sort by size: ask how much it will matter in a year and if you can undo it. Small, reversible choices get decided in the time it takes to make a cup of tea.
  • Decision deadline: decide when you will decide, then choose at that moment, ready or not. Tell someone if it helps.
  • The coin test: assign each option to heads or tails and flip. Notice if you feel relief or disappointment as it lands. That flicker is your answer.
  • Ask one trusted person, once. Polling everyone usually adds confusion.

After deciding, swap was this right for how can I make this work. The second question gives you something to do.

How to stop overthinking at night

Night is prime time for loops. There are no distractions, and a tired brain is worse at keeping worries in proportion. A small problem at 2 a.m. can feel like a disaster, then look perfectly manageable over breakfast.

  • About an hour before bed, do a 10-minute brain dump on paper.
  • Dim the lights and charge your phone outside the bedroom, or at least across the room. No feeds in the last half hour.
  • Do something calm and low-stakes: a warm shower, gentle stretching or a paper book.
  • Keep a notepad and pen by the bed. When a thought arrives, write a few words, such as call the garage Thursday, and let it go.
  • Breathe in gently for about 4 and out for about 6, five to ten times. Stop if you feel dizzy.
  • If you are still awake after what feels like about twenty minutes, get up, go to another room and do something calm and a little dull in low light. Return to bed when sleepy.

When overthinking needs more support

Everyone overthinks sometimes, and most people can loosen the habit with everyday tools. For some people, though, repetitive thoughts are part of something that deserves more support. That is a sensible step, not a sign of failure.

This guide is general wellbeing information, not therapy or medical advice. Talk to a doctor (GP) or a qualified mental health professional if your thoughts feel distressing or impossible to control, come with rituals, checking or repeated reassurance seeking, affect your work, relationships or sleep most days, or if your mood has been low for weeks. See a doctor too if sleep problems last a month or more, or if you are relying on alcohol to sleep. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, get help now: call or text 988 in the US and Canada, call 999 or Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK, Lifeline on 13 11 14 in Australia, or call or text 1737 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

Is overthinking a mental illness?

No, overthinking on its own is a common habit, not a diagnosis. Almost everyone does it sometimes, especially under stress. It can, however, be part of conditions such as anxiety or depression. If your thoughts feel distressing or uncontrollable, come with rituals or checking, or affect daily life, talk to a doctor or a qualified mental health professional.

How do I stop overthinking everything?

Start by catching loops early and moving your attention with a small physical action, like standing up or stepping outside. Then give your worries a home: a daily 15-minute worry time and a notebook for jotting them down in between. Use the worry tree to act on what you can and let go of what you cannot. Practice a little every day.

Why do I overthink at night?

At night there are fewer distractions, so your thoughts get the whole stage. Tiredness also makes threats feel bigger and solutions harder to see. Many people also use bedtime as their first quiet moment to process the day. Moving worry time to early evening, doing a brain dump before bed and keeping a notepad by the bed all help.

How do I stop replaying conversations in my head?

Review the conversation once on paper. Write only what was said, ask if you hurt anyone, what you would do differently and whether anything needs doing now. Then either plan a short repair or write a closing line. When the memory returns, answer it with already reviewed and turn your attention to something in front of you.

Can overthinking be stopped completely?

Probably not, and that is fine. Your mind will always produce thoughts, and some loops will keep coming back. The aim is to step out of them sooner and spend less time stuck. With regular practice, loops tend to get shorter and less frequent, and stepping out starts to feel natural rather than effortful.

Start today with one small step: choose a 15-minute worry time for this evening, put it in your calendar, and keep a scrap of paper with you to jot down worries until then. When a loop starts before that, stand up and do one small physical thing. Each time you step out, your brain learns it does not have to follow every thought.
