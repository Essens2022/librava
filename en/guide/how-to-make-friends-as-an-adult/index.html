How to Make Friends as an Adult: A Simple Step-by-Step Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your calendar is full but nobody comes to mind when you want to share good news, you are far from the only one, and it is not a personal failing. To make friends as an adult, go somewhere you will see the same people every week, chat a little each time, then invite one person to a short, specific plan and keep following up.

Adult friendship rarely happens by accident, because school and shared routines no longer do the work for you. The good news is that the steps are learnable. This guide shows you where to go, what to say, how to make the invitation and how to deepen a friendship once it starts.

Why making friends is harder as an adult

Most adults lose friends slowly, not through a big falling out. A move for work, a new baby, a divorce, retirement or a switch to remote work quietly removes the routines that kept you seeing people. One day you notice your circle has shrunk.

Friendship usually grows from repeated, relaxed contact over time, not from one great conversation. That is why one-off events often disappoint. You meet lovely people at a mixer and never see them again, because nothing brings you back together.

A few myths keep people stuck:

  • Everyone else has a big group. Most people have smaller circles than you think.
  • It should happen naturally. For adults, making a deliberate effort is the normal way, not a sign of desperation.
  • It is too late for me. People make close friends at every age, including in retirement.
  • If they wanted to be friends, they would reach out. Many people are quietly hoping someone else will go first.

Where to meet people you will see again

Before you sign up for anything, ask one question: will I see these people again next week? The best places share three features. You go regularly, the same people return and there is something to do together, so conversation happens naturally.

Choose a regular volunteering shift rather than one-off events, and a course that runs for eight to ten weeks rather than a single workshop. Faith and community groups, dog walks at the same time each day, the social side of work and your own neighbors also offer built-in repetition.

Online groups and friendship apps work best as a doorway to in-person meetups, such as a local hiking group that posts weekly walks. Then give each place at least four visits before you judge it. Most groups feel very different by the third or fourth time, once people start to recognize you.

How to start a conversation without dread

Lower the stakes first. You are not trying to find a best friend tonight. You are just having a short, friendly exchange. Almost every easy opener falls into one of three types:

  • Comment on something you share right now: "This rain is relentless today."
  • Ask a simple question about the situation: "Have you been to this class before?"
  • Offer something small: "Would you like to share my table? It's busy in here."

To keep it going, listen for hooks, the little details people mention, and ask a follow-up. If someone says they just got back from visiting family, ask where. Share a little about yourself too, so it feels like a chat and not an interview. Open questions, the ones that start with what, how or tell me about, work better than yes or no questions.

Save heavy topics such as money, politics and health problems for later. When you want to leave, end warmly and use their name: "Lovely talking to you, Sam. See you next week." Leaving on a high makes the next chat easier.

Arriving five minutes early and staying a few minutes after helps, because that is when most of the chatting happens.

How to turn acquaintances into friends

This is where many promising friendships quietly fade. You chat every week at the class, then the term ends and you never see each other again. Someone has to suggest meeting somewhere else, and it might as well be you.

Invite after a few pleasant conversations rather than waiting months. Link it to something you have talked about, and make it short, specific and public. "We should get coffee sometime" rarely happens. "Would you like to grab a coffee after class next Wednesday?" gives them something easy to say yes to.

Low-key first plans include:

  • Coffee or tea after your usual activity
  • A walk in a local park
  • Trying a new café, bakery or market together
  • A quick lunch near work
  • A free talk, exhibition or library event
  • A park playdate if you both have children

Afterward, send a short thank you: "Thanks for a great walk this morning. That café was a good find." If it went well, suggest the next plan within a couple of weeks, and consider making it regular, such as a monthly walk. Regular plans remove the effort of arranging every meeting from scratch.

What to do if they say no or maybe

Most no's in adult friendship are about the other person's life, not about you. People are busy with work, children, caring for parents or just a hard week. A no is part of the normal process, not a verdict on your worth.

A helpful rule of thumb is to invite someone up to three times, spaced out over a few weeks or months. The first invitation might clash with their schedule and the second might land in a stressful week. If they decline all three times without suggesting another date, it is reasonable to stop inviting and let them come to you. If they keep offering alternatives, keep trying. If someone seems clearly uncomfortable after one invitation, step back sooner.

If you notice you are always the one inviting, that is common at the start. Some people are simply less confident about asking. Over time, though, a good friendship tends to even out, and it is fine to focus your energy where it is returned.

How to deepen a friendship over time

Closeness grows when both people open up gradually. Start with low-risk sharing, such as a funny story or a mild frustration, then go a little deeper as trust builds, matching what the other person shares.

  • Be reliable. Turning up when you said you would builds trust faster than any big gesture.
  • Remember details, such as a job interview or a sick pet, and ask about them later. Write them down if your memory needs help.
  • Celebrate their good news warmly.
  • Listen to understand, not to fix. It can help to ask, "Do you want ideas, or just someone to listen?"
  • Create a small ritual, like a monthly breakfast or a yearly trip to the same fair.
  • In hard times, reach out even if you are unsure what to say, and offer specific help, such as dropping off a meal on Thursday.

Keep one or two old friendships alive on purpose while you build new ones. A short message after years apart, such as "I was thinking of you today and wondered how you are," is usually welcomed more than you expect.

Making friends as an introvert or shy person

Introversion, shyness and social anxiety are different things, and you can make friends in a way that suits who you already are. Activities with a focus, such as crafts, games or volunteering, take the pressure off constant conversation. Smaller groups and one-to-one plans are often easier than big crowds.

Try the 20-minute rule. When you go somewhere new, agree with yourself to stay 20 minutes, then you are free to leave with no guilt. The first minutes are usually the hardest, and staying past them lets you reach the point where things feel easier. A few habits help:

  • Arrive on time or a little early, when the room is quieter.
  • Give yourself a small job, like setting out chairs or handing out name tags.
  • Aim for one real exchange, not a room full of contacts.
  • Say goodbye to at least one person by name.

Plan recovery time after social events, and on quiet days stay in touch with small, low-energy messages. Look for the person standing alone near the wall. They are often relieved when someone speaks to them.

Staying safe and getting support

When you meet someone from an app or online group for the first time, meet in a busy public place, tell a friend where you are going and arrange your own transport. Do not share your home address or financial details early, and be wary of anyone who asks for money or help with payments, as that is a common sign of a scam. Leave if you feel uncomfortable.

Loneliness is common and often temporary, but if it comes with persistent low mood, hopelessness, losing interest in things or social fear that stops you living your life, talk to a doctor (GP) or a qualified mental health professional. If you are in crisis, in the US or Canada call or text 988, in the UK call 999 in an emergency, 111 or Samaritans on 116 123, in Australia call Lifeline on 13 11 14 and in New Zealand call or text 1737.

If you like structure, a plan helps. Spend two weeks finding your places and saying hello, then two weeks making first invitations, then deepen and widen your circle. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to make a friend as an adult?

It usually takes repeated contact over weeks or months, not one great conversation. Friendship tends to grow from seeing the same person many times in a relaxed setting, then spending time together outside that setting. You can speed things up a little by choosing weekly activities and inviting people out sooner rather than waiting for the friendship to happen by itself.

Where can I meet friends in my 30s, 40s or later?

Look for regular settings where the same people return: evening classes, clubs, volunteering shifts, walking or running groups, choirs, faith and community groups, parents' groups and social sports leagues. Neighbors and the social side of work count too. Pick two options that suit your budget and energy, and go at least four times before deciding.

How do I ask someone to be friends without being awkward?

You don't need to ask directly. Suggest a short, specific plan linked to something you share: "You mentioned you love Thai food. Fancy trying the new place near the office on Thursday?" Specific plans feel natural and are easy to answer. If they can't make it, offer another time and try again later.

Is it normal to have no friends as an adult?

It is far more common than people admit, especially after a move, a breakup, becoming a parent or retiring. Losing touch usually happens because routines change, not because something is wrong with you. Loneliness is a signal that you need more connection, and with small, regular steps most people can rebuild a circle at any age.

How do I make friends after moving to a new city?

Become a regular. Visit the same café, market or pool at the same time each week, and join one weekly group that matches your interests. Say yes to invitations, even small ones, and invite one person out within the first few weeks of meeting. Keep in touch with old friends too, so you have support while you build.

Your first step today is small: choose one weekly class, club or volunteering shift near you, put the next session in your calendar, and plan to arrive five minutes early and say hello to one person. Repeated, friendly contact does most of the work from there.
