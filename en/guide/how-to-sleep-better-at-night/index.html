How to Sleep Better at Night: A Simple Plan You Can Start Tonight

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you lie awake doing the math on how many hours are left, or wake at 3 a.m. and cannot drift off again, you are far from alone. To sleep better at night, get up at the same time every day, get daylight in the morning, keep your evenings dim and your bedroom cool and dark, stop caffeine by early afternoon, and get out of bed for a while if you cannot sleep.

None of that needs pills or gadgets. It works with the two systems that run your sleep, and this guide shows you how to put each piece in place, starting tonight, plus the signs that mean it is time to see a doctor.

Why you cannot sleep at night

Two systems decide when you feel sleepy. Your body clock (circadian rhythm) runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle and takes its main cue from light. Sleep pressure is a need for sleep that builds every hour you are awake and drains away while you sleep.

You fall asleep most easily when both line up: your clock says night and you have been awake long enough to build plenty of pressure. Most sleep problems come from the two drifting apart. A lie-in after a bad night, a long evening nap, bright screens at 11 p.m. or a late coffee all pull them out of step.

It also helps to know that brief awakenings between sleep cycles are normal. Everyone surfaces a few times a night. Trouble starts when you fully wake, check the clock and begin to worry. Most adults need about 7 to 9 hours, though individual needs vary.

Keep one steady wake time

You cannot force yourself to fall asleep, but you can choose when you get up. A steady wake time is the anchor for everything else: it tells your clock when the day starts and lets sleep pressure build on a predictable schedule.

  • Pick a wake time you can keep seven days a week, within about 30 minutes.
  • Start realistic. If you wake at 6:30 on weekdays and 9:30 on weekends, try 7:30 at weekends first, then bring it earlier.
  • Put the alarm across the room and skip the snooze button.
  • Get up however you slept. Sleeping in after a bad night feels kind but often makes the next night harder.

Your bedtime then follows: count back by roughly the sleep you need, and go to bed when you feel sleepy, not just because the clock says so. Give it two weeks. The first few mornings are usually the hardest.

Use light to set your body clock

Light is the strongest signal your body clock receives. Bright light in the morning tells it the day has begun, which helps you feel sleepy at the right time that evening.

  • Open the curtains as soon as you get up, or step outside for a few minutes. Daylight is far brighter than indoor lighting, even on a cloudy day.
  • Get more daylight through the day, for example a walk at lunch.
  • In the last hour before bed, switch to lamps and dimmer, warmer lights.
  • If you get up in the night, use the dimmest light that lets you move safely.

Regular meal times act as a second, weaker signal, so a steady breakfast time helps too.

Make your bedroom dark and cool

Most people are surprised by how much light gets into a room they thought was dark. Check yours at night with the lights off.

  • Dark: blackout curtains or blinds (roller shades), tape over standby lights, a rolled towel under the door, or a comfortable sleep mask.
  • Cool: many people sleep well somewhere around the mid-60s Fahrenheit (roughly 18°C), but your comfort is the real guide. Use breathable bedding in layers.
  • Quiet: a fan or steady background sound can mask noise; earplugs suit some people.
  • Calm: clear the clutter you can see from your pillow.
  • Devices out: charge your phone outside the bedroom, or at least out of reach, and turn the clock away from you.

Try to keep the bed for sleep and intimacy only. If you work, scroll and worry in bed, your brain starts to link the bed with being awake.

Daytime habits that help you sleep

Good nights start in the daytime. These are the habits that make the biggest difference for most people.

HabitWhat helps
CaffeineLast cup around midday or early afternoon. Check tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate too.
AlcoholDrink earlier, with food, and have some alcohol-free evenings. It breaks up the second half of the night.
NapsShort (20 to 30 minutes) and before mid-afternoon, or skip them for a while if you have insomnia.
ExerciseRegular movement helps. Finish hard workouts a few hours before bed if they leave you wired.
Evening food and drinkAvoid a very large meal right before bed and taper fluids in the evening.

If you drink a lot of caffeine, cut back one cup at a time to avoid headaches. And watch the sofa doze: 20 minutes asleep in front of the TV at 9 p.m. can be the reason you lie awake at 11.

A bedtime routine for adults

Your body likes cues. A wind-down routine done in the same order most nights tells your brain that sleep is coming. Set a gentle alarm one hour before bed as your signal to start.

Time before bedExample step
60 minutesDim the lights. Write tomorrow's to-do list on paper.
45 minutesScreens off or put away. Tidy one small thing.
30 minutesWarm shower or bath, then a few gentle stretches.
15 minutesRead something calm in bed under a low light.
Lights outA few slow breaths and let sleep come.

Example only. Swap in what works for you. A busy night can shrink it to 15 minutes, as long as you keep the order. On screens, the content often matters as much as the light: an exciting show or a stressful email wakes your brain up.

Fall asleep with a racing mind

Your mind often speeds up at night simply because it is the first quiet moment of the day. Give worries a home earlier.

  • Worry time: in the early evening, spend 15 minutes writing what is on your mind and one small next step for each item. Then close the notebook.
  • Keep a pen and paper by the bed for anything that pops up, so you can let it go until morning.
  • Slow breathing: breathe in through your nose for about 4, out for about 6. The longer out-breath is the part that calms you. Continue for five to ten minutes.
  • Body scan: move your attention slowly from your toes up to your forehead, softening each area. Many people drift off before the shoulders.
  • Give your mind a gentle task, such as walking through your childhood home room by room.

Trying hard to sleep tends to keep you awake. Aim to rest calmly instead, and let sleep arrive on its own. If slow breathing makes you dizzy, stop and breathe normally.

What to do at 3 a.m.

Waking in the night is normal. What you do in the next few minutes decides whether it becomes a long wake-up.

  1. Do not check the clock. Turn it away tonight so you are not tempted.
  2. Stay still, keep your eyes closed and do slow breathing or a body scan.
  3. If a worry arrives, jot it on the bedside notepad.
  4. If you are getting frustrated or wide awake, get up and go to another room with soft lighting.
  5. Do something calm and slightly dull, such as reading a book you have read before or folding laundry. Keep screens and snacks out of it.
  6. Go back to bed when you feel sleepy: heavy eyelids, yawning, reading the same line twice.

You may repeat this two or three times on a rough night, especially in the first week or two. Going by feel rather than timing yourself matters here. It works because it protects the link between your bed and sleep.

When to see a doctor about sleep

Self-help works well for many common sleep problems, but some signs need a doctor's attention. For trouble sleeping that lasts for months, the first recommended treatment is a structured talking therapy for insomnia, often called CBT for insomnia. It is available through doctors and some digital programs. Some parts, like limiting your time in bed, should be done with professional guidance.

Talk to your doctor (your GP, in the UK) or pharmacist if you snore loudly with gasping or pauses in breathing, feel very sleepy in the day or nod off without meaning to, have an uncomfortable urge to move your legs at night, have struggled with sleep for months, or have low mood or ongoing anxiety. Ask before starting or stopping any sleep aid, supplement or medicine. Never drive when drowsy. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line right away.

Bring two weeks of sleep diary notes to the appointment: bedtime, wake time, night waking, caffeine and how you felt during the day. It helps your doctor see the pattern quickly.

Frequently asked questions

How can I fall asleep fast?

Go to bed only when you feel sleepy, in a dark, cool room, after a dim and calm last hour. Then breathe slowly with a longer out-breath and let your mind drift through a peaceful scene. Avoid trying hard to sleep, since effort keeps you alert. If sleep does not come after a long while, get up, do something dull in low light and return when drowsy.

Why do I keep waking up at 3 a.m.?

Brief wake-ups between sleep cycles are normal, but several things make them longer: alcohol in the evening, a warm room, late caffeine, needing the bathroom, stress and worry, or clock watching. A sleep diary for two weeks often shows the pattern. If you also snore loudly, gasp at night or feel very sleepy in the day, talk to a doctor.

How many hours of sleep do adults need?

Most adults need about 7 to 9 hours a night as a general guideline, though individual needs vary. A good sign that you are getting enough is waking without too much struggle and feeling reasonably alert through the day. Sleep patterns also change with age, so older adults may sleep more lightly and wake earlier than they used to.

Is it bad to lie in bed awake?

An occasional long night is not harmful, but spending many nights lying awake and frustrated can teach your brain to link the bed with being awake. That is why getting up after a while, doing something calm in another room and returning when sleepy is part of the core treatment for insomnia. Go by how you feel rather than timing yourself.

What time should I stop drinking coffee?

A common rule of thumb is to have your last caffeine around midday or early afternoon. Caffeine clears slowly, so an afternoon cup can still be in your system at bedtime and make sleep lighter. People differ a lot, so try an earlier cutoff for two weeks and compare your nights. Tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate count too.

Can I sleep better naturally without pills?

Many people do, with a steady wake time, morning light, dim evenings, a cool dark bedroom, sensible caffeine and alcohol habits and a calm wind-down. Most notice changes within a few weeks of keeping these steady. For long-lasting insomnia, structured talking therapy is the first recommended treatment. Always ask a doctor or pharmacist before trying any sleep aid or supplement.
