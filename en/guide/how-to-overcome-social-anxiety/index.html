How to Overcome Social Anxiety: Gentle Steps That Really Work

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you dread phone calls, rehearse every sentence before you speak and replay conversations for hours afterward, you are far from alone, and it can get easier. To overcome social anxiety, face the situations you fear in small, gradual steps, staying long enough for the anxiety to ease, while you drop the hidden habits that keep fear going and question the anxious predictions in your head.

You do not need to become an extrovert or never feel nervous again. The aim is to feel calmer and do more of what you actually want, whether that is ordering food, speaking up in a meeting or going to a party. This guide shows you how, one gentle step at a time.

Shyness versus social anxiety

Shyness is a normal personality trait, and being introverted is not a problem to fix. Introverts may simply prefer quiet time and small groups. Social anxiety is different: it is a strong fear of being judged, embarrassed or watched that makes you avoid things you would like to do, or get through them with a lot of distress.

Common feared situations include:

  • Phone and video calls, especially with strangers
  • Meetings, speaking up in class and introducing yourself
  • Parties, group conversations and work events
  • Eating or drinking in front of others
  • Shops, restaurants, making complaints or asking for help

Social anxiety is common, recognized and treatable. Fear often grows because of avoidance: every time you escape, your brain files the situation as dangerous. The good news is that the same process works in reverse.

The cycle that keeps social anxiety going

Social anxiety tends to run in a loop with five parts, each feeding the next:

  1. Trigger: an invitation, an unknown number calling, or just thinking about next week's meeting.
  2. Anxious thoughts: gloomy predictions such as I'll say something stupid or they'll think I'm weird.
  3. Body symptoms: racing heart, blushing, sweating, shaking, a blank mind.
  4. Safety behaviors or avoidance: canceling, or going but hiding behind your phone.
  5. Relief now, cost later: you feel better in the moment, but the fear grows a little stronger.

After the event comes the post-event replay, where your mind zooms in on every awkward pause and ignores the smiles and normal moments. That replay then becomes the trigger for next time.

The hopeful part is that you do not have to fix everything at once. Change any one link, and the whole loop weakens. Try drawing your own cycle for one recent situation and circle the link you want to work on first.

How to calm your body when nerves take over

Blushing, sweating, shaking and a racing heart are your body's alarm system. They are uncomfortable but not dangerous, and they always pass. Calming the body gives your thinking brain room to work.

  • Slow breathing: breathe in gently through your nose for about 4, then out slowly for about 6. A longer out-breath tells your body to settle. Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy.
  • Muscle release: tense your shoulders for about five seconds, then let them drop. Do the same with your hands and jaw.
  • Grounding: press your feet into the floor and name a few things you can see and hear around you.

The aim is not zero anxiety. Trying to never feel nervous usually makes nerves worse. The goal is to let the feeling be there while you carry on with what matters to you.

This guide is general self-help information, not therapy or medical advice. If social fear limits your work, study, relationships or daily life, or has lasted for months, talk to your doctor (GP) or a qualified therapist. Never start, stop or change medication without medical advice. Chest pain, fainting or breathlessness that is new or severe should be checked by a doctor, and urgent symptoms need emergency care (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia, 111 in New Zealand).

Question anxious thoughts with kinder ones

Anxious thoughts feel like facts, but they are guesses, usually about the future and usually gloomy. Learning to spot the common traps takes away some of their power.

Thinking trapExampleA more balanced thought
Mind readingThey think I'm boringI don't know what they think. They seemed interested.
Fortune tellingI'll freeze and look stupidI might feel nervous, and I have managed before.
Spotlight effectEveryone noticed me blushMost people are busy thinking about themselves.
CatastrophizingIf I stumble, it's a disasterA stumble is awkward for a few seconds, then forgotten.
Discounting the positiveShe was only being politeShe chose to keep talking to me.

When an anxious thought shows up, write it down and ask: What is the evidence for it? What is the evidence against it? What would I say to a friend who thought this? That last one, the friend test, is often the quickest route to a kinder, more realistic view.

Turn your attention outward

Social anxiety pulls your attention inward. You monitor your face, your voice and your hands, and that self-focus makes you feel more awkward and miss what is actually happening.

Practice shifting attention onto the other person and the conversation:

  • Notice what they are saying, not how you sound.
  • Get curious. Ask yourself what you would genuinely like to know about them.
  • Look at the room: colors, sounds, what people are doing.
  • Practice in low-stakes places, like chatting with a cashier or asking a neighbor about their garden (yard).

Curiosity is a useful tool here. It is hard to be fascinated by someone's story and fully focused on your own blushing at the same time.

Drop the safety behaviors that feed fear

Safety behaviors are the hidden props you lean on to get through a situation: rehearsing every word, avoiding eye contact, hiding in your cell phone (mobile phone), saying very little, sticking to one safe person, leaving early or having a drink to cope. They feel protective, but they keep fear alive in three ways.

  • They steal the credit. Your brain decides you only survived because of the prop.
  • They can create the problem you fear. Rehearsing makes you sound stiff, and avoiding eye contact can seem unfriendly.
  • They keep your attention fixed on yourself.

Run a small experiment

  1. Pick one prop that feels only a little scary to drop.
  2. Write down what you fear will happen without it, and rate how strongly you believe it from 0 to 100.
  3. Go into the situation without the prop and focus outward.
  4. Afterward, write what actually happened and re-rate your belief.

If dropping it completely feels too hard, reduce it first. Check your phone once an hour instead of every five minutes, or rehearse just your first sentence. Expect more anxiety at first, and judge the experiment by what you learned, not how calm you felt.

Face your fears with a fear ladder

Gradual exposure is the heart of overcoming social anxiety. When you face a feared situation and stay, anxiety rises, peaks and then falls on its own. Repeat it a few times and the peak usually gets lower. That is how fear shrinks: through new experiences, not just new thoughts.

Build your ladder

  1. Pick one area, such as phone calls, parties or speaking up.
  2. Write a top goal, like go to a work party and chat to three new people.
  3. List 8 to 12 smaller steps and rate each from 0 to 10 for expected anxiety.
  4. Order them from easiest to hardest and fill any big gaps.

Here is a sample ladder for phone calls:

StepSituationExpected anxiety
1Call a recorded information line2
2Call a close friend for 2 minutes3
3Call a store (shop) to ask opening hours4
4Call a restaurant to book a table5
5Call to book a doctor's appointment6
6Answer an unknown number7

Start with a step around 3 or 4. Stay until your anxiety drops by about half, or repeat short tasks several times in a row. Record your rating at the start, peak and end. Move up when a step feels almost boring, and reward the effort, not how calm you felt. Never push yourself into anything genuinely unsafe. Easy conversation skills for anxious people

You do not need a perfect script. A few simple skills carry most conversations.

  • Open with something you share: the weather, the event, the food, the long line (queue).
  • Ask open questions that start with how, what or tell me about, such as What got you into that?
  • Listen and follow up on one detail they mention.
  • Share a little about yourself too, so it feels like a conversation, not an interview.
  • Treat short silences as normal. A sip of your drink or a new question works fine.
  • End politely: It was lovely chatting. I'm going to grab some food. Enjoy your evening.

Practice in tiny doses: one comment to a coworker (colleague), one question to a shop assistant. Small, regular practice builds far more confidence than one big, scary attempt.

Stop the replay and be kinder to yourself

The post-event replay feels like learning, but it is mostly a harsh review that makes you dread next time. Real learning is calm and specific: next time I'll ask a question instead of going quiet. Then let it go.

  • When the replay starts, name it: this is the replay again.
  • Write down one thing that went fine and one small thing to try next time.
  • Then shift to something absorbing, like a walk, a show or a chat.
  • After an awkward moment, talk to yourself the way you would talk to a good friend.
  • Keep a record of every step you complete, and look back at it on hard days.

Progress is never a straight line. A bad day does not erase your progress. Go back a rung if you need to, and keep going.

Low mood often goes along with long-term social anxiety. If your mood is low, or you rely on alcohol to socialize, talk to a doctor. Talking therapies, especially cognitive behavioral approaches, are the main recommended treatment. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in the US and Canada, call Samaritans on 116 123 or NHS 111 in the UK, Lifeline on 13 11 14 in Australia, or call or text 1737 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

Can social anxiety go away on its own?

For some people, mild shyness eases with age and experience, but social anxiety often lasts or grows when it is managed by avoidance. The encouraging part is that it is very treatable. Gradual, repeated practice of feared situations, along with dropping safety behaviors, helps many people. If it has lasted months or limits your life, a doctor or therapist can help.

What is the fastest way to get over social anxiety?

There is no instant fix, but the most effective approach is steady, gradual exposure. Build a fear ladder, start with a step rated around 3 or 4 out of 10, and practice it several times a week until it feels easier. Small, frequent practice works better than rare, huge challenges. Dropping safety behaviors at the same time speeds up what you learn.

How do I stop blushing and shaking in social situations?

Trying hard to stop blushing or shaking usually makes it worse, because the worry adds to the alarm. Most people notice these signs far less than you think. Slow breathing with a longer out-breath helps your body settle. Turning your attention outward to the conversation also helps. Over time, as fear drops, the physical signs tend to ease too.

Is social anxiety the same as being an introvert?

No. Introverts often prefer quiet time and small groups, and they may feel recharged by time alone, but they are not necessarily afraid of people. Social anxiety is a fear of being judged or embarrassed that leads to avoidance and distress. You can be an introvert without social anxiety, or an extrovert who feels very anxious in some situations.

Should I drink alcohol to feel less anxious at parties?

It is best not to rely on it. Alcohol may ease nerves for a short time, but it can raise anxiety the next day, make the post-event replay worse and slowly become a habit that is hard to stop. It also stops you learning that you can cope without it. Follow your country's low-risk drinking guidance, and talk to a doctor if you are worried.

Your first step today is small: write down one situation you avoid and break it into three easier versions. Pick the easiest and do it this week, staying until your anxiety starts to fall. Each small step teaches your brain something new, and those steps add up faster than you might expect.
