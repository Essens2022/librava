How to Grill for Beginners: Heat, Timing and Doneness Made Easy

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You bought a grill, or inherited one, and every cookout seems to end with burgers that are black outside and pink in the middle. The short answer is this: preheat with the lid closed, set up a hot side and a cool side, and let an instant-read thermometer tell you when food is done instead of the clock.

That one change fixes most beginner problems. Below you will find how to light gas and charcoal, how to control heat, what temperature each food needs, a timing chart, the safety rules that matter, and a simple plan for your first weekend at the grill.

What you need before your first cook

You do not need a shed full of gadgets. A basic gas grill or a kettle-style charcoal grill cooks everything a beginner wants to make, from burgers to salmon. What matters more is a small kit of tools that keep you safe and take the guessing out of doneness.

  • An instant-read digital thermometer. This is the single most useful thing you can own. Color and firmness fool everyone, a thermometer does not.
  • Two pairs of long-handled tongs, one for raw food and one for cooked.
  • A wide, sturdy metal spatula for burgers and fish.
  • A bristle-free brush or a wooden scraper for the grates.
  • Heatproof grill gloves.
  • A chimney starter if you use charcoal.
  • Separate plates or trays for raw and cooked food.
  • Heavy-duty aluminum foil (tin foil) and a couple of disposable foil pans.

Before you light anything, check that the grease tray is empty, the gas tank or charcoal bag is full, and the grill stands on a flat, stable spot outdoors, well away from the house.

Gas or charcoal grill for beginners

Both make great food, and every technique in this guide works on either one. The best grill is the one you will actually light on a weeknight.

If you are on charcoal, briquettes burn more evenly and for longer than lump charcoal, which makes them easier to manage while you learn. Lump charcoal lights faster and burns hotter, so save it for when you feel confident.

How to light a charcoal grill and a gas grill

Charcoal, with a chimney starter

  1. Take out the cooking grate and open the bottom vents fully.
  2. Fill the chimney with briquettes. Fill it to the top for high heat, a third to half full for low heat.
  3. Put 2 sheets of crumpled newspaper or 1 or 2 fire starter cubes underneath and light them.
  4. Wait 15 to 20 minutes, until the top coals are covered in light gray ash.
  5. Wearing gloves, pour the coals where you want them, put the grate back, close the lid and let the grate heat for 5 to 10 minutes.

Gas

  1. Open the lid first, always. Gas can collect under a closed lid and ignite all at once.
  2. Open the tank valve, turn one burner to high and press the igniter.
  3. If it does not light within a few seconds, turn it off, wait about 5 minutes with the lid open, then try again.
  4. Light the other burners, close the lid and preheat on high for 10 to 15 minutes.
Never add lighter fluid to coals that are already lit or warm, and never use gasoline (petrol) to start or revive a fire. Set a chimney only on the charcoal grate or another fireproof surface, never on grass or a wooden deck.

Why the two-zone fire matters most

If you learn one skill, make it this one. A two-zone fire gives you a hot side for direct heat and a cooler side for indirect heat in the same grill.

Direct heat means food sits right over the flames or coals. It is fast and fierce, and it suits thin foods that cook in under about 20 minutes: burgers, steaks, shrimp (prawns), sausages you are browning and most vegetables.

Indirect heat means food sits on the side with no fire underneath and the lid closed, so the grill works like an oven. It suits bone-in chicken, wings, ribs and anything thick.

How to set it up

  • Charcoal: pour all the lit coals onto one half of the charcoal grate and leave the other half empty.
  • Gas with two burners: light one, leave the other off.
  • Gas with three or more burners: light one or two on one side, leave the rest off.

The cool side is your escape route. When a glaze darkens too fast or fat catches fire, you slide the food across and close the lid. Most beginners spread the heat everywhere, then wonder why chicken skin burns long before the meat near the bone is cooked.

How to control the heat and stop flare-ups

Keep the lid closed as much as you can. A closed lid traps heat, cooks food from above as well as below, and starves flare-ups of air. Open it to flip or check, then close it again.

On gas, the burner knobs do the work. The lid thermometer reads the air near the top, which can be quite different from the heat at grate level, so treat it as a rough guide.

On charcoal, the vents control the fire because more air means more heat. Open the top and bottom vents fully for high heat and partly close them to lower it. Wait 10 to 15 minutes after each small change before adjusting again. Closing every vent puts the fire out.

Flare-ups

A short burst of flame when fat drips is normal. A big, lasting fire coats food in soot. To prevent it, trim thick fat, let marinades drip off, oil lightly and empty the grease tray often.

When a flare-up starts, move the food to the cool side and close the lid. It usually dies down within a minute. Never throw water on a grease fire, because it spreads burning fat.

Safe internal temperatures for grilled food

Grills vary with the weather, the fuel and the grill itself, so times are only a guide. The thermometer makes the final call. Push the probe into the thickest part, from the side for thin items like burgers and chops, and away from bone and fat. Check more than one piece, since every grill has hot spots.

FoodMinimum safe temperature
Chicken and turkey, all cuts165°F (74°C)
Ground beef, pork and lamb160°F (71°C)
Steaks, chops and roasts145°F (63°C) plus a 3 minute rest
Fish145°F (63°C) or flakes easily
Shrimp (prawns)Pink and opaque

Plenty of people like steak cooked less: rare is about 125°F (52°C), medium-rare about 130 to 135°F (54 to 57°C) and medium about 140°F (60°C). The official safe minimum for whole cuts is 145°F (63°C), so keep that in mind when you cook for young children, older adults, pregnant people or anyone with a weakened immune system.

Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) but still chewy. Cooked low and slow to about 190 to 203°F (88 to 95°C), their connective tissue melts and the meat turns tender. Rest steaks and chops for at least 3 minutes, and larger cuts for 10 to 15 minutes under loose foil. Meat keeps rising a few degrees as it rests.

Food safety and grill safety rules

A grill is an open fire, and raw meat carries germs, so a few habits protect your family.

At the food table

  • Use separate plates, tongs and boards for raw and cooked food. Never put cooked burgers back on the plate that carried them out raw.
  • Marinate and thaw in the fridge, never on the counter or in the sun.
  • Never brush raw marinade onto cooked food. Set some aside before it touches meat, or boil it hard for at least 1 minute.
  • Do not leave food out for more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F (32°C).

Around the grill

  • Grill outdoors only. Never use a grill in a garage, tent, porch or camper, even with the door open, because burning fuel makes carbon monoxide.
  • Keep the grill at least 10 feet (3 meters) from the house, fences, railings and overhanging branches.
  • Keep children and pets well back, and never leave a lit grill alone.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand nearby.
  • Brush soapy water on the gas hose each season and after changing the tank. Growing bubbles mean a leak.
  • Wire brush bristles can snap off and end up in food, so inspect the grates before cooking or use a bristle-free scraper.
  • Let ashes cool completely (it can take 48 hours), then store them in a lidded metal container.

Your first weekend grilling plan

Start simple and pick foods that teach you one skill each. Cook on a calm day with nobody watching, so you can learn where your grill runs hot without an audience.

  1. Sausages on two zones. Cook raw sausages on the cool side with the lid closed until they reach 160°F (71°C), then brown them over direct heat for a minute or two. No burst skins, no flames.
  2. Burgers over direct medium-high heat. Use beef that is about 80/20, press a shallow dimple into the center of each patty so it stays flat, flip once or twice and never squash thick patties with the spatula.
  3. Chicken thighs. They are forgiving, and a sticky glaze teaches you when to move food off the flames to stop it burning.
  4. Salmon on a hot, clean, oiled grate. It shows you that fish releases once it is ready.
  5. Corn on the cob alongside anything else, turned every few minutes until charred in spots.

Two quick meal ideas to try: thin smash burgers pressed onto a very hot skillet set on the grill for crispy edges in about 3 minutes, or honey garlic chicken thighs brushed with glaze only in the last few minutes so the sugar does not burn.

Frequently asked questions

Should the grill lid be open or closed?

Closed, most of the time. A closed lid holds heat, cooks food from above and below and limits the air that feeds flare-ups. Open it to flip, move or check food, then close it again. The main exception is very thin food over high direct heat, such as smash burgers, where you stand by with the lid up for a few minutes.

How long should I preheat a grill?

Preheat with the lid closed for 10 to 15 minutes. A gas grill usually gets there on high in that time. Charcoal needs 15 to 20 minutes in the chimney first, then 5 to 10 minutes with the lid on once the coals are spread. A properly hot grate is the best defense against sticking.

How do I know when chicken is done on the grill?

Use an instant-read thermometer in the thickest part, away from bone. Chicken is safe at 165°F (74°C). Clear juices and white meat are not reliable signs. Bone-in pieces are best cooked mostly over indirect heat with the lid closed, then moved over direct heat for a few minutes to crisp the skin.

How many coals do I need for a charcoal grill?

For most medium to high heat cooking on a standard 22 inch (57 cm) kettle, one full chimney of briquettes is plenty. For low heat, fill the chimney about a third to half full. On long cooks, add 8 to 10 fresh briquettes every 45 to 60 minutes to keep the temperature steady.

Why does my food stick to the grill?

Usually the grate was not hot, clean or lightly oiled, or you tried to flip too early. Preheat for 10 to 15 minutes, scrape the hot grates, wipe them with an oiled paper towel held in tongs, and wait. Meat and fish release by themselves once a crust forms.

Is it safe to eat a burger that is pink inside?

Color is not a reliable guide. Ground meat can look brown before it is safe or stay pink after it is safe. What matters is temperature: 160°F (71°C) for ground beef, pork and lamb and 165°F (74°C) for ground chicken or turkey, checked from the side of the patty with a thermometer.

Your first step today is simple: get an instant-read thermometer, then practice a two-zone fire with a pack of raw sausages, cooking them on the cool side until they hit 160°F (71°C) and browning them over the flames at the end. Once that feels easy, burgers, chicken and fish follow the same rules, and recipes like those in Backyard Grilling Made Simple will make sense at a glance.
