Basic Japanese Phrases for Travelers: Speak Politely From Day One

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Japan is on your calendar, and you are wondering how you will order dinner or find the right train without reading a word. The short answer: you do not need to learn the scripts. Learn about 15 polite set phrases in romaji (Japanese in our alphabet), above all sumimasen (excuse me) and arigatō gozaimasu (thank you), say them out loud for 10 to 15 minutes a day, and point at menus and maps for the rest.

This guide gives you those phrases with a simple English respelling in brackets, plus the few sound rules that make you easy to understand, what to say on trains, in taxis and restaurants, how to explain an allergy, the etiquette that matters most and the emergency numbers to save before you fly.

The 15 Japanese phrases travelers use most

Start here. With a smile and some pointing, these phrases cover most of a travel day. Read the romaji and the respelling and ignore the Japanese script for now; it is there so you can show it to someone if you get stuck.

How to pronounce Japanese words

Spoken Japanese is friendlier than it looks. It has only five vowels, and they never change: a is "ah", i is "ee", u is "oo", e is "eh" and o is "oh". Almost every syllable is a vowel alone or a consonant plus a vowel, like ka, ri or to, so you can read any word in romaji once you know them.

  • Keep the rhythm even. Japanese does not lean hard on one syllable the way English does. Say Ō-sa-ka with equal beats, not "oh-SAH-kah".
  • Hold long vowels for two beats. Romaji shows them with a line on top: Tōkyō, kūkō (airport). Length can change meaning, as with obasan (aunt) and obāsan (grandmother).
  • Double consonants are a tiny pause: kitte (stamp) is [keet-teh].
  • The r is a light tap, close to the quick "tt" in an American "butter".
  • The u often goes quiet. Desu sounds like [dess] and the polite ending ~masu like [mahss].

Saying "deh-soo" is not wrong, and people will understand you, but the quiet version sounds relaxed and natural.

Sumimasen and other polite essentials

If you learn only one word, make it sumimasen. It means "excuse me" when you want attention (calling a server, stopping a passer-by), "sorry" when you bump into someone, and often "thank you" when someone has gone to some trouble for you.

EnglishJapaneseRomajiSay it
Good morning (until about 10 or 11)おはようございますohayō gozaimasu[oh-hah-yoh goh-zah-ee-mahss]
Thank you (when a service is over)ありがとうございましたarigatō gozaimashita[ah-ree-gah-toh goh-zah-ee-mahsh-tah]
Go ahead, here you areどうぞdōzo[doh-zoh]
Nice to meet youはじめましてhajimemashite[hah-jee-meh-mahsh-teh]

English "please" has two partners. Onegaishimasu asks for something: o-mizu o onegaishimasu means "water, please". Dōzo offers something, such as "after you" or "help yourself". If someone holds a door and says dōzo, go through with a small nod and a dōmo.

Shop staff greet you with irasshaimase (welcome). You do not need to answer; a nod or a smile is perfect. A small bow from the waist works for greetings and thanks, and staff bow to customers all the time without expecting a bow back each time.

Japanese phrases for trains and taxis

Two question patterns get you almost anywhere. "... wa doko desu ka?" asks where something is, and "... ni ikimasu ka?" asks whether a train or bus goes there.

EnglishJapaneseRomajiSay it
Where is the ticket gate?改札はどこですかKaisatsu wa doko desu ka?[kah-ee-sah-tsoo wah doh-koh dess kah]
Does this train go to Nara?この電車は奈良に行きますかKono densha wa Nara ni ikimasu ka?[koh-noh dehn-shah wah nah-rah nee ee-kee-mahss kah]
Which track for Nara?奈良行きは何番線ですかNara-yuki wa nan-ban-sen desu ka?[nah-rah-yoo-kee wah nahn-bahn-sehn dess kah]
Where do I change trains?どこで乗り換えますかDoko de norikaemasu ka?[doh-koh deh noh-ree-kah-eh-mahss kah]
To Kyoto Station, please京都駅までおねがいしますKyōto-eki made onegaishimasu[kyoh-toh-eh-kee mah-deh oh-neh-gah-ee-shee-mahss]
Here is fineここでいいですKoko de ii desu[koh-koh deh ee-ee dess]

Signs in big stations are usually in Japanese and English. Ticket machines often have an English button and a fare map above them. If you cannot find your station, buy the cheapest ticket and pay the difference at the fare adjustment machine by the exit gates; this is completely normal. In big cities, rechargeable transport cards that you tap at the gate make life much easier.

Trains are quiet, so talk softly, set your phone to silent and avoid calls on board. Queue at the marked spots and let passengers off first. For taxis, a red sign reading 空車 (kūsha) means the cab is free. Stand back from the curb (kerb), because the rear door opens and closes by itself, and show your address written in Japanese.

Ordering food in a Japanese restaurant

Staff will ask how many you are: "Nan-mei sama desu ka?" Answer hitori desu (one person), futari desu (two people) or san-nin desu (three people), and hold up fingers to help. Pointing at a menu or the food models in the window is perfectly polite.

EnglishJapaneseRomajiSay it
Do you have an English menu?英語のメニューはありますかEigo no menyū wa arimasu ka?[ay-goh noh meh-nyoo wah ah-ree-mahss kah]
Two of these, pleaseこれを二つくださいKore o futatsu kudasai[koh-reh oh foo-tah-tsoo koo-dah-sah-ee]
What do you recommend?おすすめは何ですかO-susume wa nan desu ka?[oh-soo-soo-meh wah nahn dess kah]
The same as that, pleaseあれと同じものをくださいAre to onaji mono o kudasai[ah-reh toh oh-nah-jee moh-noh oh koo-dah-sah-ee]
The check (bill), pleaseお会計をおねがいしますO-kaikei o onegaishimasu[oh-kah-ee-kay oh oh-neh-gah-ee-shee-mahss]

In many ramen and quick-meal shops you order at a ticket machine by the door: put in money, press the button for your dish, then hand the ticket to the staff. To call a server, raise your hand slightly and say "Sumimasen!" or press the call button on the table.

The check is often left on your table, and you pay at the register by the door. Tipping is generally not expected in Japan and can cause confusion; a server may even run after you to return coins. A warm gochisōsama deshita on the way out is the thanks people appreciate.

Never stand chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice, and never pass food from your chopsticks to someone else's. Both are linked to funeral rites. Rest them on the holder or across your bowl.

How to explain a food allergy in Japanese

Wheat, soy, egg, fish and shellfish appear in many Japanese dishes, and a fish stock called dashi turns up in soups, noodles and sauces, even in dishes that look vegetarian. Use these patterns and swap in any ingredient.

EnglishJapaneseRomajiSay it
I have a peanut allergyピーナッツアレルギーがありますPīnattsu arerugī ga arimasu[pee-naht-tsoo ah-reh-roo-gee gah ah-ree-mahss]
Does this contain peanuts?これにピーナッツは入っていますかKore ni pīnattsu wa haitte imasu ka?[koh-reh nee pee-naht-tsoo wah hah-eet-teh ee-mahss kah]
I don't eat meat肉は食べませんNiku wa tabemasen[nee-koo wah tah-beh-mah-sehn]
Without egg, please卵抜きでおねがいしますTamago nuki de onegaishimasu[tah-mah-goh noo-kee deh oh-neh-gah-ee-shee-mahss]

The reply "Haitte imasu" means yes, it contains it, and "Haitte imasen" means no, it does not.

For a serious allergy, spoken Japanese alone is not enough. Carry a written allergy card in Japanese checked by a native speaker, listing exactly what you cannot eat, and show it every time before you order. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before the trip, and check official guidance, because some common medicines, including some allergy and cold medicines, are restricted in Japan.

Etiquette that matters more than grammar

Japanese people rarely expect visitors to know the rules, and a polite try goes a long way. A few habits make the biggest difference:

  • Take your shoes off in homes, many traditional inns, some temples and fitting rooms. Look for a step up or a row of slippers.
  • Point at things, not people. To gesture toward someone, use an open hand.
  • Carry some cash. Cards are increasingly accepted, but small shops and machines may still take cash only.
  • Carry your trash (rubbish) with you. Public bins are few, and sorting rules are strict.
  • At hot springs (onsen) and public baths, wash and rinse fully at the showers before you get in the bath. Rules on tattoos vary by place, so check first.

When you leave a shop, restaurant or taxi, arigatō gozaimashita, the past form of thank you, is the natural choice, because the service is finished. Staff will often say it to you too.

Emergency Japanese and how to get help

Japan has two emergency numbers, free from any phone: 110 for police and 119 for fire and ambulance. On a 119 call, you will be asked whether it is a fire or an ambulance; answer kyūkyū desu [kyoo-kyoo dess] for an ambulance, then give your location. Interpreting support is available in many areas, so stay on the line and say Eigo de onegaishimasu (English, please).

If you are lost or lose something, look for a kōban (交番), a small police box near stations and busy corners. Officers give directions every day and can write a report for your insurance. In strong earthquake shaking, protect your head, keep away from windows and follow staff and official instructions afterward. Save your hotel's address in Japanese on your phone and on paper.

A simple plan to learn travel Japanese

Fifteen minutes a day, out loud, beats one long Sunday session. A plan that works for most beginners:

  1. Week 1: the five vowels, long vowels and the quiet u, then the 15 phrases above.
  2. Week 2: greetings, sumimasen, onegaishimasu and dōzo; numbers 1 to 10 and asking prices.
  3. Week 3: train and taxi phrases; plan one real route out loud.
  4. Week 4: restaurant phrases, your allergy or diet sentences, and the check.
  5. Week 5 onward: emergencies, shopping and small talk, then review everything by recording yourself.

Learn the katakana for loanwords if you have time, since menus are full of them: kōhī (coffee), hoteru (hotel), takushī (taxi). The book Easy Japanese for Travelers builds this into a 60-day plan with kana charts, 40 survival phrases and allergy card wording.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most useful Japanese phrases for tourists?

The most useful are sumimasen (excuse me, sorry), arigatō gozaimasu (thank you), onegaishimasu (please), kore o kudasai (this one, please), ikura desu ka? (how much is it?), toire wa doko desu ka? (where is the restroom?) and wakarimasen (I don't understand). Add konnichiwa for hello and gochisōsama deshita after meals, and you can handle most everyday moments politely.

What does sumimasen mean in Japanese?

Sumimasen [soo-mee-mah-sehn] has three common uses. It means excuse me when you want someone's attention, such as calling a server. It means sorry for small mistakes, like bumping into someone. And it often means thank you when someone has gone to some trouble for you, with a sense of "sorry to bother you." It is the single most useful word for a trip.

Do I need to learn hiragana and katakana before visiting Japan?

No. You can travel well with romaji phrases, pointing and the English signs found in most big stations. Learning katakana is a nice bonus because menus and signs use it for loanwords like coffee, hotel and taxi. It is also worth recognizing a few sign kanji, such as 出口 (exit), 入口 (entrance) and 駅 (station).

Should you tip in Japan?

Tipping is generally not expected in Japan, in restaurants, taxis or hotels, and it can cause confusion. If you leave coins on the table, a server may chase after you to return them. Show thanks with words instead: say gochisōsama deshita after a meal and arigatō gozaimashita as you leave a shop, taxi or hotel.

What is the emergency number in Japan?

Call 110 for the police and 119 for an ambulance or the fire service. Both are free from any phone. On 119, say kyūkyū desu for an ambulance, then give your location. Interpreting support is available in many areas, so stay on the line and say Eigo de onegaishimasu, which means English, please. Keep your hotel's address written in Japanese.

How do you say thank you in Japanese politely?

Say arigatō gozaimasu [ah-ree-gah-toh goh-zah-ee-mahss]. Add dōmo in front for extra thanks. Plain arigatō is casual, for friends. When a service is finished, such as leaving a restaurant or checking out of a hotel, use the past form arigatō gozaimashita. For a quick, light thanks, dōmo on its own is fine.

Your first step today: say sumimasen and arigatō gozaimasu out loud ten times each, keeping the beats even and the final u quiet, then add three more phrases from the list. Fifteen minutes a day from now will have you ordering dinner, catching trains and thanking people with confidence when you land in Japan.
