How to Make Good Coffee at Home: Ratios, Grind and Easy Fixes

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your coffee at home tastes thin and sour one day and bitter the next, the beans are rarely the problem. To make good coffee at home, use about 1 gram of coffee for every 15 to 17 grams of water, grind fresh beans to suit your brewer, pour water just off the boil at about 195 to 205°F (90 to 96°C), and taste the cup as it cools so you can adjust one thing at a time.

This guide explains the four things that control every cup, gives you two reliable recipes, and shows how to fix sour, bitter, weak or flat coffee without buying new gear.

The four things that decide how coffee tastes

Every brewer, from a drip machine to an espresso machine, comes down to four variables you control.

  • Coffee. How much you use and what kind. The amount matters more than most people think.
  • Grind. Finer grounds release flavor faster; coarser grounds release it more slowly.
  • Water: its temperature and taste. Hotter water extracts faster, and water that tastes bad on its own makes coffee that tastes bad too.
  • Time: how long water and coffee stay in contact. A French press steeps about 4 minutes; an espresso shot runs about half a minute.

These work together, which is why each brewer has its own recipe. A fine grind with a short contact time can taste similar to a coarse grind with a long one. When something tastes off, one of these four is almost always the cause.

Sour or bitter coffee and what it means

Brewing dissolves flavor out of the grounds, and it happens in order. Bright, sharp acids come out first, sweet rounded flavors next, and heavy bitter compounds last. Coffee people call this extraction. Stop too early and you get sour; go too far and you get bitter; land in the middle and the cup tastes sweet even without sugar.

TasteWhat it meansUsual fix
Sour, thin, sharpUnder-extractedGrind finer, brew longer or hotter
Bitter, dry, harshOver-extractedGrind coarser, brew shorter or cooler
Weak but not sourToo little coffeeUse more coffee
Sweet, round, lingeringBalancedWrite the recipe down

Sour feels like biting a lemon and hits the sides of your tongue. Bitter feels like stewed black tea or burnt toast, sits at the back of the tongue and leaves your mouth dry. Let the cup cool for a few minutes before judging, because very hot coffee hides flavor. Dark roasts taste somewhat bitter by nature, so a little bitterness there is the bean, not a mistake.

How much coffee per cup

The coffee to water ratio controls strength. A reliable starting point for most methods is about 1:15 to 1:17 by weight, which is roughly 60 to 65 grams of coffee per liter of water. Since 1 milliliter of water weighs 1 gram, you can read water in either unit on a scale.

WaterCoffee at about 1:16Roughly makes
250 g (250 ml)15 to 16 gOne mug
500 g (500 ml)30 to 32 gTwo mugs
1,000 g (1 liter)60 to 65 gA small crowd

No scale yet? Use about 2 level tablespoons of ground coffee per 6 fl oz (180 ml) of water. It gets you close, but scoops vary by 20 percent or more depending on roast and grind, which is why the same bag can taste great on Monday and wrong on Wednesday. A digital kitchen scale that reads single grams is the best value upgrade in home coffee.

Like it stronger? Move toward 1:15. Lighter? Move toward 1:17. Adjusting strength with the ratio is much easier than fiddling with the grind.

Grind fresh and store beans properly

Ground coffee goes stale quickly, so grind just before brewing if you can. Use the texture as your guide rather than the numbers on a grinder, since every grinder is different.

  • Coarse, like rough sea salt: French press and cold brew.
  • Medium, like regular sand: drip coffee makers.
  • Medium-fine, like fine sand: cone drippers.
  • Fine, like table salt or a little finer: moka pot and espresso.

A burr grinder crushes beans between two surfaces set a fixed distance apart, so the particles come out even. A blade grinder smashes beans into a mix of dust and chunks, and the dust turns bitter while the chunks stay sour. If your budget allows one upgrade, a burr grinder usually helps more than a new brewer. Manual ones are often the best value. With a blade grinder, pulse in short bursts and shake between pulses.

Keep whole beans in an airtight, opaque container somewhere cool and dark, and buy amounts you will finish within about 2 to 4 weeks of the roast date. Avoid the fridge, where beans absorb moisture and smells. For longer storage, freeze sealed portions and let each one reach room temperature before opening.

How to make pour-over coffee step by step

A simple cone dripper makes a clean, bright cup and costs very little. This recipe makes one mug.

  1. Boil the kettle and let it rest 30 to 60 seconds, which brings it to about 195 to 205°F (90 to 96°C).
  2. Weigh 15 g of coffee and grind it medium-fine.
  3. Put the paper filter in the dripper, rinse it with hot water and pour that water away. This removes papery taste and warms everything.
  4. Add the coffee, level the bed and set everything on the scale. Press tare.
  5. Start a timer and pour 30 to 45 g of water to wet all the grounds. This is the bloom. Wait 30 to 45 seconds while it bubbles.
  6. Pour the rest slowly in small circles until the scale reads 250 g, finishing by about 1:45 to 2:00.
  7. Let it drain. The whole brew should take 2.5 to 4 minutes.

If it drains much faster and tastes sour, grind a little finer next time. If it drags past 4 minutes and tastes bitter, grind coarser.

How to make French press coffee that is not muddy

For two mugs, use 30 g of coarse coffee and 500 g of water just off the boil. Warm the press with hot water first, add the coffee, pour all the water, stir gently, put the lid on with the plunger up and wait 4 minutes. Press slowly, over about 15 to 20 seconds, then pour everything out straight away. Coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter.

The low-sediment version

For a cleaner, sweeter cup, leave the lid off. At 4 minutes, stir the floating crust so it sinks, skim off the foam with two spoons, then wait another 4 to 5 minutes. Lower the plunger only to just below the surface, so it works as a strainer, and pour gently, leaving the last bit in the press.

Press the plunger slowly and steadily. A fast or forced plunge can spurt hot coffee and scald your hand. If it feels very hard to push, lift it slightly and press again more gently, and use a coarser grind next time. Keep presses, kettles and hot cups away from table edges and out of children's reach.

Get better coffee from a drip machine

Most bad drip coffee comes from a few habits rather than the machine itself.

  • Weigh your coffee at about 60 to 65 g per liter. Machine cups are often only 5 to 6 fl oz, so check what your markings mean.
  • Grind to medium. Cone-shaped baskets often like medium-fine.
  • Rinse paper filters with hot water before adding coffee.
  • Use fresh, cold, good-tasting water.
  • Brew close to the full amount the machine is designed for, since small batches often brew unevenly.
  • Take the pot off the hot plate when it finishes. Within 20 to 30 minutes a hot plate makes coffee taste burnt. Pour it into a preheated insulated flask instead.

If you have tried a finer grind and more coffee and it is still sour and thin, the machine may not be getting hot enough. Good machines finish a full pot in roughly 4 to 6 minutes; a much slower one may need descaling following the maker's instructions.

Quick fixes for coffee that tastes wrong

Work through problems in this order, changing only one thing per brew.

  1. Weigh coffee and water first. Strength problems are the easiest to fix.
  2. Decide whether the cup is sour or bitter as it cools.
  3. Adjust the grind, finer if sour and coarser if bitter. Grind is your most powerful lever.
  4. Check brew time and water temperature against the normal range for your method.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best coffee to water ratio?

A good starting point is 1:15 to 1:17 by weight, meaning 1 gram of coffee for every 15 to 17 grams of water. That works out to about 15 to 16 g of coffee for a 250 ml mug, or 60 to 65 g per liter. Move toward 1:15 for stronger coffee and 1:17 for lighter. Espresso and cold brew concentrate use much stronger ratios.

Why does my homemade coffee taste bitter?

Bitter coffee is usually over-extracted, meaning the water pulled out too much. Try a coarser grind first, then check that your brew time is not running long. Leaving coffee on a hot plate or sitting on French press grounds also makes it bitter. Dark roasts taste somewhat bitter by nature, so if you drink them, a lighter roast may suit you better.

Why does my coffee taste sour?

Sour, thin coffee is usually under-extracted, so the water did not pull enough flavor out. Grind a little finer, make sure the water is just off the boil at about 195 to 205°F (90 to 96°C), and check the brew is not finishing too fast. Cheap drip machines that never get hot enough can also cause sourness.

What temperature should water be for coffee?

Aim for about 195 to 205°F (90 to 96°C). You do not need a thermometer: boil the kettle and let it sit for about 30 to 60 seconds after it clicks off. Water that is too cool tends to make sour, weak coffee, while hotter water extracts faster. A drip machine heats the water for you.

Should I store coffee beans in the fridge or freezer?

Not the fridge, because it is humid and beans absorb moisture and smells. Keep beans in an airtight, opaque container in a cool, dark cupboard and use them within about 2 to 4 weeks of roasting. For longer storage, freeze sealed portions you will use within a week and let each one reach room temperature before opening.

Is it worth buying a burr grinder?

For most people, yes. A burr grinder makes even particles, which brew more evenly and taste sweeter, and it lets you set the grind for each brewer. A manual burr grinder is affordable and works well for one or two cups. If you only make one upgrade, a burr grinder usually improves your coffee more than a new brewer.
