How to Grow Herbs Indoors: A Simple Windowsill Guide That Works

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe you have bought a pot of basil from the grocery store, watched it flop within a fortnight, and decided you are not a plant person. The short answer is that indoor herbs need three things above all: the sunniest window you have (ideally 6 hours of direct sun), a pot with a drainage hole, and water only when the compost feels dry an inch down.

Get those right and most herbs will grow happily inside. This guide walks you through choosing herbs, finding light, picking pots and compost, watering, feeding, harvesting so plants keep producing, getting through winter, and fixing the problems that trip up beginners.

Best herbs to grow indoors for beginners

It helps to know that kitchen herbs fall into two groups with different needs. Soft, leafy herbs (basil, parsley, cilantro or coriander, chives, mint, dill) like a richer, moister compost and regular light feeding. Woody Mediterranean herbs (rosemary, thyme, sage, oregano) want full sun, gritty free-draining compost, less water and very little food.

A good starter set of five covers most everyday cooking:

  • Flat-leaf parsley. Tolerant and happy on a bright sill. It is a biennial, so you replace it every year or so.
  • Chives. A hardy perennial with a mild onion flavor. Cut leaves to the base and they regrow.
  • Mint. Very vigorous, so always give it its own pot.
  • Thyme. Your sun-loving Mediterranean herb for roasts and sauces. It hates sitting wet.
  • Basil. The one everyone wants and the most demanding, because it needs warmth and plenty of light.

Parsley, chives and mint cope best with less sun, so they are the safest choice if your windows are not ideal. Rosemary can grow indoors too, but it sulks without strong light and suffers if the compost stays wet.

How much light do indoor herbs need

Light is the single biggest factor in how well herbs grow indoors. Most culinary herbs need at least 6 hours of direct sun a day in the growing season, meaning sunlight actually landing on the leaves. A room that looks bright to you may give a plant a fraction of that once it sits a few feet back from the glass.

If you live in the Southern Hemisphere, swap north and south. Trees and buildings opposite can make a south window behave more like an east one, so check for yourself: on a clear day, look at each sill at about 9 a.m., noon, 3 p.m. and 6 p.m. and add up the hours of direct sun.

Keep pots right up against the glass, not on a table behind it. Turn them a quarter turn every few days so they grow evenly instead of leaning, and wipe the window now and then, since dust cuts light more than you would think.

Choosing pots and compost for indoor herbs

Every pot needs a drainage hole. Without one, water collects at the bottom, roots run out of air and rot, and the plant wilts as if thirsty, which tempts you to water it more. If you love a pot with no hole, use it as an outer cover and lift the plastic pot inside out to water at the sink.

Skip the layer of gravel in the bottom. It actually raises the wet zone closer to the roots. Fill the whole pot with good compost instead.

Pot sizes

  • Basil, cilantro, chives, thyme, oregano: start in 6 inches (15 cm), move to 8 inches (20 cm).
  • Parsley and dill: 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) and deep, because of their long taproots.
  • Mint, sage: 8 inches (20 cm), later 10 to 12 inches (25 to 30 cm).
  • Rosemary: 8 to 10 inches (20 to 25 cm) to start.

Tiny pots dry out in hours and stunt plants, so go a little bigger rather than smaller. Terracotta breathes and dries faster, which suits Mediterranean herbs. Plastic holds moisture longer, which suits thirsty basil.

Two compost mixes

Use a quality multipurpose compost or potting mix (peat-free in the UK is easy to find), never garden soil. For soft herbs, use it as it comes, perhaps with a handful of perlite. For rosemary, thyme, sage and oregano, mix about 2 parts compost to 1 part horticultural grit or perlite.

Make supermarket herbs last longer

Those living herb pots from the grocery store are usually dozens of seedlings crammed together to look full. They fight for light, water and root space, and most collapse within a few weeks. Dividing them fixes it, and one pot can become three or four healthy plants.

  1. Water the pot and let it drain.
  2. Tip out the root ball and gently pull or cut it into 3 or 4 sections, each with stems and roots.
  3. Pot each section into its own 6 inch (15 cm) pot of fresh compost at the same depth.
  4. Water well and keep them out of strong direct sun for 2 or 3 days while they settle.

This works best with basil and parsley, and mint and chives divide easily too. Cilantro dislikes root disturbance and may flower soon after, so treat it as a quick crop and sow fresh seed every few weeks.

How often to water herbs indoors

Overwatering is the most common way indoor herbs die. Watering on a fixed schedule, a splash every morning, keeps the surface soggy, rots stems and attracts tiny flies, while the lower roots may still be dry. Check first, every time.

The finger test

Push a finger into the compost to about the second knuckle. For soft herbs like basil, parsley and mint, water when the top inch (2.5 cm) feels dry. For rosemary, thyme and sage, wait until the top 2 inches (5 cm) are dry. You will soon learn to judge by lifting the pot, too, since a dry pot feels light.

How to water

  • Pour slowly over the whole surface until water runs out of the drainage holes.
  • Empty the saucer after 10 to 15 minutes. Roots standing in water slowly suffocate.
  • Water at the base, not over the leaves, ideally in the morning, with room temperature water.
  • If a pot has dried right out and water runs straight through, stand it in 2 to 3 inches (5 to 8 cm) of water for 20 to 30 minutes, then let it drain.

Feeding and pinching for bushy plants

Fresh potting mix feeds plants for about 4 to 6 weeks. After that, give leafy herbs (basil, parsley, chives, mint, cilantro) a balanced liquid feed at half the label strength every 2 to 3 weeks from spring to late summer. Mediterranean herbs need one or two light feeds a season at most. Always water a dry plant before feeding, and stop feeding in autumn and winter, when pale growth is usually a light problem rather than hunger.

Pinching is what turns a single spindly stem into a full, leafy plant. When basil, mint or lemon balm has three or four pairs of leaves, pinch out the top inch of each stem just above a leaf pair. Two new shoots grow from the joints below. Repeat every week or two.

Never strip the big lower leaves off basil because they look the most useful. They are the plant's main solar panels. Harvest from the top, just above a leaf pair, and the plant gets bushier instead of tall and bare.

How to harvest herbs so they keep growing

  • Take no more than about a third of a plant at once, so it can recover quickly.
  • Cut stems just above a leaf pair rather than pulling single leaves.
  • Use clean, sharp scissors or your fingernails.
  • Pick little and often. A few sprigs every time you cook keeps plants compact.

Different herbs are cut differently. Basil and mint branch at leaf pairs, so snip the tops. Parsley and cilantro grow from a central crown, so cut the outer, older stems near the base and let the young inner ones grow on. Chives are cut in small bunches to about 1 inch (2.5 cm) above the compost, working around the clump.

When basil starts to form flower spikes, pinch them off to keep leaves coming (the flowers are edible, so toss them on a salad). Morning picking gives the strongest flavor, though herbs cut at dinnertime still beat anything from a packet.

Keeping herbs alive indoors in winter

Short, dull days slow most herbs down from late autumn to late winter, and that is normal. You can still pick small amounts of parsley, rosemary, thyme and sage. Basil is the fussy one: it is damaged below about 50°F (10°C), and the air right against cold glass can be several degrees cooler than the room at night, so pull pots back a few inches on cold evenings.

Keep pots away from radiators and heaters. Hot, dry air dries compost unevenly and invites spider mites. Water much less in winter, letting the top of the compost dry first.

Using a grow light

For north-facing rooms, or to keep basil going past autumn, a small full-spectrum LED grow light makes a real difference. It runs cool and uses little electricity. Hang it fairly close, typically 6 to 12 inches (15 to 30 cm) above the plants, follow the maker's guidance for your light, and run it for about 12 to 16 hours a day on a plug-in timer so plants still get a dark period.

Fungus gnats are really a watering problem. Let the top inch or two of compost dry between waterings and they usually fade within a few weeks. If cats or dogs share your home, keep herb pots out of their reach. Some common herbs, including oregano, chives and mint, can upset pets if eaten in quantity. Ask your vet about any plant you are unsure of.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest herb to grow indoors?

Chives, mint and parsley are the most forgiving indoors because they cope with less direct sun than most herbs. Chives regrow every time you cut them, mint grows vigorously in its own pot, and parsley keeps producing for months if you pick the outer stems. Basil is popular but needs more warmth and light.

Why does my supermarket basil keep dying?

Usually because the pot holds dozens of crowded seedlings competing for light, water and root space, and because it is overwatered. Divide it into 3 or 4 smaller clumps in their own 6 inch (15 cm) pots, put them in your sunniest window, water only when the top inch is dry, and keep nights above about 50°F (10°C).

Can herbs grow in a window without direct sun?

Some can. Mint, chives, parsley, chervil and lemon balm manage in bright light with only a few hours of direct sun, though they grow more slowly and less leafy. Sun lovers like rosemary, thyme and basil struggle. For a north-facing window, add a small LED grow light for 12 to 16 hours a day.

Should I mist my indoor herbs?

It is not usually needed. Water the compost rather than the leaves, because damp leaves dry slowly indoors and can invite mildew. The exception is a pot near a heater or with spider mites, where rinsing leaves under a lukewarm shower and standing the pot on a tray of damp pebbles raises humidity around it.

Can I grow different herbs in one pot indoors?

Yes, if they share the same needs. Basil and parsley get on well, as do thyme, oregano and sage in gritty compost. Do not mix the two groups, because rosemary would rot in the wet soil basil likes. Always give mint its own pot, since its runners take over anything planted beside it.

How long do indoor herbs last?

It depends on the herb. Basil and cilantro are annuals that last one season, though cilantro flowers quickly and needs resowing every few weeks. Parsley lasts about two years. Chives, mint, thyme, sage and rosemary are perennials that can live for years with repotting every one to three years in spring.

Start today by finding your sunniest window and putting one or two herbs, in pots with drainage holes, right up against the glass. Then make the finger test a habit before every watering. Those two steps alone solve most indoor herb failures, and the rest is pinching, picking and enjoying the flavor.
