How to Learn French for Beginners: A Simple Plan That Works

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe you have a trip to Paris or Quebec coming up, or your school French has faded to "bonjour" and not much else. The short answer: start with the sounds of French, then greetings, the verbs être (to be) and avoir (to have), and regular -er verbs, and practice 15 minutes every day out loud rather than for hours once a week. Speak from the first week, even if it feels silly.

This guide shows you what to learn first and in what order, how to pronounce French without a teacher, the grammar that matters most at the start, the phrases that keep a conversation going and a 90-day plan. Every French example has a simple respelling in brackets, with capitals where the voice lands a little more firmly.

Where to start learning French

Beginners often start with long word lists or verb charts and give up by week three. A better order builds each step on the last:

  1. Sounds first. Vowels, the four nasal vowels, the French R and silent letters. A week of this makes every later word easier.
  2. Greetings and introductions: bonjour, merci, s'il vous plaît, je m'appelle.
  3. Nouns with their articles (le, la, un, une), plus the verb avoir.
  4. Être, regular -er verbs and saying no with ne ... pas.
  5. Real situations: the café, shopping, directions, the hotel.
  6. Making plans with aller and talking about the past.

Then make it a habit. Fifteen focused minutes a day beats a two-hour session on Sunday, because your brain needs repeated small contacts with a new language.

MinutesWhat to do
1 to 3Read yesterday's words aloud
4 to 9Learn one small new point
10 to 12Say a short dialogue twice
13 to 15Write three true sentences about your life

The last step matters most. A sentence about your real sister or your real breakfast sticks far better than one about a textbook character. If you miss a day, skip the double session and simply pick up where you stopped.

French pronunciation for beginners

French spelling looks scary, but it follows rules. Learn these and you can read most words aloud.

  • Most final consonants are silent. Petit is [puh-TEE], Paris is [pah-REE]. The ones often heard at the end are c, r, f and l (think of the word "careful"), as in avec [ah-VEK] and hôtel [oh-TEL].
  • The -er ending of verbs sounds like é: parler is [par-LAY], never "par-ler".
  • The h is always silent: hôtel, heure [uhr].
  • Vowels are short and steady. Do not slide "oh" into "oh-oo".
  • The French u, as in tu, is a rounded "ee": say "ee", keep your tongue still and round your lips. It matters, because vous [voo] means you and vu [vew] means seen.
Nasal vowelSay itExample
an, en[ahn]enfant [ahn-FAHN], child
on[ohn]bonjour [bohn-ZHOOR]
in, ain[an]vin [van], wine
un[uhn]un [uhn], one

Nasal vowels come from letting air flow through your nose and mouth together, without touching your tongue to the roof of your mouth. The French R is made at the back of the throat, like a very gentle gargle. Say "k" a few times, relax it so air keeps flowing, add voice, and practice with merci [mehr-SEE]. A soft English R is still far better than skipping it.

Basic French phrases to learn first

Say bonjour when you walk into a shop, a pharmacy or a small café, and before any question to a stranger. Adding monsieur or madame makes it warmer. Use bonjour until early evening, then bonsoir.

French has two words for "you". Tu is informal, for friends, family, children and often people your age in relaxed settings. Vous is polite, for strangers, staff, officials and older people, and it is also the only way to address more than one person. As a beginner, use vous by default; nobody is offended by politeness. In Quebec people switch to tu more quickly, so follow the other person's lead.

Masculine and feminine nouns made simple

Every French noun is masculine or feminine, even tables and books. The gender belongs to the word, not the object, so learn every noun with its article as one unit: la table, le livre, never just "table".

MeaningMasculineFemininePlural
thelelales
the (before a vowel or h)l'l'les
a, someununedes

Some endings give strong clues. Words ending in -age, -ment and -eau are usually masculine (le fromage, le moment, le bateau). Words ending in -tion, -té and -ure are usually feminine (la question, la liberté, la voiture). There are exceptions, such as la plage (the beach), but gender mistakes almost never stop people understanding you.

French also needs an article where English often has none. "I like coffee" is "J'aime le café", not "J'aime café". A quick check: if no le, la, l', les, un, une or des sits before your noun, look again.

Write new nouns in a short sentence with the gender showing, such as "J'ai acheté une baguette". This sticks far better than "baguette = bread" on a list.

The first French verbs to learn

Two irregular verbs carry a huge share of everyday French, so learn them by heart early.

you (polite or plural)vous êtes [voo-ZET]vous avez [voo-zah-VAY]

French uses avoir where English uses "to be" in some fixed phrases: j'ai faim (I'm hungry), j'ai soif (I'm thirsty) and j'ai trente ans (I'm 30). Never say "je suis trente ans".

Next, regular -er verbs. Most French verbs end in -er and follow one pattern: drop -er and add -e, -es, -e, -ons, -ez, -ent. With parler (to speak), the forms je parle, tu parles, il parle and ils parlent all sound the same, [PARL]. Only nous parlons and vous parlez sound different. Learn one verb and you know hundreds, including habiter, travailler, aimer and manger.

To say no, wrap ne ... pas around the verb: je ne parle pas italien (I don't speak Italian). Ne becomes n' before a vowel: je n'aime pas.

How to ask questions and keep talking

French has three ways to ask a yes or no question. The rising voice is the most common in conversation, est-ce que is neutral and safe everywhere, and inversion is more formal.

  • Vous parlez anglais? (rising voice)
  • Est-ce que vous parlez anglais? [es-kuh]
  • Parlez-vous anglais? (inversion)

For other questions, learn où (where), quand (when), comment (how), combien (how much) and pourquoi (why). These repair phrases matter just as much, because they stop you freezing when you get stuck:

In tourist areas, staff may switch to English as soon as they hear your accent. They are being kind. If you want to practice, ask with a smile: "On peut parler français?" Most people are delighted.

Best ways to practice French at home

Listening takes the longest to build, so start early. Begin with audio made for learners, such as slow news in simple French or beginner podcasts where hosts speak clearly. Then move on to children's programs, songs with the lyrics in front of you, and films with French subtitles rather than English ones. Aim for the general meaning, not every word. Catching 20 percent today becomes 30 percent next month.

  • Echo practice. Play one sentence, pause and repeat it aloud, copying the rhythm. It is one of the fastest ways to improve your accent.
  • Flashcards on paper or your phone: add about five new cards a day and review old ones often.
  • Graded readers marked A1 and A2, children's picture books and short recipes. Read aloud for a few minutes.
  • Switch one device to French. You already know where every button is.
  • Find a person: a language exchange, a conversation club or a tutor for 30 minutes a week.

Do not wait until your French is perfect to speak. Nobody's is, and the mistakes you make out loud are the ones you remember and fix.

A 90-day plan to learn French

This plan assumes about 15 minutes a day, with a longer session at the weekend if you can manage it.

On day 1, record yourself introducing yourself for one minute. Keep it. On day 90, do it again and listen to both. Most learners are amazed at the difference. The book French for Beginners Made Simple follows this same order, with dialogues, exercises with answers and verb tables for each step.

Frequently asked questions

Can I teach myself French at home?

Yes. Many adults learn the basics on their own with a clear plan, a good beginner book, learner audio and daily practice out loud. The key is short daily sessions and speaking from the start. Add a conversation partner, club or tutor after a month or two, because real conversations show you what to fix and build confidence quickly.

How long does it take to learn French for beginners?

With about 15 minutes a day, most beginners can greet people, order food, ask for directions and handle simple conversations in around three months. Holding longer, relaxed conversations takes much more time and practice. Fifteen minutes a day adds up to over 90 hours a year, roughly the length of a full evening course, so steady habits matter more than intensity.

What should I learn first in French?

Start with pronunciation: the vowels, nasal vowels, the French R and silent final letters. Then learn greetings and polite phrases, nouns with their articles, the verbs être and avoir, and regular -er verbs. With these, you can introduce yourself, describe people and things and build simple sentences. Situations like cafés, shopping and directions come next.

Is French hard for English speakers to learn?

French is one of the more approachable languages for English speakers. Thousands of words are shared, such as hôtel, restaurant and question, and the grammar follows clear patterns. The tricky parts are pronunciation, noun genders and some irregular verbs. These take practice, but gender mistakes rarely block understanding, and spelling rules make reading aloud predictable once you learn them.

Should I use tu or vous in French?

Use vous with strangers, shop and hotel staff, officials and older people, and whenever you speak to more than one person. Use tu with friends, family, children and often people your age in relaxed settings. As a beginner, choose vous by default. French speakers will often invite you to switch, and in Quebec people tend to use tu sooner.

Is Quebec French different from French in France?

It is the same language with a different accent and some different words. In Quebec, le déjeuner is breakfast, le dîner is lunch and le souper is dinner, while in France they are le petit déjeuner, le déjeuner and le dîner. Quebec also says la fin de semaine for the weekend. Standard French is understood everywhere.

Your first step today: put a 15-minute French slot in your calendar for the next seven days, then spend the first one saying bonjour, merci, s'il vous plaît and au revoir out loud, and recording a short introduction you can compare with on day 90.
