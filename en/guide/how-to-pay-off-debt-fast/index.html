How to Pay Off Debt Fast: A Calm 7-Step Plan That Works on Any Income

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you are juggling cards, loans, an overdraft and a few buy now pay later plans, and the balances never seem to move, you are not bad with money. The fastest way to pay off debt is to stop adding new debt, pay the minimum on everything, and put every spare amount you can find onto one debt at a time, rolling each payment into the next as debts disappear.

That is the whole method in one sentence. The rest of this guide shows you how to do each part on a real income, with simple examples, the traps to avoid and where to get free help if the numbers just do not add up.

Pay off debt fast in 7 steps

Here is the plan at a glance. Each step gets its own section below, and one step per week is a fine pace.

  1. Pause new borrowing so the balances stop growing.
  2. Automate the minimum payment on every debt.
  3. Write every debt on one page.
  4. Choose a payoff order and stick with it.
  5. Build a small starter emergency fund.
  6. Find extra money each month and send it to one debt.
  7. Roll each finished payment into the next debt until the list is empty.

Fast here means as fast as your income honestly allows. A plan you can keep for a year beats a heroic plan you drop in three weeks.

This guide is general information, not personal financial, legal or tax advice. Every figure below is a made-up example, and the math works the same in dollars or pounds.

Stop the debt from growing first

Paying off debt while you are still borrowing is like walking up a down escalator. Most people who give up do not quit because their plan was wrong. They quit because the balance never seemed to move.

So start with a borrowing pause: a decision, made once, that you will not add new debt while you work your plan. It covers credit cards, store cards, overdrafts, buy now pay later, personal loans and letting bills go overdue. Write it down, date it and put it somewhere you will see it daily. If you share money with a partner, agree on it together.

Add a little friction

  • Take the cards out of your wallet and leave them in a drawer at home.
  • Delete saved card details from shopping sites and your phone.
  • Move any subscriptions on a credit card to your debit card if your budget allows.
  • Wait 24 hours before any non-essential purchase, and a week for bigger ones.

Automate the minimums

Missed payments bring late fees, can raise your interest rate and may show on your credit report. Set up an automatic minimum payment on every debt, timed to land after payday. Many lenders will move your due date if you ask. Then add a low balance alert at your bank so a late paycheck (payslip, in the UK) does not tip you into an overdraft.

There is no need to close every card on day one. Closing accounts can affect your credit in ways that are hard to predict, so in most cases it is enough to stop using them.

List every debt on one page

Not knowing is heavier than knowing. Set aside an hour and gather a recent statement or online login for every debt: cards, store cards, overdrafts, buy now pay later plans, personal and car loans, student loans, money owed to family, overdue bills and anything in collections.

For each one, write four things: who you owe, the balance, the minimum payment and the interest rate (often shown as APR). If the rate is missing, call and ask. Lenders get that question every day.

Leave rent or mortgage off this list, since housing is a core bill. If you are behind on housing payments, though, treat that as a priority and get free advice quickly.

Smallest balance first or highest interest first

Both main payoff methods work the same way. You pay the minimum on everything and put all your extra money on one target debt. When it is gone, you add its whole payment to the next one, so each payment grows as you go. The only difference is the order.

Smallest balance firstHighest interest first
OrderLowest balance to highestHighest rate to lowest
Big plusQuick early wins keep you goingUsually less interest paid overall
DownsideMay cost more in interestFirst win can take a long time

A worked example

Using the list above, say you can find an extra 200 a month. With smallest balance first, the store card gets 225 a month (its 25 minimum plus 200) and is gone in about two months. Then 225 rolls onto credit card B, which now gets 265 a month. With highest interest first, credit card A gets 275 a month and takes many months to clear, but you likely pay less interest in total. Same money, different order.

How to choose

  • Started and stopped debt plans before? An early win may help you stay with it this time.
  • One rate far higher than the rest? Highest interest first may save a noticeable amount.
  • Want both? Clear one or two tiny debts first, then switch to the highest rate.

Pick one, write it at the top of your list and give it at least three months before you reconsider. The best method is the one you are still following a year from now.

Build a small emergency fund first

This step feels slower, yet it is often what makes paying off debt faster in the long run. Without savings, the next car repair or broken boiler (furnace, in the US) lands straight back on a card, and you lose heart.

Aim for a starter fund: a modest, round amount that covers one typical surprise in your household. As an example only, someone might pick 500 or 1,000. If that feels impossible, start with one week of take-home pay. While you build it, keep paying every minimum.

  • Keep it in a separate savings account, not your everyday checking account (current account, in the UK).
  • Name it something like "Emergency only" and set an automatic transfer for the day after payday.
  • Before using it, ask three questions: do I really need this, does it have to happen now, and did it truly take me by surprise? If all three answers are yes, use it without guilt.
  • After using it, pause extra debt payments briefly and refill it, then carry on.

Predictable costs like car servicing, annual insurance and holiday gifts belong in sinking funds instead: small monthly amounts set aside so these bills stop pushing you back onto your cards. Once your debts are cleared, a common general guideline is to grow the fund to three to six months of essential costs.

Find extra money for your debts

Your extra amount is what is left after essentials, minimum payments, sinking funds and your starter fund. It does not need to be big. A small amount sent to one debt every month starts real progress, and these ideas help it grow.

Spend less without misery

  • Track every penny or cent for 30 days so you know where the money really goes.
  • Give every dollar or pound a job in a simple zero-based budget, where income minus planned spending equals zero.
  • Cancel forgotten subscriptions. Check your last three statements line by line.
  • Call your phone, internet and insurance providers and ask for a better price, especially when a contract ends.
  • Shop with a list, plan meals around what you already have and try a one-week no-spend challenge.

Earn a little more

Ask for a raise with evidence of what you do, pick up overtime if your energy allows or sell ten things you no longer use. Decide before the money arrives that it goes straight to your target debt. Skip anything that promises quick riches or asks you to pay upfront to start earning.

Keep a little for fun, because a budget with nothing for treats rarely survives the first ordinary weekend.

Are balance transfers worth it?

Both can help in the right situation, and both can make things worse if the habits behind the debt have not changed.

A balance transfer moves a card balance to another card, often at a lower or zero rate for a set period. Before you go ahead, check the transfer fee, the exact date the offer ends, the rate after it ends, the rate on new spending and what happens if you miss a payment. Divide the balance by the months in the offer. If that monthly figure does not fit your budget, it may save less than it seems.

A consolidation loan replaces several debts with one loan and one payment. A longer term can mean a lower monthly payment but more paid in total. If you clear your cards and keep using them, you end up with the loan and the cards. Be extremely careful with any loan secured against your home, because your home could be at risk if you fall behind.

Either way, decide what you will do with the cleared card before you move a single cent or penny, and talk it through with a free debt adviser first.

When debt feels unmanageable, get free help

If you cannot cover your minimum payments, or you rely on an overdraft or new credit just to pay for food and rent, talk to free debt advice now rather than later. Contact your lenders too. Many have hardship programs or payment arrangements, and most would rather agree a lower payment than chase a missed one. Ask for any agreement in writing.

  • In the US, look for a genuinely nonprofit, accredited credit counseling agency that is clear about any fees.
  • In the UK, free debt advice charities and the free government-backed money guidance service can help. You should not need to pay for debt advice.
  • Formal debt solutions and bankruptcy exist in both countries. They can bring real relief but have long-lasting effects, so get qualified advice first.

Red flags to walk away from

  • Large fees upfront before any work is done.
  • Promises to wipe out debt or fix your credit fast.
  • Being told to stop paying creditors and send the money to them instead.
  • Pressure to sign today, or cold calls and messages offering debt help.
  • Requests for passwords, PINs or access to your online banking.

Be very wary of payday loans and other high-cost short-term credit. They can turn a one-off problem into a long-term one.

If money worries are affecting your sleep, your mood or your health, please talk to your doctor or a support line as well. Debt is a problem with solutions, and you do not have to carry it alone.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to pay off debt?

Stop borrowing, pay the minimum on every debt and put all your spare money on one debt at a time. When it is cleared, roll its full payment into the next. Paying the highest interest debt first usually costs least overall, while paying the smallest balance first gives quicker wins. Finding extra money through cutting bills or earning more speeds up either method.

Should I save or pay off debt first?

Many people do a bit of both. Build a small starter emergency fund first while paying all your minimums, then send extra money to debt. Without any savings, the next surprise bill tends to go straight back on a card. Once your debts are gone, grow the fund toward a fuller cushion, often three to six months of essential costs as a general guideline.

How can I pay off debt on a low income?

Start with a realistic budget that covers essentials and every minimum payment, then look for small, steady amounts rather than big ones. Cancel unused subscriptions, ask providers for cheaper deals and sell things you no longer need. If you cannot cover the minimums at all, contact your lenders and a free debt advice service right away. They can look at your whole situation at no cost.

Is the smallest balance first method better than highest interest first?

Neither is better for everyone. Highest interest first usually saves more money, because you clear the most expensive debt sooner. Smallest balance first often keeps people motivated, because accounts close quickly. If you have quit plans before, an early win may matter more than the math. You can also clear one tiny debt first, then switch to the highest rate.

Will paying off debt improve my credit score?

It may help over time, but no one can promise a specific change. In general terms, paying on time every month and using less of your available credit tend to support your credit history. You can check your credit reports for free through official routes in the US and UK, and it is worth fixing any errors you find before you worry about the score.

Do debt relief companies really work?

Be very careful. Some debt settlement companies charge large fees upfront and tell you to stop paying your creditors while they negotiate, which can lead to extra fees, interest, damaged credit and legal action. Before you sign anything, speak to a free nonprofit credit counseling agency in the US, or a free debt advice charity or the government-backed money guidance service in the UK.
