How to Play Chess for Beginners: Rules, Moves and a Simple Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have a chess set at home and a child, grandchild or friend asking for a game, you can learn enough to play a real game in one evening. To play chess, you set up the 32 pieces on a 64-square board, take turns moving one piece at a time (White moves first), capture enemy pieces by landing on their square, and win by checkmating the other king, which means attacking it so it has no way to escape.

This guide walks you through the setup, how every piece moves, the three special moves, how games end, the opening ideas that matter most and a short habit that will save you from most beginner blunders.

How to set up a chess board

Place the board so each player has a light square in the right-hand corner. People often say "light on the right" to remember it.

  1. Put the rooks in the corners.
  2. Put the knights next to the rooks.
  3. Put the bishops next to the knights.
  4. Put the queen on the remaining square of her own color: the white queen on a light square, the black queen on a dark square.
  5. Put the king on the last empty square beside her.
  6. Fill the row in front with eight pawns.

When you are done, the queens face each other across the board, and so do the kings. Each side has 1 king, 1 queen, 2 rooks, 2 bishops, 2 knights and 8 pawns.

The columns are called files and are lettered a to h from White's left. The rows are called ranks and are numbered 1 to 8 from White's side. So every square has a name, such as e4 or d5. The four squares in the middle (d4, e4, d5 and e5) are the center, and they matter a lot.

How each chess piece moves

A piece captures by moving onto a square occupied by an enemy piece and removing it. No piece except the knight can jump over others, and you can never capture your own pieces.

PieceHow it movesRough value
KingOne square in any direction, never into checkPriceless
QueenAny distance in a straight line or diagonal9
RookAny distance along a file or rank5
BishopAny distance diagonally, always on one color3
KnightAn L shape: two squares one way, then one to the side, jumping over pieces3
PawnOne square forward, or two on its first move; captures one square diagonally forward1

The point values are guidelines that help you decide if a trade is good. Giving up a knight (3) to win a rook (5) is a good deal. Giving up a queen for a bishop is not.

Two things confuse almost every beginner. The knight always lands on a square of the opposite color from where it started. And pawns are the only pieces that move one way but capture another, so a pawn blocked by a piece directly in front cannot move at all.

Castling, en passant and promotion

Castling

Castling is the only move where you move two pieces at once. Your king moves two squares toward a rook, and that rook hops over to the square on the king's other side. It tucks your king into safety and brings a rook toward the middle. You may castle only if:

  • Neither the king nor that rook has moved yet in the game
  • There are no pieces between them
  • Your king is not in check
  • The king does not pass over or land on a square attacked by an enemy piece

En passant

If an enemy pawn moves two squares from its starting position and lands right beside your pawn, your pawn may capture it as if it had moved only one square. You must do it on the very next move or the chance is gone.

Promotion

When a pawn reaches the far end of the board, it must be promoted to a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same color. Almost everyone picks a queen. You can never promote to a king, and you can have more than one queen.

Check, checkmate and stalemate

When a piece attacks the enemy king, that is check. A player in check must get out of it at once, in one of three ways:

  1. Move the king to a safe square.
  2. Block the attack by putting a piece in the way.
  3. Capture the attacking piece.

You can never make a move that leaves your own king in check. If there is no legal way out, it is checkmate and the game is over immediately. The king is never actually captured.

Stalemate is different, and it catches winning players all the time. If the player to move is not in check but has no legal move at all, the game is a draw. When you are far ahead, count your opponent's legal moves before each move so you do not hand them a draw.

A game can also be drawn by agreement, when the same position appears three times, when 50 moves pass on each side with no capture or pawn move, or when neither side has enough pieces left to checkmate, such as king against king.

How to start a chess game well

You do not need to memorize long opening lines. Follow four principles and you will reach a healthy position in most games.

  • Control the center. Start with a center pawn, such as e4 or d4, and aim your pieces at d4, e4, d5 and e5.
  • Develop your knights and bishops. Bring them off the back row early, usually knights before bishops, and keep knights away from the edge.
  • Castle early, ideally within the first 8 to 10 moves, and leave the pawns in front of your castled king alone.
  • Connect your rooks. Once the pieces between them have moved, your rooks protect each other and your opening is finished.

Avoid bringing your queen out early, moving the same piece twice without a reason and making lots of pawn moves. Here is a calm, sensible start that follows every principle. Play it through on your board:

1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 Bc5 4. O-O Nf6 5. d3 O-O 6. Nc3 d6

Both sides have a center pawn, both knights out, a bishop out and a castled king. That is the kind of position to aim for. As Black, answer 1. e4 with 1...e5 and 1. d4 with 1...d5, then develop and castle.

Watch out for the four-move checkmate. A common beginner trap has White play an early queen and bishop at the weak f7 square (f2 for White). If you see a queen and bishop both aiming there, guard it with a move such as ...g6 or ...Qe7 and keep developing.

The blunder check that saves games

Most beginner games are lost by simply leaving a piece where it can be taken for free. A short routine before every move catches most of these mistakes. It is built on three words: checks, captures, threats.

  1. After your opponent moves, ask what their move does. Does it give check, attack one of your pieces or set up a fork or checkmate?
  2. Look for your own checks, captures and threats. This is how you spot the free pieces your opponent leaves hanging.
  3. Pick two or three reasonable moves and picture your opponent's best reply to each.
  4. Before you touch a piece, imagine your move played and look from your opponent's side. What checks can they give? What can they capture?

A piece is hanging when it can be captured and nothing can recapture. Before you decide a piece is safe, count how many of your pieces defend it and how many enemy pieces attack it.

It takes about 20 seconds once you are used to it. If any answer makes you uncomfortable, pick a different move and check again.

Easy tactics that win pieces

Most tactics are double attacks: one move makes two threats, and your opponent can only deal with one.

  • Fork: one piece attacks two enemy pieces at once. Knights and pawns are the best forkers. A knight that attacks the king and the queen at the same time wins the queen.
  • Pin: a piece cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it. A bishop pinning a knight to the king is the classic example.
  • Skewer: the pin turned around. You attack a valuable piece, it moves away, and you take the piece behind it.
  • Discovered attack: one piece moves out of the way and reveals an attack by the piece behind it.

To find tactics in your own games, look for checks and captures, pieces lined up on the same file, rank or diagonal, and squares where a knight could hit two targets. Ten minutes of tactics puzzles a day builds this vision faster than anything else.

How to checkmate with a queen

Once you win material, you need to know how to finish. The most useful pattern is king and queen against a lone king.

  1. Use your queen to shrink the box the enemy king can move in, staying a knight's move away from it so it cannot attack her.
  2. Push the king step by step toward the edge of the board.
  3. Bring your own king up to help, because the queen cannot mate alone.
  4. Deliver checkmate on the edge, with your king guarding the queen.

Before every queen move near a cornered king, check that your opponent still has at least one legal move. Taking away the last one without giving check is stalemate.

Also learn the back-rank mate. A king stuck behind its own three pawns can be mated by a rook or queen on the back row. Giving your own king one escape square, by moving one of those pawns a step later in the game, prevents it.

A simple practice plan for your first month

Short, regular sessions of 20 to 40 minutes beat the occasional long evening. Use a real board whenever you can, and play slower games while you learn, at 10 to 15 minutes per player or more.

WeekFocus
1Setup, piece moves, special moves, check and mate, and writing down a full game
2Piece values, the blunder check in every game, and 10 tactics puzzles a day
3Opening principles, a simple setup for White and Black, and basic checkmates
4Endgames, the full thinking routine and reviewing your losses

After each loss, find the one move where things went wrong and write it down in a sentence. Do your own thinking first before checking with a computer. Chess Made Easy for Beginners lays this out as a full 30-day plan with a task for every day.

Playing with children? Keep games short, praise good moves and give odds, such as starting without your queen, instead of losing on purpose.

Frequently asked questions

Can a beginner learn chess quickly?

Yes. Most adults can learn the setup and how the pieces move in an evening and play a complete game the same night. Playing sensibly takes a few weeks of short practice: learn the opening principles, use a blunder check before each move and solve a few tactics puzzles a day. Within about a month, most beginners play noticeably calmer, more confident games.

What is the best first move in chess for beginners?

Moving a center pawn two squares, either 1. e4 or 1. d4, is the best start for beginners. Both moves claim space in the center and open lines for your queen and a bishop. After that, develop your knights and bishops, castle within the first 8 to 10 moves and connect your rooks, rather than memorizing long sequences.

Can a king take a king in chess?

No. A king can never move next to the other king, because that would put it in check, and no move may leave your own king in check. For the same reason the two kings always stay at least one square apart. The king can capture other pieces, but only if the square is not defended by another enemy piece.

Can a pawn move backward in chess?

No, pawns only ever move forward. A pawn moves one square straight ahead, or two squares on its very first move, and captures one square diagonally forward. When it reaches the far end of the board it must be promoted, usually to a queen, and the new piece can then move in any direction allowed for that piece.

What is the difference between checkmate and stalemate?

In checkmate, the king is in check and there is no legal move to escape, so the attacking side wins. In stalemate, the player to move is not in check but has no legal move at all, and the game is a draw. Stalemate often happens when the winning side carelessly takes away the last free squares.

How do you win at chess as a beginner?

Stop giving pieces away and take the ones your opponent leaves hanging. Before every move, check what your opponent's last move threatens and if your move leaves anything undefended. Follow the opening principles, look for forks and pins, trade pieces when you are ahead, and learn to checkmate with a king and queen so you can finish winning games.

Tonight, set up the board from memory, light square on the right and queen on her own color, then play one slow game against a friend, a family member or yourself. Before every move, say "checks, captures, threats" quietly and look at the board from the other side. That one habit will win you more games this month than any opening you could memorize.
