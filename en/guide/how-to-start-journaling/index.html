How to Start Journaling: A Simple 10-Minute Guide for Beginners

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe you have a pile of pretty notebooks with four pages filled in each, or maybe you have never kept a journal at all. To start journaling, take any notebook and pen, choose a regular 10-minute slot, write the date and answer one simple question, such as what happened today and how you feel about it. That is a complete first entry.

This guide shows you what to write, five easy journaling methods to try, how to use a journal for stress and worry, 30 beginner prompts, and how to keep going after the first week. You do not need to be a good writer. Nobody is grading your spelling.

What you need to start a journal

You need any notebook, any pen and about 10 minutes where you will not be interrupted. A cheap school notebook works just as well as a leather-bound one, and many people find it easier to write in because there is no pressure to make it perfect.

Paper and digital both work. The better choice is the one you will actually use.

SpeedUsually slowerOften faster
Finding old entriesFlip through pagesSearch by words
DistractionsUsually fewerDepends on the device
PrivacyNeeds a safe placeNeeds a password or lock

One common mistake is saving a beautiful notebook for thoughts that seem important enough. That creates pressure before you have written a word. Your journal is a working space, and ordinary Tuesday thoughts belong in it. A good test is to write half a page before committing: if the pen smudges or the notebook will not stay open, swap it.

How to write your first journal entry tonight

You do not need a plan or a method yet. Tonight, give yourself 10 minutes and follow these steps. Stop when the time is up, even mid-sentence.

  1. Write today's date at the top of the page.
  2. Spend about 3 minutes on what happened today, in plain words, as if telling a friend.
  3. Spend 3 minutes on how you feel right now and where you notice it in your body.
  4. Spend 2 minutes listing a few small things that went well, even a good cup of tea.
  5. Use the last 2 minutes for one thing you are looking forward to tomorrow.

Your first entries may feel awkward or a bit dull. That is normal and usually fades within a few days. Entries about the weather and what you had for dinner still do the most important job, which is getting you to sit down and write.

When and where to journal

The best time is simply the time you can protect for a few minutes. Morning writing helps some people notice how they feel and set priorities before the day begins. Evening writing suits people who like to reflect, notice small wins and put unfinished thoughts down before sleep. Lunch breaks work if mornings are rushed and evenings unpredictable.

Try three different times over three days and notice which felt easiest. Then link journaling to something you already do every day, such as your first cup of coffee or getting into bed. A small starting ritual helps too: the same chair, the same lamp, a warm drink.

Remove little obstacles before you need to write. Keep the notebook and a working pen together in a convenient, reasonably private place, or make your digital journal quick to open. Leaving a question at the top of the next page, like What felt important today?, removes the decision about where to begin.

Five easy journaling methods for beginners

There is no single right way to journal. Try these and keep the ones that suit you.

Free writing

Set a timer for 5 to 10 minutes and write without stopping, editing or rereading. If your mind goes blank, write I don't know what to write until something comes. This quiets the inner critic and empties a busy head.

Gratitude journaling

Write three good things from your day and one sentence on why each mattered. Make them specific. I am grateful for my family is fine, but My sister called while I was folding laundry and her laugh lifted my afternoon captures a memory you will enjoy rereading.

Quick dot lists

On busy days, write short bullet points instead of sentences: felt nervous before the meeting, enjoyed lunch outside, need to call Dad. A simple mood or habit tracker can sit beside it as a small table.

Prompt journaling

Answer one question a day. Prompts are perfect when you open the notebook and feel stuck.

Reflection and planning

Once a week, look back and ask what went well, what was hard and what you want to try next week. This turns your journal into a quiet helper for goals and decisions.

How to journal for anxiety and stress

Many people find that writing worries down makes them feel more manageable. Research suggests writing about feelings can help some people feel better, although results vary and it is not a replacement for treatment.

Try a worry dump. Set a timer for five minutes, write What is taking up space in my mind right now? and list everything without editing: bills, an unanswered message, tomorrow's meeting, fear of sleeping badly. When the timer ends, circle one item you can act on in the next day and leave the rest for now.

Then sort what is left into two columns.

I can influenceI cannot control
How I prepareSomeone else's opinion
When I send a messageWhen they reply
My bedtime routineFalling asleep instantly

For each worry, write one of three answers: I can take this action, I need more information, or This is uncertain, and I cannot settle it tonight. Before bed, finish with a short closing note: one thing you handled today and one thing that can wait until morning.

Journaling is helpful for many people, but it is not therapy or medical care. If writing brings up feelings that feel overwhelming, stop, put the notebook away and talk to someone you trust, your doctor (GP in the UK) or a mental health professional. If you are in crisis or think you might harm yourself, contact local emergency services or a crisis line straight away.

Journal prompts for beginners

Use one prompt a day, in any order. Answer in a few sentences or write for the full 10 minutes. If a question feels uncomfortable, choose another.

Getting to know yourself

  1. What small activity makes an ordinary day feel better?
  2. When do you feel most comfortable being yourself?
  3. Which personal quality has helped you through hard times?
  4. How would you describe yourself without mentioning your work?
  5. What childhood interest would you enjoy trying again?
  6. What have you learned about yourself recently?

Gratitude and small joys

  1. What made you smile today, even briefly?
  2. Who made your week a little easier, and how?
  3. What ordinary thing would you miss if it disappeared?
  4. Describe a meal, sound or smell you enjoyed this week.
  5. What is something your body did for you today?
  6. Which place makes you feel calm?

Stress and feelings

  1. What is taking up the most space in your mind right now?
  2. What do you need more of this week, and less of?
  3. Name your mood in one word. Where do you feel it in your body?
  4. What would you say to a friend in your situation?
  5. What is one thing you can let go of tonight?
  6. What helped the last time you felt like this?

Goals and growth

  1. What would make next week feel like a good week?
  2. What is one small step you could take tomorrow?
  3. What did you handle better this month than last month?
  4. Which habit would you like to grow, and why?
  5. What are you ready to stop doing?
  6. What would you try if you knew you could not fail badly?

Relationships

  1. Who do you appreciate but rarely tell?
  2. What conversation have you been putting off?
  3. Write a short letter you will never send.
  4. Which friendship would you like to nurture?
  5. What does support look like for you?
  6. What did a recent disagreement teach you?

How to make journaling a daily habit

Most people quit because they expect too much. Give yourself a two-minute minimum. On hard days, one sentence counts: Today was tiring because too many things needed me at once. You are practicing the act of returning to the page, not producing insight.

Keep a tiny entry ready for low-energy days. Answer two questions in a sentence each: How am I feeling right now? and What is one thing I want to remember about today? This is especially helpful when you are traveling, caring for someone or working irregular hours.

Plan a backup for unusual days. If you normally write before bed but are staying with relatives, write two sentences earlier in the evening instead of skipping.

When you miss days, and everyone does, do not try to catch up. Write today's date and start from today. If you keep slipping, ask what got in the way. Often it is something small, like the notebook living in the wrong room or the time slot clashing with family life. How long a habit takes to feel automatic varies a lot, so judge it by whether journaling is becoming easier, not by a streak.

How to keep your journal private

Honest writing needs a sense of safety. If you worry someone might read your journal, you will start writing for them instead of yourself.

  • For paper, keep the notebook somewhere only you use, such as a bedside drawer or a bag you carry. Some people write a polite private note on the first page.
  • For digital journals, use a device password, a protected document or a private folder, and keep a backup so you do not lose years of entries.
  • If you share a home, a short conversation about privacy can help. You might say your journal is a place you think out loud and you would like it left unread.
  • Decide what to keep. Some people store old notebooks in a box, while others reread and recycle pages they no longer want.

When you do reread old entries, read kindly. Notice how far you have come rather than judging the person who wrote them. The book Journaling for Beginners Made Simple includes 365 prompts and a 30-day starter plan if you would like more structure.

Frequently asked questions

What should I write in my journal as a beginner?

Start with what happened today, how you feel about it and one small thing that went well. If that feels hard, answer a single prompt, such as what is taking up space in your mind right now. There is no wrong content. Everyday details count, and they often become the most interesting entries to reread later.

How long should I journal each day?

About 10 minutes is a comfortable starting point for most beginners. On busy days, two minutes and a single sentence still count and keep the habit alive. Some people enjoy longer sessions on weekends. Consistency matters more than length, so choose an amount you could manage on a tired day.

Is it better to journal in the morning or at night?

Either works. Morning journaling helps some people set priorities and notice their mood before the day starts. Night journaling suits people who like to reflect, note good moments and clear their mind before sleep. Try both for a few days and keep whichever time you can protect most easily.

Can journaling help with anxiety?

Many people find that writing worries down makes them feel more manageable, and research suggests writing about feelings can help some people feel better. Try a five-minute worry dump, then sort worries into what you can influence and what you cannot. Journaling is not a treatment, so talk to your doctor if anxiety is affecting your daily life.

What if I miss days or forget to journal?

Missed days are normal. Do not try to catch up on every day you skipped. Write today's date and start again with a short entry. If gaps keep happening, make it easier: keep the notebook and pen together where you will see them, link journaling to an existing habit and lower your minimum to two minutes.

Do I need a special journal or app to start?

No. Any notebook and pen will do, and a simple private document on your phone or computer works too. Many people find a cheap notebook easier because there is no pressure to make it look perfect. Choose something you can open quickly and keep reasonably private, and change it later if you like.

Your first step is small: find any notebook and pen today, put them beside your bed or your kettle, and tonight write the date plus a few lines on what happened, how you feel and one good thing. Ten minutes, no editing, and you have officially started journaling.
