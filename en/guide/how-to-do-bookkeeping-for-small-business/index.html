How to Do Bookkeeping for a Small Business: A Simple Weekly System

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your receipts live in a shoebox, a glovebox and three email folders, you are far from alone, and it is easier to fix than it feels. The short answer: open a separate business bank account, choose a short list of income and expense categories, record every transaction once a week in about 30 minutes, and check your records against your bank statement once a month.

Bookkeeping simply means recording money in and money out and putting each item in a category. It is not the same as doing your taxes, which is where an accountant usually helps. This guide walks you through a setup and routine that works for freelancers, tradespeople and small shops in the US and UK, with a worked example you can copy.

Step 1. Separate business and personal money

Mixing business and personal spending causes most other bookkeeping problems. When your coffee, groceries and supplier payments all come out of one account, every line on your statement needs a decision, and it is easy to miss real business costs or claim personal ones by mistake.

  • Open a separate business bank account, even if you are a sole proprietor (sole trader in the UK) and the law does not require it.
  • Use a separate card only for business spending.
  • Pay all business income into that account and pay all business costs from it.
  • Pay yourself by transferring money to your personal account on a regular schedule.

Those transfers to yourself are called owner's draws in the US and drawings in the UK. They are not a business expense and do not reduce your tax, because sole proprietors and sole traders are taxed on business profit, not on what they take out. Company owners are usually paid differently, through payroll and often dividends in the UK, so plan that with an accountant.

Step 2. Choose a spreadsheet or bookkeeping software

The best system is the one you will actually open every week. A spreadsheet is free and works well for a low-volume business with a few dozen transactions a month. Bookkeeping software costs a monthly fee but can pull in bank transactions automatically, send invoices and produce reports, which saves real time as you grow.

You will also hear two pairs of terms:

  • Single entry vs double entry. Single entry is a simple list of income and expenses, like a checkbook register. Double entry records every transaction in two accounts, as a debit in one and a credit in another, and the totals must always match. That built-in check is why most bookkeeping software uses it behind the scenes.
  • Cash vs accrual. Cash basis records income when money lands and expenses when you pay them. Accrual records income when you earn or bill it and expenses when you receive the bill, even if no money has moved.

Many small businesses start with single entry on the cash basis. Rules on which basis you may use vary, so check with your accountant and official guidance from the IRS or HMRC.

Step 3. Set up simple categories

Your list of categories is called a chart of accounts. It groups everything into income, expenses, assets (what the business owns), liabilities (what it owes) and equity (the owner's stake). Around 15 to 30 accounts in total suits most small businesses.

Expense categoryTypical items
Advertising and marketingAds, flyers, website hosting
Bank and payment feesAccount fees, card processing fees
InsuranceLiability, professional insurance
Office suppliesPaper, ink, postage
Professional feesAccountant, lawyer, bookkeeper
Rent and utilitiesWorkspace rent, electricity, internet
Software and subscriptionsBookkeeping and design tools
Vehicle expensesFuel, repairs, parking, or mileage
SubcontractorsFreelancers who help you

If you sell products, keep a separate cost of goods sold (cost of sales) line for stock and materials, so you can see your gross profit. Keep money you put into the business and loans out of your income categories, since neither is income. Ask your accountant whether to match your categories to the expense lines on your tax form, which makes year end faster. A category in your books does not automatically make a cost tax deductible (allowable, in the UK); your accountant applies those rules.

Step 4. Record income and expenses every week

For every money movement, record five things: the date, a short description, the category, the amount and the proof (an invoice, receipt or statement line). Then protect a 30-minute slot each week, same day and time. A small regular habit beats a stressful marathon at tax time.

Your 30-minute weekly routine

  1. Open your business bank and card accounts and look at every transaction since last week.
  2. Record or categorize each one in your spreadsheet or software.
  3. Match each expense to its receipt and file the receipt.
  4. Check your invoice list and mark anything that has been paid.
  5. Add anything you are unsure about to a questions for my accountant list and move on.

A worked example

Dan is a self-employed electrician on the cash basis. One week he records two paid invoices of $850 and tabindex="-1",400, so his income is $2,250. His expenses are cable and fittings tabindex="-1"20 (cost of sales), fuel $45, his phone bill $60 and a bank fee $25, which add up to $250. His profit for the week before tax is $2,250 minus $250, which is $2,000.

That $2,000 is not all spending money. Part of it belongs to the tax authorities, which is why Step 7 matters. If you sell through an online platform, record the full sale as income and the platform fee as a separate expense, rather than only the amount that reaches your bank.

Step 5. Send clear invoices and track who owes you

A good invoice answers every question a customer might use as a reason to delay payment. Include:

  • Your business name and contact details, and the customer's name and address.
  • A unique invoice number, in an unbroken sequence such as 1001, 1002, 1003.
  • The invoice date and a clear due date.
  • A description of the work or goods, with quantity, rate and line totals.
  • Sales tax (US) or VAT (UK) shown separately, if you charge it.
  • The total due and exactly how to pay.

Agree payment terms before you start the work and repeat them on the invoice. Take a deposit on larger jobs. Each week, review your list of unpaid invoices, oldest first, and follow up on a fixed schedule: a friendly reminder, then a phone call after two reminders, then a final notice. Keep a short note of every contact.

Step 6. Reconcile your bank account every month

A bank reconciliation checks that your books match your bank statement and explains every difference. It catches missed transactions, typing errors and double entries while they are still small. Do it for every business account, including credit cards, at least once a month.

  1. Pick the end date, usually the last day of the month.
  2. Note the closing balance on the bank statement and the balance in your books on the same date.
  3. Tick off every transaction that appears in both places.
  4. Items left unticked in your books are usually timing items: deposits not yet cleared and payments not yet taken. Adjust the bank balance for these.
  5. Items left unticked on the statement are usually bank-only items such as fees and interest. Add them to your books.
  6. Compare the two adjusted balances. If they match, you are done.

If they do not match, look for a transaction of exactly the difference, an amount entered as income instead of an expense (the difference will be double the amount), or transposed digits such as 54 typed as 45. A difference that divides evenly by 9 often points to transposed digits.

Step 7. Set money aside for tax as you go

Self-employed people usually pay tax during the year. In the US, sole proprietors generally pay income tax and self-employment tax on profit, often through quarterly estimated payments generally due in April, June, September and January. In the UK, sole traders file Self Assessment, with the online return due by 31 January after the tax year that ends on 5 April, and may make payments on account. Always check the current rules.

The simplest protection is a separate savings account for tax. Each time a customer pays you, move a fixed percentage of the payment into it, using a figure your accountant suggests for your situation. Sales tax and VAT you collect are not your income either, so record them as amounts you owe until you pay them over.

This guide is general information, not tax, legal or financial advice. Rules, rates, thresholds and deadlines change and depend on where you live and how your business is set up. Check with a qualified accountant (a CPA or enrolled agent in the US, a chartered or certified accountant in the UK) and official sources such as the IRS, your state tax agency or HMRC.

Step 8. Keep receipts and records you can find

Keep anything that supports a figure in your books or on a tax return: receipts, invoices, bank and card statements, mileage logs and records for any home office claim. Paper receipts printed on thermal paper fade, so photograph them promptly.

  • Use one digital folder per tax year, with a single receipts inbox where every new receipt lands first.
  • Name files the same way every time, for example date, supplier and amount.
  • Move receipts from the inbox to their place during your weekly session.
  • Log business mileage as you drive, with the date, purpose, start and end points and miles.
  • Back everything up once a month.

How long to keep records varies. As a general guide, the IRS expects at least 3 years and longer in some cases, while HMRC generally expects 5 years after the 31 January deadline for sole traders and 6 years for limited companies. Check the current rules for your situation.

Step 9. Read your three key reports

Once your books are reconciled, three reports tell you how the business is really doing.

ReportThe question it answers
Profit and loss statementDid I make money over this period?
Balance sheetWhat do I own and owe on this date?
Cash flow statementWhy did my bank balance change?

Profit and cash are different. You can be profitable on paper and still short of cash if customers pay late or you have just bought stock. A simple 13-week cash forecast, listing expected money in and money out week by week, shows the tight weeks while there is still time to act. Look at your profit and loss monthly and your balance sheet quarterly, and the numbers soon stop feeling scary.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do my own bookkeeping for my small business?

Yes. Most sole proprietors, sole traders and small business owners can keep their own books with a spreadsheet or bookkeeping software and about 30 minutes a week. The keys are a separate business bank account, a short list of categories and a monthly bank reconciliation. Many people still use an accountant for tax returns, structure and planning, which costs less when the records are tidy.

What is the easiest bookkeeping method for a small business?

For many very small businesses, the easiest method is single-entry bookkeeping on the cash basis. You keep one list of income and expenses and record each item when money actually moves in or out of your account. As you grow, take on staff or carry stock, double entry and accrual accounting give a fuller picture. Check with an accountant which basis suits you.

How often should I do bookkeeping?

Weekly works best for most small businesses. A 30-minute session each week keeps transactions categorized, receipts filed and invoices followed up while details are fresh. Then once a month reconcile every account and glance at your profit and loss. Once a quarter, handle tax payments and review your balance sheet. Leaving it all until tax time usually takes far longer.

What is the difference between a bookkeeper and an accountant?

A bookkeeper records and organizes day to day transactions, reconciles accounts and keeps the records accurate. An accountant usually works from those records to prepare tax returns, give tax and structure advice and help with bigger decisions. Many small businesses do their own bookkeeping, or hire a bookkeeper when time gets short, and use an accountant once or a few times a year.

What happens if I mix business and personal expenses?

Mixing them makes it harder to see your real profit, easier to miss business costs you could claim, and easier to claim personal costs by mistake. It also makes reconciling slow. Start using a separate business account and card from today, then go back through recent statements and mark personal items clearly. An accountant can help you correct past records if needed.

Do I need receipts for every business expense?

You should keep proof for every expense you record. A receipt or invoice is best, but if one is lost, a bank or card statement, an order confirmation email or a short written note of the date, supplier, amount and business reason can often help support it. Photograph paper receipts quickly, since thermal paper fades, and file them during your weekly session.

Your first step today is to open, or start using, a separate business bank account and block out a 30-minute slot in your calendar for the same time every week. Everything else in this guide builds on those two habits.
