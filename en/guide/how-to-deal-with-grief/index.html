How to Deal With Grief: Gentle, Practical Ways to Get Through Each Day

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If someone you love has died, you may feel as though the ground has gone from under you, and nobody handed you instructions. To deal with grief, take it one day at a time: let the waves of feeling come and pass, keep a few small daily routines, eat, drink and rest even when you do not want to, lean on people who can help, and get extra support if your grief stays overwhelming for many months.

There is no right way or right timetable to grieve. This guide explains what grief is really like, gives you simple tools for the hardest moments, and shows you the signs that mean it is time to talk to someone.

What grief is really like

Grief rarely moves in neat steps. It comes in waves. One hour you are coping with paperwork, the next a song in the supermarket leaves you in tears. Most people move back and forth between missing the person and getting on with daily life, and that back and forth is normal.

Grief brings many feelings, and some of them come as a surprise:

  • Shock and numbness, especially in the first weeks
  • Deep sadness and yearning for the person to come back
  • Anger at doctors, at family, at the person for leaving, or at yourself
  • Guilt and "if only" thoughts that replay the past
  • Relief, especially after a long illness, often followed by guilt about the relief
  • Anxiety, including worry about your own health or losing someone else

Grief also lives in the body: tiredness, aches, a tight chest or throat, sleep changes and appetite changes. Many people forget things, lose their train of thought or cannot read more than a page. This fog is common and usually lifts with time. Dreaming about the person or briefly feeling they are near is also common, and it does not mean you are going mad.

Are there really five stages of grief?

You may have heard that grief moves through five stages in order. It is one old model, and many people find it does not match what they feel. You might feel several of these feelings in one afternoon, skip some, or return to one many times.

A more helpful picture is that grief does not shrink, but your life slowly grows around it. The pain is still there on hard days, but there is gradually more room for other things too: work, friends, small pleasures. You do not have to "get over" the person or stop loving them to feel better.

Getting through the first days and weeks

Early on, aim only to get through each day. Write a short list of three things for tomorrow and let everything else wait.

  • Say yes to specific offers: meals, school runs, phone calls, shopping.
  • Ask one organized friend to tell other people the news for you.
  • Keep a notebook for dates, names and phone numbers, since grief fog makes details slip.
  • Get a medical certificate of the cause of death, then register the death. Deadlines vary by place (in England and Wales it is usually within 5 days), so check locally.
  • Contact a funeral director (funeral home) and look for a will.
  • Ask for several certified copies of the death certificate. Banks, pensions, insurers, employers and utilities will all want one.

If you can, put off big decisions such as selling the home, moving or large money moves for a while. Be wary of unexpected calls, emails or offers after a death, as scammers target bereaved people.

How to cope when a wave of grief hits

Waves usually rise, peak and fall within minutes, even when they feel endless. Decide what you will do on a calmer day, because thinking clearly in the middle of one is hard.

  1. Name it. Tell yourself, "This is a grief wave. It will pass."
  2. Find a safe spot. Step outside, sit in the car or go to the bathroom. You are allowed to leave.
  3. Breathe in gently for a count of four and out for a count of six.
  4. Ground yourself: feel your feet on the floor and name five things you can see.
  5. Let the tears come if they want to. Fighting a wave often makes it last longer.
  6. Comfort yourself afterward with water, a warm drink or a message to someone who understands.

Write your own three steps on a small card and keep it in your wallet or bag. Common triggers include songs, smells, places, their handwriting and dates, so it helps to expect them.

Small daily anchors

Anchors are tiny things you do every day that give shape to a shapeless time. Open the curtains and stand in the daylight, make your bed, walk to the end of the street, water the plants, or call the same friend on the same evening each week. On heavy days, doing your anchors counts as a good day.

Looking after your body while grieving

AreaWhat helps
SleepSame wake-up time, morning daylight, a calm hour before bed, no caffeine later in the day, a notepad for racing thoughts
RestLying down with eyes closed counts; keep naps to about 20 to 30 minutes
FoodSmall amounts often; soup, eggs, yogurt, toast and porridge (oatmeal) are easy
FluidsKeep a glass of water where you can see it
MovementA ten-minute walk outside, even slowly
MedicinesKeep taking anything prescribed, and keep checkups

It is fine to sleep in another room or with a light on for a while if your bed feels too empty. Be careful with alcohol or other ways of numbing the pain. They may help for an evening, but they tend to make sleep, mood and grief worse, and alcohol can interact with medicines. Ask a pharmacist if you are unsure.

Never put chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting down to grief: call 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia or 111 in New Zealand. See your doctor (GP) if sleep or eating problems last, if a health condition is getting worse, or before starting any new medicine, supplement or big change to your diet or exercise.

How to handle anniversaries and hard days

The first year brings a run of firsts: their birthday, yours, holidays and the anniversary of the death. The days before are often harder than the day itself.

  • Decide ahead how you want to spend the day, and with whom.
  • Plan an escape route, such as leaving a family gathering early.
  • Choose one small way to mark it: a walk they loved, their favorite meal, a candle.
  • Tell people what you need, including if you want them to say the person's name.
  • Try a new tradition if the old ones feel too painful.

Some days will catch you off guard months or years later. That does not mean you are going backward. It is also fine to laugh and enjoy yourself on these days, and it does not mean you loved them any less.

When to get more help for grief

Ordinary grief can be very painful and still be normal. But for some people it stays stuck. Talk to a doctor or a grief counselor or therapist if, many months after the loss (often around a year), you notice:

  • Intense longing or constant preoccupation with the person most days
  • Feeling unable to accept the death, or as if part of you has died
  • Numbness, or no positive feelings at all
  • Feeling life has no meaning without them
  • Being unable to work, care for yourself or keep up relationships

Seek help sooner for signs of depression that last more than a couple of weeks, panic attacks, flashbacks after a sudden or violent death, or drinking to cope. Bereavement counseling and support groups are often available free or at low cost through doctors, hospices, community groups and charities. Asking for help is a sensible step, not a failure.

If you have thoughts of ending your life or of not wanting to be here, get help right now. In the US or Canada call or text 988. In the UK call Samaritans on 116 123 (free, 24 hours), NHS 111, or 999 in an emergency. In Australia call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand call or text 1737. Or call your local emergency number.

Frequently asked questions

How long does grief last?

There is no set timetable. The most intense, raw period often eases over months, but grief can return in waves for years, especially around anniversaries. Many people find grief does not end so much as change: life slowly grows around the loss. If your grief is still as intense as the early weeks many months later and stops you functioning, talk to a doctor or counselor.

Is it normal to feel angry or relieved after someone dies?

Yes. Anger is a common part of grief, whether it is aimed at doctors, family, the person who died or yourself. Relief is also common, especially after a long illness or a hard caring role. Neither feeling means you did not love the person. Talking these feelings through with someone you trust can ease the guilt that often comes with them.

Why can't I concentrate or remember things since the death?

Grief uses a lot of mental energy, so many people lose their train of thought, forget appointments or struggle to read. This is often called grief fog or grief brain and usually eases with time. Keep a notebook for dates and tasks, set reminders and avoid big decisions for now. See a doctor if it does not improve or is getting worse.

What should I say to someone who is grieving?

Keep it simple and honest: "I'm so sorry. I'm thinking of you." Saying the person's name and sharing a memory is often welcome. Avoid phrases like "everything happens for a reason" or "at least they had a long life." Offer specific help, such as bringing a meal on Tuesday, and keep in touch in the months after the funeral, when support often fades.

Can grief make you physically ill?

Grief can affect your body in real ways, including tiredness, aches, headaches, upset stomach, poor sleep and catching more colds. Existing health problems may flare up. Keep eating, drinking and taking prescribed medicines, and see your doctor if symptoms last. Never assume chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting is grief: call emergency services straight away.

How do I explain death to a child?

Use clear, honest words such as "died" and "dead", not "went to sleep" or "lost", which can confuse or frighten children. Answer questions simply and expect the same questions again. Keep routines steady, tell the school, and let older children choose whether to go to the funeral after you explain what happens. Seek help if a child's distress is severe or lasting.

For today, choose just one small anchor, such as opening the curtains and standing in the daylight for a minute, and write three steps for your next grief wave on a card you can carry.
