How to Grow Vegetables in Pots: An Easy Guide for Any Balcony

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

No garden bed? You can still pick your own tomatoes, salad and herbs. To grow vegetables in pots, choose containers with drainage holes that are big enough for each crop, fill them with potting mix rather than garden soil, put sun-loving crops where they get 6 to 8 hours of direct sun, water deeply whenever the top inch feels dry, and feed fruiting plants once they flower.

This guide gives you the pot sizes, a crop choice for every kind of space, a watering check, a simple feeding plan and gentle fixes for slugs, aphids and other common problems.

Read your space before you buy anything

Spend a day watching your balcony, patio or doorstep. Note when direct sun arrives and leaves, how windy it gets and how far you have to carry water. Those three things decide what will grow far more than your enthusiasm does.

  • Sun. Fruiting crops such as tomatoes, peppers, beans and zucchini need 6 to 8 hours of direct sun. Leafy greens and herbs like parsley, mint and chives cope with 3 to 4 hours.
  • Weight. Wet potting mix is heavy. On a balcony, check weight limits with your landlord or building manager before filling large pots.
  • Wind dries pots fast and snaps tall plants, so sheltered corners suit tomatoes and beans.
  • Water access matters, because in summer some pots need water daily.

What size pot do vegetables need

Small pots dry out quickly and stunt plants, so size is the single biggest decision. These are practical starting points for one plant or a small group. One gallon here means a US gallon, about 3.8 liters.

CropContainer volumeSuggested depth
Bush or cherry tomato5 to 10 gal (20 to 40 L)12 in (30 cm) or more
Compact zucchini (courgette)5 to 10 gal (20 to 40 L)12 in (30 cm) or more
Pepper, chili or eggplant (aubergine)3 to 5 gal (12 to 20 L)10 to 12 in (25 to 30 cm)
Bush beans or peas, small group3 to 5 gal (12 to 20 L)10 to 12 in (25 to 30 cm)
Short carrots2 to 5 gal (8 to 20 L)10 to 12 in (25 to 30 cm)
Lettuce, radishes, most herbs1 to 2 gal (4 to 8 L)6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm)
Strawberries, per plant1 to 2 gal (4 to 8 L)8 in (20 cm) or more
Potatoes in a grow bag10 gal (40 L) or more16 in (40 cm) or more

Every pot needs drainage holes, ideally several across the base. Do not add gravel or broken pottery in the bottom as a substitute; it does not improve drainage and steals root space. If a lovely planter has no holes, use it as an outer cover with a draining pot inside.

Choosing the best containers

There is no perfect material, and mixing them is fine.

MaterialProsWatch out
PlasticLight, affordable, holds moistureDark colors overheat in sun
TerracottaBreathable, stableDries quickly, can crack in frost
Fabric grow bagsGood airflow, easy to storeDry faster, may need support
WoodInsulates rootsCan rot; avoid unknown treatments
MetalDurableHeats quickly in direct sun

A practical mix is light plastic for tomatoes, terracotta for rosemary and thyme, which like drier roots, and fabric bags for potatoes. On hot balconies, pale pots keep roots cooler than black ones. Upcycled containers work if they are clean, food-safe and you add drainage holes. Self-watering pots with a reservoir underneath are a good choice if you are often away.

Use potting mix, never garden soil

Garden soil compacts in a pot after a few waterings, squeezing out the air roots need, and it can bring in weed seeds and pests. Buy a bag labeled potting mix or container compost. In the UK, look for multipurpose or container compost, with peat-free options widely available.

A good mix stays loose when squeezed, holds moisture without staying soggy and has a fairly even texture. You do not need the most expensive bag; a reliable general-purpose mix usually beats an elaborate homemade recipe.

A simple feeding plan

  • Mix a slow-release fertilizer into the potting mix at planting, following the label.
  • For tomatoes, peppers, zucchini and strawberries, switch to a liquid high-potassium feed (often sold as tomato feed) once flowers appear. Many labels suggest every 7 to 14 days, but follow yours.
  • Lettuce, radishes, beans and peas usually need little or no extra feeding in fresh mix.
  • Keep rosemary and thyme lean, as rich feeding softens growth and dulls flavor.

More is not better. Overfeeding can scorch roots and give you lush leaves with few fruits.

How often to water vegetables in pots

Your finger is the best tool. Push it about 1 inch (2.5 cm) into the mix away from the stem. Dry at that depth means water; moist means check again later. Small pots can also be judged by lifting them, since a dry pot feels much lighter.

When you water, pour slowly until water runs out of the drainage holes. A quick splash on top leaves the deeper roots dry. Mornings are usually best, with an evening top-up in hot spells.

ConditionsCheck moisture
Cool, cloudy weatherEvery 1 to 2 days
Mild growing weatherDaily
Hot, sunny weatherMorning and evening
Windy conditionsAt least daily
WinterEvery few days, often much less water

To save effort, group pots together so they shade each other, add a layer of mulch on top of the mix and use bigger pots, which dry more slowly. Saucers protect floors, but empty standing water after it drains, especially in cool weather. If very dry mix sheds water down the sides, water in several slow rounds.

Best vegetables to grow in pots for beginners

Start with crops you actually eat, and keep the first season small. A sensible first pair in part sun is a pot of parsley and a container of loose-leaf lettuce. With 6 to 8 hours of sun, try a compact cherry tomato with a pot of basil.

  • Cherry and bush tomatoes. Support plants before they get heavy. For cordon types grown on one stem, pinch out the small side shoots that form between the main stem and leaf branches. Leave bush types largely alone, or you remove future fruit.
  • Lettuce and salad leaves: pick outer leaves and the plant keeps producing. Sow a little every two to three weeks for a steady supply.
  • Radishes: often ready in a few weeks from sowing.
  • Peppers, chilies and bush beans: compact, productive and happy in 3 to 5 gallon pots. Pick beans often to keep more coming.
  • Herbs: chives, parsley, thyme and rosemary, with mint always in its own pot because its roots take over.
  • Potatoes in a grow bag: add more mix around the stems as they grow.

Tomatoes, peppers, beans, squash and basil are tender, so plant them outside only after your last frost date. Frost dates vary a lot, so check a local source. In the Southern Hemisphere, the seasons are reversed.

Gentle fixes for common pests

Pots make pest control easier because you can inspect every plant up close. A quick weekly look under leaves catches most problems early.

Many problems look alike, so look for more than one clue before acting. The book Container Vegetable Gardening Made Simple has a fuller problem solver and a season-by-season planting calendar for both hemispheres.

Tomato and potato leaves can be toxic to pets, so keep curious cats and dogs away from them. Never eat green potatoes. Wash your hands after handling potting mix, and wash all produce before eating.

Frequently asked questions

What vegetables are easiest to grow in pots?

Loose-leaf lettuce, radishes, bush beans, chard and herbs such as chives and parsley are among the easiest. They grow in modest pots, tolerate less sun than fruiting crops and give quick harvests. Cherry and bush tomatoes are also rewarding if you can give them 6 to 8 hours of direct sun and a pot of at least 5 gallons (20 liters).

What size pot do I need for tomatoes?

A compact or bush tomato needs at least 5 gallons (about 20 liters) of potting mix, and a vigorous cherry tomato often does better in 10 gallons (about 40 liters), with a depth of 12 inches (30 cm) or more. Smaller pots dry out very quickly in summer, which can lead to split fruit and blossom end rot.

Can I use garden soil in containers?

No. Garden soil compacts inside a pot after repeated watering, leaving few air spaces around the roots, and water can sit in the pot too long. It may also carry weed seeds, pests and diseases. Use a bagged potting mix or container compost instead, which stays light, absorbs water readily and drains well.

How much sun do container vegetables need?

Fruiting crops such as tomatoes, peppers, beans and zucchini need about 6 to 8 hours of direct sun a day. Leafy greens and herbs like lettuce, parsley, mint and chives can manage with 3 to 4 hours. Map your space over a day before buying plants, and put your sunniest spot to work for the crops that need it most.

Can you grow vegetables in pots on a balcony?

Yes, balconies are great for salad, herbs, peppers and compact tomatoes. Check sun hours and wind first, and ask your landlord or building about weight limits, because wet potting mix is heavy. Use saucers where drips would fall on neighbors, but empty them after watering, and secure tall plants and supports against wind.

Do container plants need fertilizer?

Most do, because nutrients wash out of pots with every watering. Start with a slow-release fertilizer mixed into the potting mix, then give tomatoes, peppers, zucchini and strawberries a liquid high-potassium feed once they flower, following the label. Leafy crops, radishes, beans and peas usually need little extra. Avoid overfeeding, which can scorch roots.

Your first step today: watch your space and note how many hours of direct sun it gets, then pick two crops that suit it, such as lettuce and parsley for part sun or a cherry tomato and basil for full sun. Buy one bag of potting mix and two pots with drainage holes, and you are ready to plant this weekend.
