What to Eat With Kidney Disease: A Calm Guide to the Renal Diet

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have just been told your kidneys are not working as well as they should, you may have left the clinic with a list of foods to avoid and no idea why. The short answer: most people with kidney disease are advised to eat less salt and fewer processed foods, while limits on potassium, phosphorus, protein and fluids depend on your stage and your blood test results, so only limit what your own kidney team asks you to.

This guide explains the five nutrients your team watches, which foods tend to fit best, a simple way to read labels, sample meal ideas and the warning signs that need urgent help. It is general information to bring to your appointments, not a personal plan.

Why food matters with kidney disease

Your kidneys filter waste from your blood, balance water, keep minerals such as sodium, potassium and phosphorus at safe levels, help control blood pressure and support your blood and bones. When they work less well, waste and extra minerals can build up, fluid can collect in the ankles or lungs and blood pressure can rise.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is grouped into stages 1 to 5, based mainly on a blood test called eGFR plus other tests such as protein in the urine. Stages 1 and 2 mean some damage with filtering close to normal or mildly lower, stage 3 means moderately lower function, stage 4 severely lower and stage 5 kidney failure, where dialysis or a transplant may be needed.

Food is one place where you can lighten the load. But the right kidney diet is the one written for you, right now. Advice in early stages is often gentle and focused on salt, blood pressure and blood sugar. Later stages may add limits on potassium, phosphorus or protein, and dialysis changes things again. Cutting out every food on every list at once often leads to eating too little and losing muscle, which is its own problem.

The five nutrients your kidney team watches

NutrientWhy it mattersWho usually limits it
SodiumRaises blood pressure, thirst and swellingAlmost everyone, often under about 2,300 mg a day
PotassiumToo much in the blood can upset heart rhythmOnly people whose blood potassium runs high
PhosphorusBuilds up and can weaken bones and harm blood vesselsMany people in later stages and on dialysis
ProteinBuilds muscle but makes waste the kidneys must clearModerate before dialysis; more is usually needed on dialysis
FluidsExtra fluid can cause swelling and breathlessnessMainly people on dialysis or with significant swelling

If you also have diabetes, your plan has to fit both conditions. White rice, pasta and white bread, often suggested for lower potassium and phosphorus, still raise blood sugar, so portion size matters. A renal dietitian can balance the two for you. If you do not have one, ask your kidney doctor or family doctor (GP) for a referral.

Cutting sodium without losing flavor

One level teaspoon of salt holds roughly 2,300 mg of sodium, a full day's limit for many people. Most sodium does not come from the salt shaker, though. It comes from bread, cheese, deli meats, canned soups, sauces, ready meals, takeout and snacks. Cooking more meals from fresh ingredients is the biggest step you can take.

Build flavor in other ways:

  • Fresh garlic and onion, or plain garlic and onion powder (not garlic salt).
  • Lemon and lime juice and zest, and vinegars such as cider or rice vinegar.
  • Fresh herbs like parsley, dill, basil and chives, plus black pepper, paprika, cumin and ginger.
  • Browning: roasting, searing and slowly cooking onions add deep, savory flavor.

Your taste buds adjust to less salt within a few weeks, and many people find old favorites start to taste too salty.

Salt substitutes and "lite salt" products often replace sodium with potassium chloride, which can push blood potassium to dangerous levels in kidney disease. Do not use them unless your kidney team has said they are safe for you.

Low potassium foods and swaps

Not everyone with kidney disease needs to limit potassium. Some people are told to eat a wide range of fruit and vegetables. If your blood potassium runs high, the goal is not zero: it is choosing lower potassium foods more often and watching portions.

Generally lower potassiumHigher potassium (check with your dietitian)
Apples, berries, grapes, pineappleBananas, oranges and orange juice
Cabbage, cauliflower, green beansPotatoes and sweet potatoes
Bell peppers, onions, cucumber, lettuceTomatoes and tomato sauces
Zucchini (courgette)Spinach and cooked leafy greens, avocado
White rice, pasta, white bread, couscousBeans, lentils and dried fruit

If your dietitian agrees, leaching removes some potassium from root vegetables: peel, cut into thin slices about 1/8 inch (3 mm) thick, rinse, soak in plenty of warm water for at least 2 hours or bring to a boil, drain, then cook again in fresh water. Always discard the cooking water and never use it for gravy. Drain and rinse canned (tinned) vegetables too. Leached vegetables are still not unlimited.

Phosphorus and hidden phosphate additives

Phosphorus occurs naturally in meat, fish, eggs, dairy, nuts, seeds, whole grains, beans and lentils. Your body absorbs only part of it. Phosphate additives in processed food are absorbed much more easily, often almost completely, so avoiding them lets you cut a lot of phosphorus without giving up protein.

Additives are common in deli meats, hot dogs, sausages, chicken nuggets, processed cheese slices and spreads, some cake and pancake mixes, dark colas, some bottled iced teas and some frozen meals. Phosphorus is often missing from the nutrition panel, so read the ingredients list instead. Look for any word containing "phos", such as phosphoric acid, sodium phosphate or disodium phosphate, and choose a plainer version.

If your doctor prescribes a phosphate binder, it only works when taken with food, so take it with meals and snacks exactly as directed. Never change the dose yourself.

Protein and fluids by stage

Protein advice changes more sharply than anything else. People not on dialysis are often advised to eat moderate or sometimes reduced amounts. People on dialysis usually need more, because dialysis removes some protein. Going too low can cause muscle loss and weakness, so never cut protein on your own.

As a rough guide, a portion of cooked meat, chicken or fish is about the size of a deck of cards or your palm, or one to two eggs. Your dietitian will tell you how many portions suit you.

Most people in early stages do not need to limit fluids unless told. If you have a fluid limit, anything liquid at room temperature counts, including soup, ice, ice cream, gelatin (jelly) and larger amounts of sauce. To cope with thirst:

  • Eat less salt, the biggest cause of thirst.
  • Drink from a small cup and spread fluids across the day.
  • Rinse your mouth with cold water and spit it out.
  • Measure ice chips and count them in your total, as your team advises.

Kidney-friendly meal ideas

Many kidney-friendly meals are ordinary family food cooked without salt. For someone limiting potassium and phosphorus, a day might look like this:

  • Breakfast: a herb and onion omelet with white toast, or a plain bagel with cream cheese, cucumber and dill.
  • Lunch: a chicken salad sandwich with grapes and celery, or a cucumber and dill salad with a small portion of tuna or egg.
  • Dinner: lemon dill cod with herbed white rice and lemon green beans, or a garlic chicken and pepper stir-fry with white rice.
  • Snacks: unsalted herb popcorn, an apple, a few grapes or unsalted crackers.

For a simple salt-free seasoning, mix garlic powder, onion powder, dried oregano, paprika and black pepper in a jar and use it on chicken, fish and vegetables. Cooking for the family is easy: serve higher potassium sides such as baked potatoes on the side for others, and let anyone who wants salt add a pinch at the table. Shopping and label reading for kidney health

Check three things when you pick up a packaged food:

  1. Sodium. In the US and Canada, 5% Daily Value or less per serving is low and 20% or more is high. In the UK, Australia and New Zealand, compare per 100 g; green for salt on a UK traffic light label is a good sign. Salt is about two and a half times the sodium figure.
  2. The ingredients list, for "phos" words and, if you limit potassium, potassium chloride.
  3. Meat and poultry labels. Some fresh chicken and meat is "enhanced" or injected with a solution of salt water and phosphates. Choose packs that list only the meat itself.

When eating out, choose grilled, roasted or baked meat or fish with plain rice, pasta or bread, ask for sauces on the side and for no added salt, and bring your binders if you take them.

Talk to your kidney doctor, renal dietitian or pharmacist before changing your diet, and check before taking any herbal remedy, supplement or over-the-counter medicine, including some pain relievers and cold remedies. Avoid star fruit (carambola) and its juice completely, as it contains a toxin damaged kidneys cannot clear.

Warning signs that need urgent help

Call 911 in the US or 999 in the UK for chest pain, severe shortness of breath or fainting.

Contact your kidney team promptly or get urgent care for:

  • Muscle weakness, numbness or tingling, or an irregular, slow or racing heartbeat, which can be signs of high potassium.
  • Sudden severe swelling or much less urine than usual.
  • Confusion or extreme drowsiness.
  • On dialysis: fever, redness, swelling or bleeding at your access site, or a sudden large weight gain between sessions.

Keep your team's phone numbers on the fridge, and if you are unsure whether a symptom is serious, call and ask.

Frequently asked questions

What foods should I avoid with kidney disease?

Almost everyone should cut back on salty and processed foods such as deli meats, canned soups, ready meals and salty snacks. Avoid star fruit completely, and avoid salt substitutes made with potassium unless your team approves. Whether you also need to limit potassium, phosphorus, protein or fluids depends on your stage and blood results, so only limit those foods if your kidney team asks you to.

What is a renal diet?

A renal diet is an eating plan for people with kidney disease that controls the nutrients damaged kidneys struggle to balance: sodium, potassium, phosphorus, protein and fluids. It is not one fixed list. Early stages usually focus on lower salt, blood pressure and blood sugar, while later stages and dialysis can add other limits. A renal dietitian sets the details based on your blood tests.

Are eggs good for kidney disease?

Eggs are a useful, good-quality protein for many people with kidney disease, and they are often recommended for people on dialysis, who usually need more protein. Egg yolks contain some natural phosphorus, so if your phosphorus is high, your dietitian may suggest how many whole eggs to eat or suggest egg whites. Ask how eggs fit into your protein portions.

Should I drink more water with kidney disease?

Not necessarily. Most people in early stages do not need to limit fluids and can drink normally unless told otherwise, but forcing large amounts is not usually advised either. People on dialysis or with significant swelling are often given a daily fluid limit, which includes soups, ice and other foods liquid at room temperature. Follow your own kidney team's advice on how much to drink.

Can diet reverse kidney disease?

Diet cannot reverse chronic kidney disease or replace treatment. Eating less salt, following your team's advice on potassium, phosphorus, protein and fluids, and keeping blood pressure and blood sugar well controlled can help protect kidney health and ease the load on your kidneys. Take your medicines as prescribed and keep your regular blood tests and appointments.

Is rice good for kidney disease?

White rice is often a good fit for people limiting potassium and phosphorus, because it is lower in both than brown rice and many whole grains. Cook it without salt and flavor it with herbs, lemon or garlic instead. If you also have diabetes, keep an eye on portion size, since white rice raises blood sugar. Your dietitian can tell you how much suits you.

Your first step today: write down your stage, your daily sodium goal and whether you need to limit potassium, phosphorus, protein or fluids, leaving blanks for anything you are not sure about. Take that page to your next appointment and fill in the gaps with your kidney team, then start by cooking one fresh, salt-free dinner this week.
