How to Deal With Toddler Tantrums: Calm Steps That Really Help

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your toddler just collapsed on the kitchen floor because their cereal went in the wrong bowl, you are in very good company. To deal with a toddler tantrum, stay calm yourself, keep everyone safe, stay close while using very few words, hold the limit kindly instead of giving in, and reconnect with a cuddle once the storm passes, saving any short talk for later.

Tantrums are a normal part of life between ages 1 and 5, not a sign that you are failing. This guide explains why they happen, how to prevent many of them, exactly what to do before, during and after, and when it is worth talking to a doctor.

Why toddlers have tantrums

A tantrum is a flood of feeling. Your toddler's emotions are big and fast, while the parts of the brain that calm those feelings are still developing. Their words also lag far behind what they feel, so frustration comes out as screaming, kicking or flopping on the floor.

In the middle of a tantrum, your child cannot think clearly or take in reasons. It is a bit like talking to someone mid-sneeze. What they can take in is your tone, your face and your presence, which is why how you act matters more than what you say.

Tantrums are not manipulation in the adult sense. Toddlers do learn what works, so if screaming reliably gets a cookie (biscuit), you will hear more screaming. But the feeling itself is real. You can respect the feeling and still keep the limit.

How to prevent toddler tantrums

You will not prevent every tantrum, and you should not try. Some are simply a toddler meeting a limit they dislike. But most meltdowns have a backstory, and four triggers sit behind a large share of them.

TriggerWhat helps
HungerFood every few hours: 3 meals and 2 or 3 planned snacks, plus one in your bag
TirednessProtect naps, watch for eye rubbing or wild energy, earlier bedtime after a missed nap
Sudden changeConcrete warnings such as "Two more turns, then home" or a timer
No saySmall real choices: red cup or yellow cup, hop or walk to the car

When you feel a storm brewing, run through four quick questions: when did they last eat, how did they sleep, what just changed, and do they feel bossed around? The answer is often in the first two.

Routines help too. A predictable rhythm for mornings, meals and bedtime means fewer fights over every step. Swap questions for warm statements: "Do you want to leave now?" invites "No," while "It's time to leave. Do you want to hop or walk like a giant?" keeps the plan and hands over a little control.

What to do when the tantrum starts building

Most meltdowns give early signs: a whiny voice, a tight face, refusing everything or getting rough with toys. That is your window. Get down to your child's level and name what you see: "You're getting really frustrated with that puzzle."

  • Offer help, a break, a drink of water or a snack.
  • Change the scene, such as moving to another room or stepping outside.
  • Try a bit of silliness, like a funny voice or a toothbrush that is suddenly a train.
  • Lower demands for a few minutes if you can.

If a limit is involved, be kind about it: acknowledge the wish, state the limit and offer something they can have. "You wish you could have another cookie. Cookies are done for today. You can have an apple or some cheese."

What to do during a tantrum

Once the flood starts, your job is not to fix it or end it fast. It is to keep everyone safe and be the calm one in the room. A simple sequence helps:

  1. Calm yourself first. Breathe in through your nose and out slowly through your mouth. Tell yourself, "This is a tantrum, not an emergency." Looking calm on the outside is enough to start.
  2. Keep everyone safe. Move breakable or dangerous things away. If your child is about to hit, step in: "I won't let you hit. I'm going to hold your hands." In a parking lot (car park), carry them somewhere safe, screaming or not.
  3. Stay close and say little. Some children want holding; others need you sitting a short distance away. Use a few soft phrases: "I'm here." "You're so mad."
  4. Hold the limit kindly. Giving in to stop the noise teaches that tantrums work. You can be warm while the answer stays the same.
  5. Wait for the wave to pass. You will often hear the crying change from angry to sad, or see your child look for comfort. That is your cue to open your arms.

You can still change your mind if a limit was unnecessary, like the wrong-colored bowl. Just do it because you rethought it, ideally early, and not because the screaming got louder.

Never shake a child, for any reason. If you feel you might lose control, put your child somewhere safe, such as their crib (cot) or a room with nothing dangerous in it, and step away for a few minutes to breathe. If you often feel this way, or feel low, hopeless or overwhelmed for weeks, talk to your doctor. Asking for support is a strong, sensible step.

What to say to a toddler having a tantrum

Fewer words are better. A brain that is already overloaded cannot process a speech. These short lines work in most situations:

  • "I'm here. I'll stay with you."
  • "You're so mad that we're leaving. It's okay to be mad."
  • "We're still going home. I'll help you into your car seat."
  • "I won't let you kick. I'm going to move back a little."
  • "You really wanted that. It's hard when we can't have things."

Avoid threats, bribes, shaming and lectures. Spanking or harsh punishment may stop the noise in the moment, but it teaches fear and that big people hurt small people when they are upset, which tends to make behavior harder over time.

If your child shouts "I hate you!", treat it as a toddler's way of saying "I'm really, really angry." A calm "You're very angry with me right now. I still love you" shows that big feelings do not break your relationship.

After the tantrum is over

Reconnect first. A hug, a smile or a quiet "That was a big feeling. You got through it" tells your child they are still loved after falling apart. That message matters more than any lesson.

Resist the urge to lecture straight away. Your child's brain is still settling, so a speech will not stick and may restart the tears. Later, at bedtime or the next day, talk briefly about feelings and what to try next time: "You were very angry when we left the park. Next time you could stomp your feet and say 'I'm mad!'" For a 2-year-old, one sentence is plenty.

If you yelled, repair it: "I yelled earlier and I'm sorry. I was frustrated, but yelling isn't okay. I'm working on staying calm." Repair shows your child how to make things right after a mistake, which is a skill they will use for life.

Handling a tantrum in public

Public meltdowns add an audience, and with it the pressure to do something fast, which often means giving in or getting harsh. Most people watching have been there themselves, and the few who judge are not raising your child.

  • Move somewhere quieter if you can: outside, your car or a calm corner. Leaving a cart behind is fine.
  • Keep your voice low and your words few.
  • Hold the limit you set. Buying the candy now teaches that public screaming works.
  • Have a ready phrase for onlookers: "We're okay, thanks. Just a big feeling."
  • Move on afterward. You owe nobody an explanation.

For example: "You wanted that toy so much. We're not buying it today. Let's go outside and take some big breaths. We can take a picture of it to remember." Prevention helps here as well: shop when your child is fed and rested, keep trips short and bring a snack and a small book.

When to talk to a doctor about tantrums

Tantrums on their own are common and usually not a cause for concern. It makes sense to check in with your pediatrician, family doctor (GP) or health visitor if tantrums are very frequent and intense and come with other worries, such as:

  • Your child regularly hurting themselves or others
  • Losing skills they used to have
  • Very little speech for their age
  • Seeming unusually unhappy much of the time
  • Ongoing problems with sleep, eating or growth

You know your child best. If something feels off, it is always okay to ask, and a professional can help you understand what is going on without guessing.

To make the plan stick, write six words on a sticky note for the fridge: breathe, safe, close, quiet, limit, reconnect. Share it with your partner, grandparents and babysitters so your child meets the same calm response from everyone. Frequently asked questions

What age do toddler tantrums stop?

Tantrums are common from about age 1 and often peak somewhere between 2 and 3, then usually become less frequent as language and self-control grow through the preschool years, up to around 4 or 5. Every child is different. If tantrums stay very frequent and intense, or come with other worries, talk to your pediatrician, GP or health visitor.

Should I ignore my toddler's tantrum?

It usually works better to stay nearby and calm rather than ignore your child completely. You do not need to argue, bargain or give in, but your presence shows they are not alone with a huge feeling. Use very few words, keep everyone safe and hold the limit. Some children prefer a little space, so sit close without forcing touch.

Is it normal for a 2-year-old to have tantrums every day?

Yes, daily tantrums are common at this age, especially when a child is hungry, tired or facing a change. Look for patterns: the time of day, missed naps or long gaps between snacks. Small changes, like an earlier snack or a warning before leaving the park, often reduce how many happen. Check with a doctor if you have other worries about development.

How do I stop a tantrum fast?

Once a tantrum is in full swing, there is rarely a fast fix, and trying to rush it often makes it longer. Focus on staying calm, keeping your child safe and saying very little. The quickest wins come before the meltdown: notice early signs, offer food, rest or a choice, and give clear warnings before transitions.

Should I give in to stop a tantrum?

Try not to. If a tantrum ends because your child got what they were screaming for, they learn that tantrums work, which usually means more of them. Acknowledge the feeling warmly and keep the limit. If you realize a limit was not needed, it is fine to change your mind, ideally early and because you rethought it.

What if my toddler hits or bites during a tantrum?

Step in calmly and firmly to stop it: "I won't let you hit," and gently hold their hands or move out of reach. Keep other children safe first. Avoid hitting back or shouting, which teaches the opposite of what you want. Later, when everyone is calm, practice gentle hands and words they can use when angry.

Today, write your six-word plan (breathe, safe, close, quiet, limit, reconnect) on a sticky note and put it where you will see it during the hardest part of your day, then check that the next tricky stretch, like the drive home or the hour before dinner, has a snack and a clear warning built in.
