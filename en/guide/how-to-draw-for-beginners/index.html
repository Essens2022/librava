How to Draw for Beginners: 8 Simple Steps From Lines to Shading

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have always wanted to draw, but somewhere along the way someone told you that you had no talent, and your stick figures still look a little unwell. The short answer: drawing is a set of learnable skills, and you can learn them with an HB and a 2B pencil, plain paper and 15 to 20 minutes a day by practicing looking, steady lines, simple shapes, measuring and shading, in that order.

This guide walks you through each of those skills with exercises you can do today using things from around your home, such as a mug, an apple, a shoe and your own hand. At the end you will find a simple 30-day plan to turn it into a habit.

What you need to start drawing

You can begin with very little, and expensive kit will not make your first drawings better. Here is a sensible starter set:

  • An HB pencil and a 2B pencil, with a 4B and a 6B to add later
  • A plastic eraser, plus a kneaded eraser (putty rubber) if you can find one
  • A pencil sharpener
  • A sketchbook or a stack of plain printer paper
  • A table, a supportive chair and good light

Pencil letters tell you how hard the lead is. H pencils are harder and lighter; B pencils are softer and darker. HB sits in the middle.

GradeMarkBest for
HBMedium greySketching and guide lines
2BSlightly darkerOutlines and midtones
4BSoft and darkShadows and texture
6BVery soft, very darkThe deepest darks

Use light pressure with a softer pencil rather than pressing hard with a harder one. Heavy pressure dents the paper and leaves shiny grooves you cannot shade over.

Learn to see what is really in front of you

Your brain is brilliant at recognizing a mug in a split second, and that speed works against you when you draw. It hands you a symbol: a flat rim, a neat C for a handle, perfectly straight sides. A real mug has an oval rim, a handle that is thicker at the top and one side in shadow.

Try this quick test. Draw a shoe from memory for 30 seconds. Then put your real shoe on the table and draw it for 2 minutes. The first drawing is a symbol; the second, however wobbly, contains things you actually noticed.

Two exercises that train your eye

  • Contour drawing. Follow the edge of your relaxed hand slowly with your eyes while your pencil moves at the same speed. Look at your hand about 80 percent of the time and the paper 20 percent. Spend a full 10 minutes and include creases and nails.
  • Blind contour drawing. Do the same without looking at the paper at all. The result will look funny, with fingers like bananas, and that is fine. It builds the link between eye and hand.

Another favorite is copying a simple printed photo upside down. Because your brain cannot easily name the parts, it shows you lines, angles and shapes instead.

How to draw steady, confident lines

Shaky, scratchy lines are the most common beginner problem, and they come from drawing with the fingers and wrist only. Use these habits instead:

  • Hold the pencil a little further back than you would for writing.
  • Use your writing grip (the tripod grip) for small details, and an overhand grip, with your palm over the pencil, for long lines and broad shading.
  • Move from the elbow or shoulder for any line longer than your finger.
  • Keep your grip relaxed. White knuckles mean you are squeezing too hard.

Ghosting is the single most useful line habit. Mark a start dot and an end dot, hover the pencil just above the paper, look at the end dot and rehearse the movement several times. Then lower the pencil and draw the line in one stroke, still looking at where it is going.

If a line goes wrong, do not erase it straight away. Draw a better light line beside it and darken the one you like later. A single slightly curved line drawn with confidence always looks better than a straight line built from dozens of hairy scratches.

A 5-minute warm-up makes your first real drawing better. Fill a page with straight lines between dots, rows of circles, ellipses that go round two or three times and a few spirals and waves, all drawn from the shoulder.

Break objects into simple shapes

Almost everything you will ever draw can be built from four basic forms: the sphere, the cube, the cylinder and the cone. An apple is a sphere with a dent, a mug is a cylinder with a curved handle, a lamp is a cone sitting on a cylinder and a book is a flattened box.

Start by drawing each form on its own:

  1. Sphere: draw a circle, then add a light curved line across it to show it is round.
  2. Cube: draw a square, a second square offset behind it, and join the corners.
  3. Cylinder: draw an ellipse on top, two straight sides and a curved bottom edge that matches the top ellipse.
  4. Cone: draw an ellipse for the base and two lines meeting at a point above its center.

Ellipses matter a lot here. An ellipse is a circle seen at an angle, and it always has rounded ends, never pointed ones like a lemon. The closer an ellipse is to your eye level, the flatter it looks, so the rim of a mug looks flatter than its base. Draw around each ellipse two or three times in one movement to smooth it out.

Then go on a shape hunt around your home. Pick a kettle, a bottle or a lamp and sketch it lightly as stacked forms before adding any detail.

How to get proportions right

Your eye is wonderful at recognizing things and poor at judging their size on paper, so it enlarges parts it thinks are important. The fix is to stop guessing and start comparing, using your pencil as a measuring stick. This is called sighting.

  1. Sit where you can see your object and paper without moving your head, and stay there.
  2. Hold the pencil upright at arm's length with your elbow locked straight.
  3. Close one eye.
  4. Line the tip up with one edge of the object and slide your thumb to the opposite edge. That length is your unit.
  5. Compare other parts against it, for example how many rim widths fit into the mug's height.

You copy ratios, not actual sizes. If the mug is one and a half units tall, it should be one and a half times as tall as it is wide on paper, whatever size you draw it. Shoes surprise most people: they are often 3 to 4 times longer than they are tall.

Two more checks catch the rest. Draw the negative spaces, such as the hole inside a mug handle or the gaps between chair legs, because your brain has no fixed idea of how they should look. And hold your drawing up to a small mirror, or turn it upside down, before you start shading. Mistakes jump out instantly.

Basic shading for beginners

Value means how light or dark something is. Lines show edges, but values show the shape between them, and most beginners shade too lightly and never use real darks.

Start with a value scale: draw a strip of 5 to 7 boxes, leave the first as white paper, make the last as dark as your 4B or 6B allows, put a halfway grey in the middle and fill the steps between. Squint at it to check the jumps are even.

Then put an orange or a pale ball under a single desk lamp and look for these five parts:

PartWhere to find it
HighlightThe brightest spot, facing the light
MidtoneThe gentle grey between light and dark
Core shadowThe darkest band, a little inside the edge
Reflected lightA faint glow near the bottom of the shadow side
Cast shadowOn the table, darkest where the ball touches it

Shade in layers: block in the shadow shapes lightly, lay a pale tone over everything except the highlight, add the shadow side and cast shadow, push the darkest darks, then lift highlights with a kneaded eraser. You can build tone with smooth graphite, hatching (parallel lines), cross-hatching (crossed lines) or stippling (dots).

Blend gently with a paper blending stump or a tissue, never your finger, since skin oils leave patches that refuse graphite. And skip the dark outline around the object. Real objects have edges where one value meets another, not black lines.

Easy things to draw for beginners

Draw real objects from life rather than copying other drawings, because looking is the skill you are building. Good first subjects, roughly in order of difficulty:

  1. A mug, a jar or a glass, for ellipses and cylinders
  2. An apple or an orange, for shading a sphere
  3. A shoe, for proportions and curves
  4. A houseplant or a few leaves, for organic shapes
  5. Your own hand, for contour drawing
  6. A pet, using quick 1-minute gesture sketches of ovals and a single curved line of action
  7. A simple face, using basic proportions

For a face seen from the front, the eyes sit about halfway down the whole head, which shocks most people since we tend to put them too high. There is about one eye width between the eyes, the bottom of the nose sits about halfway between the eyes and the chin, and the mouth is about a third of the way from nose to chin.

For a first still life, set up an apple, a mug and a bottle under one lamp, overlapping slightly and at different heights. Make three small thumbnail sketches of different arrangements, pick the best one and draw it over two or three short sessions: big shapes first, then shadows, then textures.

A 30-day drawing practice plan

Short daily sessions beat occasional long ones. Give each 15 to 20 minute session the same shape: a 2-minute warm-up, 10 to 15 minutes on the exercise with a timer, then the date and one short note under your drawing, such as rim too round or good shadows.

WeekFocusSample exercises
1Seeing and linesBlind contour of your hand, upside-down copy, ghosted line pages
2Shapes, proportion and valueFour basic forms, measured mug, negative space chair, value scale
3Perspective, still life and natureA box and a house, apple and mug still life, a houseplant
4Animals and peoplePet gesture sketches, a face with guidelines, your hand in three poses

On Day 1, draw a mug for 5 minutes without erasing and keep the page. On Day 30, draw the same mug from the same angle and put them side by side. Drawing for Beginners Made Simple sets out all 30 days in detail, with step by step exercises for each one.

If you miss a day, pick up where you left off.

Frequently asked questions

Can anyone learn to draw?

Yes. Drawing is a set of skills, not a gift you are born with: looking carefully, making steady lines, judging sizes and seeing light and shadow. Each one improves with practice, much like learning to type or drive. The people who improve fastest are usually the ones who draw something small most days and stay curious about their mistakes, not the ones who seemed talented at the start.

How long does it take to learn to draw?

Most beginners see a clear difference within about 30 days of 15 to 20 minute sessions, especially in line confidence, proportions and shading. Comparing your Day 1 drawing with the same subject on Day 30 usually shows it. Building real fluency takes months of regular practice, but you do not need to wait that long to enjoy it or to make drawings you are proud of.

What should a beginner draw first?

Start with simple, solid objects you can put on a table, such as a mug, an apple, a shoe or a jar. They teach ellipses, basic forms, proportion and shading without moving. Your own hand is a great early subject for contour drawing. Leave faces and pets until you have practiced measuring and quick gesture sketches for a couple of weeks.

Should I draw from photos or from real life?

Both have a place, but real life teaches you more at the start because you have to judge depth, light and shape yourself. Photos are useful for moving subjects such as pets and people, and copying a simple printed photo upside down is a great seeing exercise. When you do use photos, choose ones with clear light and shadow and avoid tiny screens where details disappear.

Why do my drawings look flat?

Usually your values are too close together. Beginners stay in safe middle greys and never use real darks. Pick one clear light source, push the core shadow and cast shadow darker with a 4B or 6B, and keep the highlight as clean white paper. Making a value scale first helps you see and control the full range from light to dark.

How do I stop my lines from being shaky?

Shaky lines come from drawing slowly with just the fingers and wrist. Hold the pencil further back, keep your grip relaxed and move from the elbow or shoulder. Ghost each line in the air first while looking at its end point, then draw it in one smooth stroke. A few minutes of warm-up lines and circles before each session makes a noticeable difference.

Your first step today is small: find a spot with good light, grab a pencil and some paper, write today's date at the top and draw a mug from your kitchen for 5 minutes without erasing. Put that page somewhere safe, then come back tomorrow for a blind contour drawing of your hand, and in a month you will have proof on paper of how much you have learned.
