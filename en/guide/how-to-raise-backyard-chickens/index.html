How to Raise Backyard Chickens: A Beginner's Step-by-Step Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Fresh eggs, happy hens scratching in the yard and a bit less garden waste sound lovely, and they are, with some planning first. To raise backyard chickens, check your local rules, start with 3 to 6 hens of a calm breed, give them a predator-proof coop with at least 3 to 4 sq ft (0.3 to 0.4 m²) per bird plus a run of 8 to 10 sq ft (0.75 to 1 m²) per bird, feed a complete layer feed with clean water every day, and lock them in every night.

Hens need 15 to 30 minutes of care a day and can live 5 to 10 years. This guide takes you through each step in order, from rules and breeds to feeding, health and eggs, so you can decide with confidence and start well.

Are backyard chickens right for you

Be honest about the work before you spend money. Chickens need someone every morning and every evening, in rain, snow and on holiday weekends.

  • Time: about 10 to 15 minutes morning and evening, plus a weekly clean.
  • Cost: a sturdy coop and secure run for 3 to 6 hens often costs several hundred dollars or pounds. Home eggs are rarely cheaper than store eggs, at least for the first few years.
  • Vet care: a visit to a vet who treats poultry can cost more than the hen did. Decide ahead what you are willing to spend.
  • Lifespan: hens lay best in their first couple of years but often live 5 to 10 years.
  • Holidays: you need a neighbor or friend who will let the hens out, check water and lock up every night.

Get local prices for a coop, a month of feed and a vet consultation, write them down and add 20 percent for the things you have not thought of yet. Most new keepers underestimate their first year.

Check the rules before you buy chickens

Rules vary a lot by area, so check them before you buy a single bird, and get answers in writing where you can.

  • In the US, check city or county zoning, any homeowners association (HOA) rules or covenants, and your lease if you rent. Many places limit flock size or require a set distance from neighbors' homes.
  • In the UK, check your deeds or tenancy agreement and any council rules. In Great Britain, keepers of poultry, including small backyard flocks, are now required to register with the Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA). Check the current rules where you live.
  • Roosters are often banned in towns. You do not need one for eggs, as hens lay without a rooster.
  • Authorities in both countries can order poultry to be housed during bird flu outbreaks. These orders are legal requirements, so plan a run you can cover.

Talk to your neighbors before the hens arrive. Mention how many hens, that there will be no rooster and that you will keep things clean, and offer the odd box of eggs. A friendly chat early prevents most complaints later.

Best chicken breeds for beginners

Calm, friendly breeds make the first year much easier. Every bird is an individual, so treat these as tendencies rather than promises.

Many hybrid layers lay around 250 to 300 eggs a year in their first years, while heritage breeds lay fewer but often for longer. Bantams (small chickens) suit small spaces and children, but larger hens may bully them.

Start with 3 to 6 hens, never just one, because chickens are flock animals and get lonely. Stay within any local limits.

Chicks or point-of-lay hens

You can start with day-old chicks or with point-of-lay hens (often called pullets in the US), which are young females close to laying age, usually 16 to 22 weeks old.

FactorChicksPoint-of-lay hens
Cost per birdLowerHigher
Extra kitBrooder and heat sourceNone beyond the coop
Time to first eggAround 18 to 22 weeksOften a few weeks
Risk of a roosterSome, even when sexedVery low

For most beginners who mainly want eggs, point-of-lay hens are the simpler start. Chicks are great fun and often grow up tame, but they need a brooder kept at about 95°F (35°C) at chick level in week one, reduced by about 5°F (3°C) each week, or a heat plate used as the maker directs. Feed chick starter first, then grower, then layer feed once laying begins.

Whatever you buy, buy from a reputable seller and keep new birds apart from any existing flock for about 30 days.

How much space chickens need in a coop and run

These are commonly cited minimums. More space means calmer hens and fewer squabbles.

  • Coop floor: about 3 to 4 sq ft (0.3 to 0.4 m²) per bird.
  • Run: about 8 to 10 sq ft (0.75 to 1 m²) per bird.
  • Perches: 8 to 12 inches (20 to 30 cm) per bird, set higher than the nest boxes so hens sleep on perches, not in the nests.
  • Nest boxes: one per 3 to 4 hens.
  • Ventilation: vents high in the coop, above the perches, so stale air and moisture escape without drafts on sleeping birds.
  • Bedding: dry wood shavings or straw, kept dry. Damp bedding causes smell and health problems.

Choose a well-drained, partly shaded spot that you can reach easily in bad weather. Many cheap flat-pack coops are flimsy and poorly ventilated, so check the build quality and the latches before you buy, or build your own if you have the tools.

How to keep chickens safe from predators

Foxes, raccoons, hawks, rats and dogs all want your hens. Most losses happen at night or because one small gap was left open.

  • Use hardware cloth (welded mesh) of about 1/2 inch (13 mm) wherever a predator can reach. Chicken wire keeps chickens in but does not keep predators out.
  • Stop diggers with a buried skirt or a mesh apron 12 to 24 inches (30 to 60 cm) wide laid flat around the run.
  • Cover the top of the run to stop birds of prey and climbers.
  • Fit two-step latches on every door, hatch and nest box lid. Raccoons can open simple hooks.
  • Take in or cover feed at night if rats are around.

Every evening, count the hens on the perches, check corners and nest boxes for a stray, latch every door and glance at the run for new holes or dig marks. If you use an automatic door, still check it with your own eyes. If you will be out after dark, ask a neighbor to close up or shut the hens in early.

What to feed backyard chickens

A complete layer feed of about 16 percent protein should be the main diet for laying hens. A full-size hen eats roughly 4 to 5 oz (110 to 140 g) a day, a bit more in cold weather. Most keepers fill a feeder and let the hens eat freely through the day.

  • Oyster shell: offer it in a separate dish so hens can take what they need for strong eggshells.
  • Grit: give insoluble grit if your hens get treats or cannot range much, since they need it to grind food.
  • Water: clean water every day, all day. In freezing weather, check it at least twice a day and keep a spare waterer to swap.
  • Treats: keep them under about 10 percent of the diet. Too many treats means fewer eggs.
  • Avoid: avocado, raw dry beans, chocolate, moldy food and salty food.

In the UK, it is illegal to feed kitchen scraps or catering waste to poultry, even vegetarian scraps from your own kitchen, because of disease risk. In the US, many states also restrict it, so check your state rules. Store feed in a lidded metal bin to keep rats and damp out.

Keep your flock healthy

Chickens hide illness, so a short daily look is your best early warning. Watch for a hen who is hunched, quiet or fluffed up, stops eating or drinking, has changed droppings, a swollen face or eyes, breathing noises, or a sudden drop in eggs.

  • Find a vet who treats poultry before you need one.
  • Check under the feathers every month for mites and lice, especially around the vent.
  • Clean droppings from the coop weekly and do a full deep clean about twice a year.
  • Give a dry dust bath area. Dust bathing is how hens keep their feathers and skin healthy.
  • Follow vet advice and label instructions on any medicine, including the egg withdrawal period when you must not eat the eggs.
  • Use dedicated boots for the coop, keep wild birds away from feed, and quarantine new birds for about 30 days.

Report suspected bird flu straight away. In the UK, this is a legal duty and you report to APHA. In the US, contact your state veterinarian or the USDA. Signs can include sudden deaths, swelling of the head, breathing trouble and a sharp drop in eating or laying.

Collecting and handling eggs safely

Hens lay most in their first year and during long spring and summer days, then less with age, heat, molting and winter. Shorter days mean fewer eggs, which is normal.

  1. Collect eggs at least once a day, twice in hot weather.
  2. Keep nest bedding fresh so eggs stay clean.
  3. Dry clean light dirt with a dry cloth or brush. If you must wash, use running water warmer than the egg and refrigerate washed eggs.
  4. Throw away cracked or badly soiled eggs.
  5. Refrigerated eggs keep about 3 to 5 weeks. Once chilled, keep them chilled.
Hygiene first. Chickens can carry Salmonella even when they look healthy. Wash your hands with soap and water after touching hens, eggs or anything in the coop, keep chickens out of the kitchen and do not kiss or snuggle them. Children under 5 should not handle poultry, and older children need supervision. Cook eggs until firm, and talk to your doctor if anyone in your home is pregnant, elderly or has a weakened immune system.

Frequently asked questions

How many chickens should a beginner get?

Most beginners do well with 3 to 6 hens. Chickens are flock animals and should never be kept alone, so three is a sensible minimum. Six hens of a good laying breed will usually give a family plenty of eggs in spring and summer. Check local limits first and size your coop and run for the number you plan, not fewer.

Do you need a rooster for hens to lay eggs?

No. Hens lay eggs without a rooster. You only need a rooster if you want fertile eggs to hatch chicks. Roosters are often banned in towns because of noise, and they can be rough with hens and protective around people. Most backyard keepers in suburban areas keep hens only, which also keeps neighbors much happier.

How much does it cost to start raising chickens?

Your biggest cost is a sturdy coop and secure run, which for 3 to 6 hens often runs to several hundred dollars or pounds, plus hardware cloth, a feeder and a waterer. After that, feed is the main monthly expense, with bedding, grit and oyster shell. Budget for vet visits too, and add about 20 percent for surprises.

At what age do chickens start laying eggs?

Most hens start laying at around 18 to 22 weeks old, depending on breed, season and daylight. Point-of-lay hens are sold at about 16 to 22 weeks and often start within a few weeks of arriving. Hens lay most in their first year or two, then gradually fewer, and they pause during molting and in winter.

Do chickens need a heat lamp in winter?

In most climates, adult hens do not need heat. They cope with cold well if the coop is dry, well ventilated and free of drafts at perch level. Heat lamps in coops are a serious fire risk because of dust, bedding and feathers. Focus on keeping water unfrozen, checking it at least twice a day in freezing weather.

Do backyard chickens smell?

A clean, dry coop smells very little. Smell comes from damp bedding and built-up droppings, so the fix is good ventilation, dry bedding, removing droppings weekly and keeping water from spilling into the bedding. A well-drained site helps too. If your coop smells strongly of ammonia, it needs cleaning and better airflow, which also protects your hens' breathing.

Before you buy any birds, take one step today: look up the rules for your address. In the US that means city or county zoning and any HOA papers; in the UK, your deeds, tenancy and APHA registration. Once you know how many hens you can keep, you can size the coop, choose your breeds and plan the rest with confidence.
