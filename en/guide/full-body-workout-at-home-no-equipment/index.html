Full Body Workout at Home, No Equipment: A Simple 15-Minute Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

No gym, no weights and only a small patch of floor is still plenty to get stronger and fitter. The short answer: warm up for about 6 minutes, then do a circuit of five bodyweight moves (a squat, a push-up, a glute bridge, a core move and a cardio move) for 40 seconds each with 20 seconds rest, repeat it 3 times for a 15-minute main block, cool down, and do this 2 or 3 times a week.

Below you will find the warm-up written out, step-by-step cues for each exercise, the beginner circuit, a quiet no-jump version for apartments (flats), how to make it harder over time, and a sample week. Everything works in a space about 6 by 6 feet (2 by 2 meters).

Can you get fit at home without equipment?

Yes. Your body weight is enough resistance for most people for many months, and you can keep making moves harder long after they feel easy. A good home routine covers four kinds of fitness at once: strength (squats, push-ups), cardio (marching, step jacks), mobility (warm-up moves and stretches) and balance (lunges and single-leg work).

Muscles adapt to a slightly harder challenge than they are used to, given rest in between. That is why consistency beats intensity. Three 15-minute sessions a week for three months will do far more for you than one brutal hour you never repeat.

Many national health services suggest adults aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening work on at least 2 days, and less sitting. A few short bodyweight sessions plus some walking gets you close. Realistic changes come over weeks: stairs feel easier, you sleep better, and your rep counts go up.

Talk to your doctor (GP) first if you have a heart or lung condition, high blood pressure or diabetes, are pregnant or recently gave birth, have joint problems, a recent injury or surgery, or have been inactive for a long time and are older. Stop and call your local emergency number (911, 999, 112 or 000) for chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting or an irregular racing heartbeat. Stop the exercise for sharp joint pain or dizziness.

Set up a safe space for home workouts

Clear an area about 6 by 6 feet (2 by 2 meters), roughly the size of a large rug. Check that you can swing your arms overhead and out to the sides without hitting anything.

  • Use a non-slip surface. A mat or carpet is ideal; never exercise in socks on a smooth floor.
  • Wear supportive shoes, or go barefoot on a non-slip mat.
  • Only use sturdy furniture. A chair for dips or squats must not have wheels and should be pushed against a wall.
  • Keep water nearby and open a window if the room gets stuffy.

Take a 2-minute fitness snapshot before your first session so you can see progress later: count wall push-ups in 30 seconds, count how many times you can stand up from a sturdy chair and sit back down in 30 seconds, and time a plank (on knees or toes) up to 60 seconds. Write the three numbers down and repeat the test after about 6 weeks.

A 6-minute warm-up before your workout

Flow from one move to the next with no rest, breathing steadily. A warm-up should leave you ready, not tired.

1 to 2Arm circles, then shoulder rolls30 + 30 seconds

3 to 4Leg swings, holding a wall, each side30 + 30 seconds
4 to 5Good mornings (hips back, flat back), then calf raises30 + 30 seconds

Afterward, cool down for about 5 minutes: a minute of slow marching, then gentle stretches held 20 to 30 seconds each for your calves, the front and back of your thighs, and your chest in a doorway, finishing in a kneeling child's pose or lying on your back hugging your knees.

The best bodyweight exercises for a full body workout

Five moves cover your whole body. Each has an easier and a harder version, so pick the one where the last few reps feel hard but your form stays clean.

Squat

Stand with feet about hip to shoulder width apart. Push your hips back and bend your knees as if sitting into a chair, chest up, knees following the line of your toes. Breathe in on the way down and out as you stand. Easier: sit down to a chair and stand up. Harder: take 3 seconds to lower.

Push-up

Hands slightly wider than shoulders, body in one straight line from head to heels (or knees). Lower your chest toward the surface, elbows angled back at about 45 degrees rather than flared out, then press away. Work down the ladder over the weeks: wall, kitchen counter (worktop), knees on the floor, then full push-up.

Glute bridge

Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Squeeze your buttocks and lift your hips until your body forms a line from shoulders to knees, pause, then lower slowly. Avoid arching your lower back at the top.

Bird dog or dead bug

For bird dog, kneel on all fours and slowly reach one arm forward and the opposite leg back, keeping your hips level, then switch. For dead bug, lie on your back with arms up and knees bent above your hips, and lower the opposite arm and leg while your lower back stays gently pressed to the floor.

Marching or step jacks

March briskly with high knees, or step one foot out to the side while raising your arms, then switch. These keep your heart rate up without jumping.

15-minute full body workout for beginners

Do the warm-up first. Then work through this circuit: 40 seconds of work, 20 seconds of rest, moving straight to the next exercise. That is 5 minutes per round. Do 3 rounds, resting about 60 seconds between rounds if you need it.

Aim for an effort of about 6 or 7 out of 10. On the talk test, you should be able to say a sentence between moves but not sing. Finish each set feeling you could do 2 or 3 more good reps. If you are puffing hard in round one, slow down; you want the last round to look like the first.

Short on time? Do the warm-up and just one round. Once you start, you will often finish more, and on the days you do not, you still kept the habit going.

A quiet no-jumping workout for apartments

Jumping is optional. A no-jump workout is kinder to knees, the pelvic floor and the neighbors below, and it still gets your heart working. This version suits flats, shared homes and sleeping children. Do 3 rounds after your warm-up.

For a split squat, take a long step forward, keep your weight between both feet and lower your back knee toward the floor, then rise. Keep your front knee in line with your toes. Land softly and train on a mat to cut down on noise.

How to make a bodyweight workout harder

Progress by changing one thing at a time, about once a week. Muscles adapt fairly quickly, but tendons and joints take longer, so stacking several changes at once is how knees and shoulders start to complain.

You are ready to progress an exercise when you can complete all your planned sets at the top of the rep range with clean form, at an effort of about 6 out of 10, two sessions in a row. Then choose one of these:

  1. Add a couple of reps (up to about 15 to 20, then switch tools).
  2. Add a round, for example going from 3 to 4.
  3. Move one rung up the ladder, such as counter push-up to knee push-up.
  4. Slow down: a 3-second lowering on squats, push-ups and split squats.
  5. Pause for 2 seconds at the hardest point, such as the bottom of a squat.
  6. Use one leg, such as a single-leg glute bridge.
  7. Hold longer: add 5 to 10 seconds a week to planks and side planks.
  8. Trim the rest slightly, only if your form holds.

Tempo and pauses are the most underused tools at home. Eight slow push-ups with a pause at the bottom can feel harder than twenty fast ones, and they make no noise at all.

How often should you do a full body workout?

Two or three full body sessions a week, with at least one rest or easy day between them, is a strong routine for most people. Your muscles get stronger during the recovery, not during the workout itself.

If that is more than you can manage now, two sessions a week that you actually complete is a strong start. Muscle soreness 1 to 3 days after a new workout is normal and should ease, not worsen. Frequently asked questions

Is a 15-minute workout enough?

For many beginners, yes. A focused 15-minute circuit two or three times a week, plus a warm-up, a cool-down and some walking, builds real strength and fitness over a few months. What matters most is doing it consistently and making it slightly harder as you improve. As your fitness grows you can move to 20 or 30-minute sessions if you enjoy them.

Can you build muscle with bodyweight exercises only?

Yes, especially as a beginner or returner. Muscles respond to a challenge that is hard for them, whether it comes from weights or your own body. Choose versions where 8 to 15 reps feel hard, and progress by moving to harder versions, slowing the lowering phase, adding pauses or using one leg at a time. Eating regular balanced meals with some protein supports recovery.

Should I do a full body workout every day?

Not hard strength sessions. Muscles need time between hard sessions to recover and adapt, so leave at least one rest or easy day between full body workouts. On the days in between, walking, gentle mobility or a short low-intensity movement break are good choices. Two or three full body sessions a week suits most people.

What is the best home workout for beginners with no equipment?

A simple circuit of five moves works well: a squat to a chair, a wall or counter push-up, a glute bridge, a bird dog and marching in place. Do each for 40 seconds with 20 seconds rest, for 3 rounds, after a short warm-up. It covers legs, upper body, core and a little cardio, and every move has an easier and harder version.

How can I work out at home without making noise?

Skip jumping and choose quiet moves that still raise your heart rate: step jacks instead of jumping jacks, brisk high-knee marching, split squats, slow push-ups and floor core work. Train on a mat or rug to absorb sound, land softly, and use slow lowering and pauses to make moves harder without speed. A quiet circuit can be just as effective.

How long until I see results from home workouts?

Many people notice changes within 2 to 4 weeks, such as feeling less puffed on the stairs or managing more reps. Visible changes in strength and shape usually take longer, often 8 to 12 weeks of regular training. Retesting simple measures, such as push-ups in 30 seconds or a plank hold, every 6 weeks shows progress more reliably than a mirror.

Today, clear a 6 by 6 foot space, write down your three starting numbers (wall push-ups, chair sit-to-stands and plank time), and do the 6-minute warm-up followed by one round of the beginner circuit. Next time, add a round. Small, steady steps like that are what make home workouts stick.
