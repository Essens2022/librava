Balance Exercises for Seniors: A Safe 10-Minute Routine at Home

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have started holding the banister a little tighter or feel wobbly turning around in the kitchen, you are not alone, and it is never too late to get steadier. The best balance exercises for seniors are short, frequent and done right next to a sturdy support: standing with your feet close together, standing on one leg, heel-to-toe walking and gentle weight shifts, paired with simple leg-strength moves like sit-to-stands and heel raises.

About 10 minutes a day on at least 3 days a week is a realistic start. This guide shows you how to check your starting point, how to do each exercise safely, how to progress and how to make your home safer at the same time.

Why balance gets harder with age

Your balance relies on three systems working together: your eyes, your inner ear, and the nerves in your feet and joints that sense where your body is. With age, each can become a little less sharp, and muscles in the legs and hips tend to weaken if they are not used. Reactions to a trip can slow too.

Fear of falling adds to the problem. Many people start avoiding stairs, walks or busy places, which means less practice and weaker muscles, which makes them feel even less steady. The good news is that balance and strength respond to practice at any age, and many people find they feel steadier within a few weeks of regular, gentle work.

Safety first before you start

This is general fitness information, not medical advice. Check with your doctor or physical therapist (UK: physiotherapist) before starting, especially if you have a heart condition, osteoporosis, a joint replacement, recent surgery, dizziness or a history of falls. Stop and seek medical help straight away for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, dizziness or sharp pain.
  • Always practice right next to a sturdy support, such as a kitchen counter or a heavy chair without wheels.
  • Clear the space of rugs, cords and clutter.
  • Wear supportive shoes, or go barefoot on a non-slip floor.
  • Breathe steadily and never hold your breath.
  • Mild effort is fine; sharp pain means stop.
  • Warm up first with a minute of seated or standing marching and some ankle circles.

If you have osteoporosis, avoid loaded forward bending and twisting to the end of your range, and ask a professional which moves suit you.

Check your balance in two minutes

A quick self-check gives you a starting point so you can see progress later.

Single-leg stance with support

  1. Stand facing your counter, fingertips resting on it, feet hip width apart.
  2. Shift your weight onto your right foot and lift your left foot an inch or two off the floor. Look at a fixed point at eye level.
  3. Time how long you can hold it, up to 30 seconds. Stop when your foot touches down or you grip the counter.
  4. Repeat on the other leg and write down both times.

30-second chair stand

Sit in a chair placed against a wall, arms crossed over your chest. Count how many times you can stand up fully and sit back down in 30 seconds, breathing out as you rise. If you need your hands, that is fine; just note "hands" so you test the same way next time.

One side is usually steadier than the other, which is normal. Retest every four weeks. Going from 7 stands to 10 in two months is a fine result at any age. If you cannot stand up once without your hands, mention it to your doctor or physical therapist.

The support ladder, how to progress safely

The easiest way to make a balance exercise harder or easier is to change how much you hold on. Think of it as a ladder:

  1. Both hands holding the counter firmly
  2. Both hands resting lightly
  3. One hand resting lightly
  4. Fingertips of one hand touching
  5. One hand hovering just above the counter, ready to grab it

When you can hold a position steadily for 30 seconds, or complete the reps with good control on two or three separate days, move up one rung. If you wobble a lot, step back down. Your hand stays near the counter at every stage. Some people spend weeks on one rung, and that is fine.

Standing balance exercises for seniors

Stand facing or side-on to your counter, knees soft rather than locked, eyes on a fixed spot at eye level.

Narrow and tandem stance

Start with feet together, side by side. When that is steady for 30 seconds, place the heel of one foot beside the arch of the other. Then progress to one foot directly in front of the other, heel touching toe. Hold each for 10 to 30 seconds, twice, and switch feet.

Single-leg stance

Lift one foot an inch or two and hold for 5 to 30 seconds, 2 or 3 times each leg. To make it easier, lift only your heel. Lower the foot at the first big wobble instead of fighting it.

Weight shifts

With both hands on the counter, rock slowly forward onto the balls of your feet, then back onto your heels, lifting toes slightly. Do 10 slow rocks, keeping your body upright like a tall tree.

Clock reaches

Hold the counter with one hand and stand on the leg nearest it. Imagine a clock on the floor. Tap your free foot lightly at 12, 3 and 6 o'clock, returning to center each time. Do 5 rounds, then turn around and repeat on the other side.

Heel-to-toe walk

With one hand on the counter, walk slowly along it placing each heel just in front of the other toes. Take 10 to 20 steps, stop, turn carefully, change hands and come back. Do 2 or 3 lengths.

A common mistake is staring at your feet. Keep your gaze at eye level, because looking down tips your posture forward and takes away information your eyes give your brain.

Leg strength moves that support balance

Stronger legs and better balance go together. Add these on 2 or more days a week.

Make sit-to-stands easier with your hands on the armrests or a higher seat, and harder by crossing your arms or lowering over a count of four. Many official guidelines suggest about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as walking, plus strength and balance work on 2 or more days, so check the guidance where you live and build up gradually.

Finish with three slow breaths and a minute sitting down. Do it on at least 3 days a week, when you feel fresh, and stop if you get tired. Short, frequent practice tends to work better than long, occasional sessions, so sprinkle it into your day: stand on one leg holding the counter while the kettle boils, or practice tandem stance at the sink while brushing your teeth.

Once the basics feel easy, add one new challenge at a time, such as slowly turning your head as if checking for traffic, and step down a rung on the ladder when you do.

Prevent falls at home and know what to do

  • Remove loose rugs and trailing cords; keep stairs clear.
  • Use brighter bulbs in hallways, stairs and the bathroom, plus night lights.
  • Fit grab rails by the toilet and in the shower or bath.
  • Wear well-fitting shoes with a firm heel and grippy soles, not loose slippers.
  • Have your eyes tested regularly and take care on stairs with bifocal or varifocal (progressive) glasses.
  • Ask your doctor or pharmacist for a medication review, as some medicines cause dizziness. Never stop a medicine without advice.

If you do fall, stay calm and lie still for a moment. Check for pain. If you feel able, roll onto your side, push up to hands and knees and crawl to a sturdy chair, then rise slowly. If you are hurt or cannot get up, call for help and keep warm. Keep your phone in your pocket, and consider a personal alarm and a daily check-in with a friend.

Call 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia or 111 in New Zealand if you hit your head, have severe pain, cannot move a limb, feel confused or cannot get up. If you take blood thinners and hit your head, get medical advice even if you feel fine. Tell your doctor about every fall.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best balance exercise for seniors?

There is no single best one, but standing on one leg with a hand on a kitchen counter is one of the most useful, because every step you take puts your weight on one leg. Combine it with tandem stance, heel-to-toe walking and leg-strength moves like sit-to-stands and heel raises. Short daily practice next to a sturdy support works well.

How long does it take to improve balance?

Many people notice they feel steadier within a few weeks of practicing on at least 3 days a week, though everyone is different. Track progress by retesting your single-leg stance and 30-second chair stand every four weeks. Look for everyday signs too, like feeling more confident stepping off a curb or turning around in the kitchen.

Can you improve balance at 70 or 80?

Yes. Muscles and balance respond to practice at any age. Start with the easiest versions, holding the counter with both hands, and move up gradually. Seated exercises such as marching, ankle circles and seated heel raises are a good starting point if standing is difficult. Check with your doctor or physical therapist first, especially if you have fallen before.

How often should seniors do balance exercises?

Aim for at least 3 days a week, and daily if you can. About 10 minutes is enough for a useful session. Many guidelines suggest strength and balance work on 2 or more days a week alongside about 150 minutes of moderate activity such as walking. Short sessions spread through the day, like while the kettle boils, also count.

What should I do if I feel dizzy when I stand up?

Sit back down straight away and rest. Stand up slowly in stages: sit on the edge of the chair or bed, move your feet and ankles, then rise while holding a support. Tell your doctor, because dizziness on standing can be linked to blood pressure or medicines. Stop exercising and get medical help if dizziness comes with chest pain or breathlessness.

Should I use a cane or walker for balance?

A walking aid is a tool, not a sign of giving up. A cane (walking stick), walking poles or a walker (rollator) can help you stay active and get out more. Ask a physical therapist to recommend the right type and set it to the correct height, because the wrong height can strain your back and make you less steady.

Start today with one thing: stand at your kitchen counter, rest your fingertips on it and time how long you can stand on each leg, up to 30 seconds.
