How to Become a Real Estate Agent: A Clear 7-Step Path for Beginners

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you are thinking about selling homes for a living, maybe after years in another job, you probably want a straight answer before you spend money on a course. To become a real estate agent you usually meet your local age and education rules, complete an approved pre-licensing course, pass a licensing exam and background check, then work under a licensed broker or agency while you build a client base.

The details change a lot depending on where you live, and the part nobody advertises is that pay is usually commission-based and slow to start. This guide walks through every step, from checking the rules to getting through your first year, with checklists you can use today.

How to become a real estate agent in 7 steps

Here is the whole path at a glance. Most people take it one step at a time over several months, and that is a perfectly sensible pace.

  1. Check the official rules with your local real estate regulator or licensing authority.
  2. Confirm you meet the age, education and residency requirements, and that nothing in your background needs early review.
  3. Complete an approved pre-licensing course or the required units of study.
  4. Pass the licensing exam or assessments.
  5. Complete any background check and fingerprinting, then apply for your license.
  6. Interview several brokerages (agencies) and join one that will sponsor and train you.
  7. Set up your business basics, find your first clients and plan your first year.

Before step one, it helps to be honest about what the job involves day to day, because that decides whether the rest is worth your time.

What real estate agents really do

Agents help people buy, sell or rent property, and they coordinate a long list of other people along the way: lenders, inspectors or surveyors, lawyers or conveyancers and the agent on the other side. Showing beautiful homes is a small slice of the week. Much of it goes on prospecting for clients, paperwork, follow-up calls and keeping deals on track.

Expect evenings and weekends, since that is when buyers are free. Expect rejection too. Sellers choose another agent, buyers go quiet, and a sale can fall through days before closing (completion, in the UK).

The truth about commission pay

Most agents are paid by commission, which means you are paid when a sale closes, not for the hours you put in. You might work with a buyer for months and earn nothing if they decide not to buy. Many new agents earn little or nothing in their first months, and plenty leave within a year or two.

That is why a savings cushion covering several months of living costs matters so much before you start. Some people keep part-time work while they build up, if their brokerage and local rules allow it.

Skills that matter more than a sales background

  • Self-discipline, because nobody makes you call people on a quiet Tuesday.
  • Resilience, so a lost deal stings for an evening, not a month.
  • Organization, for deadlines, documents and follow-ups.
  • Comfort with basic math and simple technology.
  • Honesty under pressure, which protects your clients and your license.

Real estate license requirements by country

Licensing is local, and the rules change, so treat this table as a starting map and always confirm the current requirements with your official regulator.

WhereWho licensesTypical route
United StatesEach stateApproved course, state exam with national and state parts, background check, sponsoring broker
United KingdomNo general licence in most of the UKLegal duties instead: redress scheme, anti-money laundering supervision, consumer rules
CanadaEach provinceCourses from a designated provider, exams, registration with a brokerage
AustraliaEach state or territoryShort entry-level units, registration, work under a licensed agent
New ZealandNational authorityRecognized qualification, work under a licensed agent

In the US, required course hours vary widely between states, some states add post-licensing training, and you renew through continuing education. In the UK, letting agents have extra rules, including client money protection, and in Scotland letting agents must be registered and qualified. Voluntary professional qualifications are valued by many UK employers.

Answer every background question on your application truthfully. If something in your past worries you, ask your regulator whether it affects eligibility before you pay for a course. This guide is general career information, not legal or financial advice.

Costs to plan for

  • The pre-licensing course or study units, plus study materials.
  • Exam fees and possible retake fees.
  • Background check, fingerprinting and license application fees.
  • Association or membership dues where relevant, once you start.

How to pass the real estate exam

Most licensing exams are multiple choice, and in the US they usually have a national section and a state-specific section. Start by downloading your regulator's current exam outline. It tells you exactly which topics are tested.

The topics that come up again and again

  • Agency relationships: who you represent and what duties you owe them.
  • Contract basics: offers, acceptance, conditions and deadlines.
  • Property law: ownership types, easements and title.
  • Finance basics: how loans and mortgages work in general terms.
  • Fair housing and equality law.
  • Real estate math: area, percentages and prorations.

A study plan that fits around real life

Short, regular sessions beat weekend cramming for most adults. Thirty to sixty minutes a day, five days a week, works well. Spend the first weeks learning the material, start practice questions early, and keep a list of every question you get wrong with the reason why.

In the final two weeks, sit full timed practice tests. For the math, practice until you can split an odd-shaped room into rectangles and triangles, and divide a yearly cost by days to work out each party's share up to the closing date.

A few memory tricks help: teach a topic out loud to a friend, draw a timeline from offer to closing, and review yesterday's notes for five minutes before starting today's. If you need to retake the exam, that is common and says nothing about the agent you will become.

How to choose the right brokerage

Your first brokerage (agency, in the UK) shapes your training, your costs and your habits, so interview several. Options include large offices with formal training, small independent offices, teams led by an established agent and online brokerages with fewer office costs. In the UK, many high street agencies employ new negotiators on a base salary plus commission or bonus.

Questions to ask in every interview

  • Would I be an employee or an independent contractor?
  • What are all the fees and splits, and what would I owe in a month when I close nothing?
  • What does training for new agents cover, and will I have a named mentor?
  • Who reviews my contracts before they go out?
  • Can I speak with two agents who joined in the past year?
  • What is your safety policy for showings and open homes?

Red flags

  • Pressure to sign before you have read the agreement.
  • Vague answers about fees, or refusal to put them in writing.
  • Promises of big earnings in your first year.
  • Encouragement to cut corners on disclosures or advertising.
  • Requests for large upfront payments for leads or programs.

Training, mentoring and an ethical culture often matter more than the size of the commission split, especially in year one.

Set up your business before your first client

Many agents are independent contractors or self-employed, which means you are running a small business with its own costs, taxes and records. A few early habits save a lot of stress later.

  • Meet an accountant and register as self-employed or as a business if your country requires it.
  • Open a separate business account and a tax savings account.
  • Move money for tax into that account the day any commission arrives.
  • Check whether you need professional indemnity or errors and omissions insurance, and whether your car insurance covers business use.
  • Keep every receipt and log business mileage with dates and purposes.
  • Store client documents securely, with strong passwords and two-step verification.

Delay non-essential spending, such as paid advertising or new equipment, until your business has income. Because pay comes in waves, many agents pay themselves a steady monthly amount from their cushion rather than living sale to sale.

How new real estate agents find their first clients

Your first clients usually come from people who already know you, or from their referrals. Make a list of everyone you know: friends, family, former colleagues, neighbors, parents from school, people from clubs or your place of worship. Then tell them personally, without pressure, that you are now helping people buy and sell homes.

A short message works well: "I have just started as a licensed agent with a local office. No need to do anything, but if you or anyone you know ever has questions about moving, I would be glad to help."

Other ways to meet people

  • Host open homes (open houses) for experienced agents in your office.
  • Pick one neighborhood and get to know it well over time.
  • Join community events and local groups for real, not just to hand out cards.
  • Post useful local information online rather than constant sales messages.

Record every conversation in a simple contact database or spreadsheet the same day, and set a follow-up date. Follow do-not-call or telephone preference rules, anti-spam rules for emails and texts, and privacy law wherever you work.

Your first year as a new agent

A first year often moves through four rough phases: foundation in months one and two, steady activity in months three to five, building momentum in months six to nine and an honest review in months ten to twelve.

Daily and weekly habits

  • A set block each morning for calls, personal messages or notes.
  • Add every new contact to your database the same day.
  • Check transaction deadlines daily.
  • Preview several homes for sale each week so you know your market.
  • Host or attend at least one open home where possible.
  • Take at least one full day off each week.

Track a few simple numbers: people contacted, real conversations about property, appointments set and follow-ups done on time. They show you what is working long before sales do.

Agent safety matters from day one. Meet new clients at the office first and verify their identity, share your schedule with a colleague, keep your phone charged, park where you cannot be blocked in and leave any appointment that feels wrong.

Ethics belongs in every week too. Treat every client equally, never steer buyers toward or away from neighborhoods, describe properties accurately, keep deposits only in proper trust or client accounts and refer legal, tax and lending questions to the right professionals. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to become a real estate agent?

It depends on where you live. In many US states, people finish the course, pass the exam and get licensed within a few months, but required course hours vary widely. Australia often has a short entry-level route, while Canada and New Zealand involve longer courses. Background checks and application processing add weeks. After that, building a steady client base usually takes much longer, often well into the first year.

Do you need a degree to be a real estate agent?

In most places you do not need a university degree. Typical requirements are a minimum age, a certain level of schooling, an approved course and a passed exam or assessment. Some regions add residency or background requirements. Check your official regulator for the exact rules, since education minimums differ between states, provinces and countries and can change.

Can I be a real estate agent part time?

Many people start part time while keeping another job, which can ease the pressure of irregular commission pay. Ask each brokerage whether it accepts part-time agents, because some do not. Be realistic: buyers and sellers expect quick replies, and evenings and weekends are busy, so you need enough flexible time to serve clients properly and keep your follow-up going.

Is the real estate exam hard?

Many people find it challenging because it tests legal terms, contracts, agency duties and math in a short time. It is very manageable with steady study. Use your regulator's content outline, practice questions from the first week, review every wrong answer and finish with timed practice tests. If you do not pass the first time, retaking is common and allowed in most places.

Do estate agents need a licence in the UK?

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland there is no general licence to practise as an estate agent. Agents must still belong to an approved redress scheme, register for anti-money laundering supervision and follow consumer protection rules, including giving buyers material information. Letting agents face extra rules, and in Scotland they must be registered and qualified. Rules are evolving, so check current official guidance.

Do new real estate agents get paid a salary?

Usually not. Most agents in the US, Canada, Australia and New Zealand work on commission as independent contractors, so income depends on closed sales and can be irregular. Some UK high street agencies employ new negotiators with a base salary plus commission or bonus. Ask each brokerage how you would be paid, and plan for several lean months.

Your first step today is simple: find your official real estate regulator, read the current licensing requirements for where you live and write them on one page with the costs and timeline. Once you know the real rules, every other decision, from courses to brokerages, gets much easier.
