How to Use AI at Work: A Plain-English Guide to Getting Hours Back

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you keep hearing about AI at work but your one or two tries gave you bland, generic answers, you are in very good company. The short answer: use an AI chat assistant for first drafts, summaries, rewrites and planning, give it clear context every time, keep private data out of it, and check every fact before anything leaves your desk.

You do not need to be technical to do any of this. This guide walks you through what these tools are good and bad at, how to ask so you get useful answers, the easiest tasks to start with, and the safety habits that keep you out of trouble.

What AI is actually good at in an office job

An AI chat assistant predicts what a useful reply looks like, based on patterns learned from a huge amount of text. That makes it very good at producing words and very unreliable at producing facts it cannot see. Picture an eager, fast intern on their first day: well read, never tired, but new to your company and inclined to guess rather than admit it does not know.

Here is a quick way to decide whether to reach for it.

Good atNot good at
First drafts of emails, memos and reportsExact facts, prices, dates and laws you have not checked
Summarizing long threads and documentsMath you will rely on without redoing it
Rewriting for tone or a different readerYour private context, such as what your manager promised
Brainstorming ideas and optionsJudgment calls about hiring, health, money or law
Turning messy notes into lists, tables and plansTelling you honestly that your plan is weak (unless asked)

A good first exercise is to list the tasks you do in a typical week and ask the assistant which ones it could help draft, summarize or structure, and what you would still need to check. Start with the tasks that eat the most time and need the least private information.

How to write AI prompts that get useful answers

A prompt is simply what you type to the assistant. Most strong prompts contain five parts. You will not need all five every time, but when an answer disappoints you, one of them is usually missing.

  1. Role: who it should act as, such as an experienced office manager.
  2. Task: the exact job, such as draft a reply that accepts the meeting but moves it to Thursday.
  3. Context: who the reader is, what has already happened and what you want to achieve.
  4. Format: length and shape, such as under 120 words with a subject line.
  5. Tone: friendly, firm, formal or plain, plus anything to avoid, like corporate jargon.

Context matters most. The assistant knows nothing about your situation except what you type, so two or three sentences of background often make the difference between a stiff template and a draft you can send with small edits.

Compare these two. A weak prompt: Write a follow-up email to a client. A strong one: You are an experienced account manager. Write a follow-up email to a client I met on Tuesday about a printing contract. She liked the proposal but worried about delivery times. Confirm we can deliver in 5 working days and suggest a short call next week. Under 120 words, warm and confident, not pushy.

Three tricks that fix most bad answers

  • Ask it to interview you first. Say: before you write anything, ask me up to 5 short questions, one at a time. Great for job ads, plans and tricky letters.
  • Show an example. Paste a past email or update you liked (with private details removed) and ask for a new one in the same style.
  • Treat the first answer as a draft. Reply with short notes like make it shorter, less apologetic, or simpler words for a non-specialist.

Adjectives such as amazing, engaging and professional do very little. Facts about the situation do a lot.

Easy ways to use AI for emails, meetings and reports

Email is the best place to start, because the tasks are short, frequent and easy to check. Then move on to meetings and documents.

Email

  • Paste a few rough bullet points and ask for a reply under 100 words in a friendly, clear tone.
  • Paste your own draft and ask for a tone check: how might a busy reader feel reading this, and what would you soften?
  • Ask for a polite no that offers one alternative, or a gentle follow-up that does not sound like nagging.
  • Paste a long thread (if you are allowed to share it) and ask for the decisions made, open questions and who is waiting on whom.

Meetings

  • Give the goal and the time available, and ask for an agenda with a time per item and the decision needed for each.
  • Paste your messy notes and ask for a summary with three headings: decisions, action items (owner, task, deadline) and open questions.
  • Ask for a follow-up email to attendees, sent while it is still fresh.

Reports and presentations

  • Ask for an outline first, then draft one section at a time from your notes.
  • Ask for a one-paragraph executive summary for a senior reader with no time.
  • Ask it to read your draft as a tough reviewer and list the weakest points, without rewriting.

Use AI to plan your week and your projects

AI makes a surprisingly good thinking partner when your to-do list has turned into a wall of guilt. Paste the list (names removed), say how many focused hours you really have and which meetings are fixed, and ask for a realistic order of work with the two things to do first.

A few planning tasks it handles well:

  • Breaking a big, vague project into steps, each small enough to start in under an hour.
  • Working back from a deadline to a timeline, with a buffer for the things that always slip.
  • Listing what could go wrong with a plan, how likely each risk is and one way to reduce it.
  • Turning a process you repeat into a checklist a new colleague could follow.
  • Laying out pros and cons for a decision, then arguing against the option you prefer.

Assistants tend to agree with the framing you give them, so ask what could go wrong rather than asking if your plan is good. The decision itself stays with you.

Is it safe to use AI at work with private information

When you type into a chat assistant, your words usually go to the company that runs the tool. Depending on the product, your account and your settings, they may be stored or used to improve the tool. So treat the chat box like an email to an outside company.

Unless your employer has clearly approved the tool and the settings for that kind of information, keep these out:

  • Names, addresses, phone numbers and dates of birth of real people
  • Customer, client, patient or student records
  • Employee details such as pay, health, reviews or discipline
  • Passwords, account numbers and access codes (never, under any approval)
  • Unreleased financial results, contracts, deals and confidential plans
  • Documents that belong to someone else

Most tasks do not need the private details at all. Instead of naming the customer and pasting their account number, write: a customer whose refund is two weeks late is frustrated and has threatened to complain publicly. Use placeholders like [customer name], then add the real details yourself in your own email. Watch for indirect clues too, such as the only night shift engineer in a small office.

Check your policy first. Find your organization's AI policy (ask your manager or IT if you cannot), use the approved tool and account, and look at your tool's history and training settings. Health, legal, finance and education work often has extra rules, so ask your compliance or IT team before using any real case details.

How to check AI answers before you use them

Assistants can sound completely confident and still be wrong. They sometimes invent statistics, quotes, sources and even organizations, a problem usually called hallucination. It is a normal side effect of how the tools work, not a rare glitch, and wrong answers read just as smoothly as right ones.

Slow down when you see precise numbers with no clear source, studies or experts you have never heard of, quotes from real people, links you have not opened, or legal, medical and tax details stated as simple fact. Asking the assistant whether it is sure is not checking. Checking means a source outside the conversation.

For anything that matters, run this routine:

StepWhat you do
1. Read it allRead the whole output slowly, not just the top
2. Find the factsMark every number, name, date and claim
3. VerifyConfirm key facts in an official or trusted source
4. Redo the mathRecalculate totals and percentages in a spreadsheet
5. Check the contextCompare with what you know about the situation
6. Own itEdit until you would happily sign it

A handy prompt after any factual answer: List every specific claim in your answer in a table, with how confident you are and what kind of source I should use to verify it. Do not add new facts. It turns a vague worry into a short to-do list.

Build a daily 10-minute AI habit

The most common way people lose the benefit is simple: they have one good week, then forget what worked and stare at the empty box. A small routine fixes that.

  1. Minutes 1 to 3: paste your to-do list (private details removed) and ask for a realistic order of work.
  2. Minutes 4 to 7: get a first draft of the one email or section you have been avoiding.
  3. Minutes 8 to 10: set up something for later, such as an agenda or tomorrow's report outline.

Keep a prompt library, which is just a document of prompts that worked, each with a short name and [square brackets] for the parts that change. When a conversation goes well, ask the assistant to rewrite your instructions as one reusable prompt with placeholders. Store templates only, never versions with real names or figures.

Most assistants can now save standing instructions (sometimes called custom instructions or project notes), so you stop repeating yourself. Keep them general, for example: I work in HR at a mid-sized company, use US spelling, keep answers short, do not open with praise. Check what your tool and plan offer.

Will AI take my job and what to do now

Many tasks will change, and some routine drafting will take far less time. Jobs, though, are bundles of tasks, and people who learn to use these tools well tend to become more useful, not less. Think in tasks, not job titles: sort your weekly work into what AI can draft, what it can help with, and what needs you.

The skills that grow in value are the ones the assistant lacks: judgment, relationships, knowledge of your field and your customers, and the ability to ask good questions. Keep your own skills sharp by writing important first drafts yourself now and then, and asking the assistant to critique rather than rewrite.

Know when to leave it closed, too. A condolence note or an apology for your own mistake should come from you.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to use AI at work?

It can be, if you use the tool your employer approves and keep confidential information out. Do not paste names, client or patient records, employee details, passwords or unreleased financial data unless your organization has approved the tool and settings for that. Remove identifying details, check your history and training settings, and read your company's AI policy. If there is no policy, ask your manager or IT before using any work information.

Do I have to tell my boss I use AI?

It depends on your workplace policy, so check it first. Some organizations ask you to say when AI helped write something, others only limit which tools and information you can use. Even where it is not required, a short message asking which tool is approved and how to use it safely is a smart move. Most managers would rather answer a question than clean up a mistake.

Can AI write my work emails for me?

Yes, for the first draft. Give it a few bullet points, the reader, what you want to happen and the tone, and ask for a short reply. Then read it closely, fix any facts and add real names yourself. Keep truly personal messages, such as apologies or condolences, in your own words. You stay responsible for everything you send, whoever wrote the first version.

How do I stop AI from making things up?

You cannot stop it completely, but you can reduce and catch it. Ask the assistant to mark anything it is unsure about, to say it does not know instead of guessing, and to avoid inventing statistics, quotes or sources. Then verify every important fact in a trusted source outside the chat, open any links yourself, and redo key numbers in a spreadsheet.

What is the best AI tool for work?

The best one is usually the tool your employer has approved, because it comes with settings and terms your organization has checked. The well-known general chat assistants all handle drafting, summarizing and rewriting well. Features such as reading uploaded files, voice conversations and saved instructions vary by tool and plan and change often, so check what yours offers rather than chasing the newest option.

Your first step today: pick one small task you do every week, such as a routine email or meeting summary, write a five-part prompt for it with private details removed, use it twice, then save the version that worked.
