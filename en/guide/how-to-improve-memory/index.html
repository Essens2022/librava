How to Improve Memory: Simple Habits and Tricks That Work at Any Age

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you keep walking into rooms and wondering why, or a name slips away just as you need it, you are in very good company. To improve your memory, pay attention on purpose when something matters, use a few simple memory techniques such as chunking and vivid mental pictures, and support your brain with regular movement, good sleep, company and new learning.

Most everyday slips are normal, especially after 50, and many of them are really lapses of attention rather than lost memories. This guide shows you the techniques that work, the daily aids that take the load off, the habits that keep your brain in good shape, and the signs that mean it is time to talk to a doctor.

Why memory slips happen as you age

Memory is not one thing. You have a short-term working memory for what you are juggling right now, a long-term store for facts and life events, a skills memory for things like riding a bike, and a memory for future tasks, such as taking a tablet at lunchtime. These systems age differently.

What often slows downWhat stays strong or grows
Processing speedVocabulary and general knowledge
MultitaskingPractical skills and routines
Finding names and words quicklyJudgment and life experience
Learning new technologyThe ability to keep learning

Tiredness, stress, a cold, a few drinks or a bad night can make these normal changes much more noticeable for a day or two. So judge your memory on an ordinary day, not your worst one. The good news is that your brain keeps adapting and forming new connections at any age, which is why the steps below can help.

Start with attention, the easiest memory boost

Many forgotten moments are really things that never went in. You put the keys down while thinking about dinner, so your brain never stored where they went. The fix is to pay attention on purpose when something matters.

  • Stop for a few seconds and look at what you are doing.
  • Say it out loud: "Passport, top drawer of the desk." Hearing it gives your brain a second route in.
  • Turn off the radio or put down the other task when someone gives you directions.
  • Ask people to slow down or repeat instructions. Nobody minds.

Try this the next time you park the car or put away something important. Pause, name the place out loud, and notice one detail about it. You will be surprised how often that is all it takes.

Memory techniques that really work

People with remarkable memories mostly use methods anyone can learn. Try each of these and keep the ones that suit the way you think.

Chunking

Your working memory holds only a few separate items at once, so group them. A long phone number is easier as three chunks than eleven digits. A shopping list of eight items becomes three groups: dairy, fruit and bakery, with a count for each.

Pictures and links

Your brain remembers vivid, silly pictures far better than plain words. To buy eggs, stamps and batteries, picture a giant egg wearing a coat of stamps and leaning on a battery like a walking stick. You can also link new facts to old ones: parking on level 3, row G becomes "George and his 3 dogs."

The memory route

This ancient method uses a place you know well. Pick ten stops through your home, such as front door, hall table, coat hooks and kitchen sink. Place each item on your list at a stop with a strange, colorful image, then walk the route in your mind to recall them. Once your route is fixed, you can reuse it for every new list.

Spaced repetition

Review new information after ten minutes, then the next day, then after three days, then a week. The same 20 minutes spread over a week beats 20 minutes of cramming in one evening. Index cards with a question on the front and the answer on the back work well.

How to remember names and numbers

Names are hard because they are arbitrary. Nothing about a face tells you the person is called Susan. These steps help:

  1. Listen to the name instead of planning your own greeting.
  2. Repeat it straight away: "Lovely to meet you, Susan."
  3. Use it once or twice in the conversation.
  4. Link it to an image or someone you know: "Susan with the sunhat."
  5. Jot it down afterward with where you met.

If you missed it, ask again early on. It is far less awkward than waiting. For numbers, look for meaning: 1945 is the end of the war, 0712 might be a sister's birthday on July 12. Rhyming pictures also help, such as one is a bun, two is a shoe, three is a tree and four is a door, so 342 becomes a tree growing through a door and kicking off a shoe. Keep PINs and passwords in your head this way rather than on paper.

Everyday aids that take the pressure off

Using aids is not cheating or giving up. Pilots use checklists. Aids handle the daily admin so your brain is free for the things that matter, and they are best at stopping the most annoying slips, like missed appointments and tablets.

  • A launch pad by the door. One bowl, shelf or basket for keys, glasses, phone and wallet or purse. Everything goes there the moment you come in.
  • One calendar, not five. Check it at breakfast and look at the week ahead every Sunday.
  • One running to-do list and a kitchen notepad for shopping, sorted by area of the shop.
  • A weekly pill organizer, filled at the same time each week and linked to a daily habit like breakfast.
  • Recurring phone alarms with clear labels, such as "Evening tablets."
  • Simple trays or folders for bills, letters and forms to deal with.

If you miss a dose or cannot recall taking a medicine, do not take a double dose. Check the leaflet or ask your pharmacist. If you often forget medicines, tell your doctor or pharmacist, who can suggest simpler routines or packaging.

Daily habits that support your brain

No single habit protects your memory, but research suggests a number of things may lower the risk of memory problems or help keep the brain healthy. They are good for the whole body too.

HabitA realistic target
Move moreBuild up to about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus strength and balance work on 2 days
SleepAbout 7 to 9 hours, with a regular wake-up time
Eat wellPlenty of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, nuts, fish and olive oil
Stay connectedRegular contact with friends, groups or volunteering
Keep learningA language, an instrument, a craft or a discussion group
Check your sensesHearing tests and eye tests, and hearing aids if advised
Protect your heartKnow your blood pressure, do not smoke, keep alcohol low

Heart health is brain health, because your brain depends on a steady blood supply. Low mood and stress also cloud memory and concentration, and both are treatable. Start where it is easiest for you, such as a 10 minute walk with a friend, which counts for movement and company at once.

This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor before starting a new exercise plan if you have a health condition or have been inactive, and ask your doctor or pharmacist before making big changes to your diet or medicines. Never stop a prescribed medicine on your own.

Do brain games and supplements help?

Brain-training games tend to make you better at the game itself, but there is limited evidence that the benefit carries over to everyday memory. Real-world learning with a bit of challenge, time with people and physical activity are better bets. Puzzles are fine if you enjoy them, just mix in something new now and then.

There is no good evidence that any vitamin, herbal or "brain" supplement prevents memory decline in healthy people. The exception is a genuine shortage: low vitamin B12 can cause memory problems, and a simple blood test from your doctor will show it. Do not self-prescribe; ask first.

Normal forgetfulness or something to check?

Occasional slips are part of ordinary aging. What matters is a pattern of changes that are new, frequent, getting worse or affecting daily life.

Usually normalWorth seeing a doctor
Forgetting a name, then recalling it laterRepeating the same questions often
Misplacing glasses now and thenGetting lost in familiar places
Needing a moment to find a wordOften losing words or the thread of a conversation
Occasionally missing a billTrouble with money, medicines or familiar tasks
Slower to learn new techChanges in personality, mood or judgment

Many causes of memory problems are treatable, including medicine side effects, low B12, thyroid problems, depression, stress, sleep problems, hearing loss, infections and alcohol. Take notes, a list of your medicines and a companion to the appointment.

Get urgent help for sudden confusion that comes on over hours or days (often an infection, dehydration or a medicine reaction): see a doctor the same day. For stroke signs such as a drooping face, arm weakness, slurred speech, sudden severe headache or sudden vision loss, call 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia or 111 in New Zealand straight away.

Frequently asked questions

Can you really improve your memory after 50?

Yes. Your brain keeps forming new connections at any age, and memory is as much about technique as talent. Paying attention on purpose, chunking, vivid pictures and spaced review all work for older adults. Habits like regular walking, good sleep, staying social and learning something new also support the brain, though no habit can promise to prevent memory loss.

Why do I forget why I walked into a room?

It is a very common slip and usually a sign of divided attention, not a failing memory. You set the goal while thinking about something else, so it never fully registered. Saying the task out loud as you set off ("getting the scissors") often stops it happening. If you do forget, going back to where you had the thought usually brings it back.

What is the fastest way to improve short-term memory?

Reduce the load on it. Do one thing at a time, turn off background noise and group information into chunks, such as splitting a phone number into three parts. Repeat new information out loud and try to recall it a few minutes later. Writing things on a single list or calendar frees working memory for whatever you are doing right now.

What foods are good for memory?

No single food boosts memory, but a Mediterranean-style pattern is linked with good brain and heart health. That means plenty of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, nuts, fish and olive oil, with fewer processed foods. Drink enough water through the day, and keep alcohol within low-risk guidance or less. Check with your doctor before big diet changes if you have a health condition.

Is it normal to forget names as you get older?

Yes. Taking longer to recall names and words is one of the most common changes with age, and the name often pops up later. It is more of a concern if you forget names often and never recall them, or if it comes with other changes like getting lost or repeating questions. In that case, see a doctor to check for treatable causes.

When should I worry about memory loss?

See a doctor if memory problems are getting worse, affect daily life, or others have noticed changes. Warning signs include repeating questions, getting lost in familiar places, trouble with bills or medicines and changes in mood or judgment. Seeing someone early helps, because many causes are treatable. Sudden confusion needs same-day medical help, and stroke signs need an emergency call.

Pick one step today: set up a launch pad by your front door for keys, glasses and phone, and next time you put something important down, pause and say where it is out loud. When that feels easy, add a daily walk and build your first memory route.
