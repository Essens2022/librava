How to Study Effectively: 7 Proven Techniques That Actually Work

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you study for hours, highlight half the textbook and still go blank in the exam, the problem is usually the method, not your brain. To study effectively, test yourself instead of rereading, spread short reviews over days and weeks, mix different types of problems, explain ideas in your own words, and protect your focus and sleep.

This guide explains each of those techniques in plain words, with exact steps, a review schedule, a sample study week and an exam countdown. It works for high school and college (university) students and for adults studying for a certification or professional exam.

Why rereading and highlighting do not work well

Rereading feels productive because the page looks familiar. But recognizing something when you see it is not the same as being able to recall it on a blank exam paper. Highlighting has the same problem. It is mostly decoration unless you do something active with what you marked.

Forgetting is also normal and predictable. Most new information fades fast in the first few days unless you revisit it. Cramming can get you through tomorrow's quiz, but much of it is gone a week later.

The good news is that a little effort in the right place goes a long way. Learning that feels slightly hard, like struggling to recall an answer, tends to stick much better than learning that feels smooth. Researchers sometimes call this a desirable difficulty.

You may have heard that you should study in your learning style, such as visual or auditory. Research does not support matching teaching to a preferred style. Everyone benefits from combining words and pictures, and from the methods below.

Technique 1, active recall instead of rereading

Active recall (also called retrieval practice) means pulling information out of your memory rather than putting it in again. Every time you successfully remember something, the memory gets stronger.

Blank page recall

  1. Write a topic at the top of a blank page.
  2. Close your notes, book and every tab.
  3. Write everything you remember for 8 to 10 minutes: terms, steps, examples, links between ideas.
  4. When you run dry, keep going for one more minute.
  5. Open your notes and fill gaps in a different color.

The gaps you fill in are your most valuable study material. They show you exactly what to review next.

Other ways to practice recall include past papers, end-of-chapter questions, writing your own questions in the margin of your notes and flashcards. Make good cards by putting one idea per card, phrasing the front as a question and always trying to answer before you flip. Be strict: a vague or slow answer counts as wrong.

Technique 2, spaced practice with a simple schedule

Spacing means spreading your study of a topic over several short sessions across days and weeks instead of one long session. Each review catches the memory just as it starts to fade, so it lasts longer every time.

ReviewWhenWhat to doTime
FirstNext dayBlank page recall10 to 15 minutes
Second2 to 3 days laterFlashcards or questions10 minutes
ThirdAbout a week laterPractice questions10 to 15 minutes
FourthA few weeks laterMixed questions10 minutes

If a review felt easy, leave a longer gap next time. If it was a struggle, bring the next one closer. The first review, the day after you learn something, is the one most worth protecting.

The flashcard box system

Divide a shoebox into four sections. New cards and any card you get wrong go in Level 1, reviewed daily. Correct answers move up one level: Level 2 every 2 to 3 days, Level 3 weekly, Level 4 every 2 to 3 weeks. The box does the spacing for you, so time goes to the cards you struggle with.

Technique 3, mix your practice problems

Most textbooks give you 15 problems of the same type in a row. That feels smooth, but in the exam questions arrive in no particular order, and the hard part is recognizing which method each one needs. Mixing problem types, often called interleaving, trains exactly that skill.

  • Learn each new method on its own first, using worked examples.
  • Once you can solve one without peeking, add it to the mix.
  • Build sets with a few problems from this week, a few from last week and one or two from weeks ago.
  • Before solving, say or write what type of problem it is and why.

Mixing also shows you which types you confuse with each other, so your next session can target exactly those pairs. The same idea works beyond math: mix vocabulary topics in a language, or periods and themes in history.

Technique 4, ask why and explain it simply

Facts stick better when they are connected to other things you know. As you study, keep asking why and how. Why does this happen? How does it connect to last week's topic? What would happen if one part changed?

Then explain it to an imaginary beginner, out loud or on paper, using plain words and a concrete example. Wherever you stumble or reach for jargon, you have found a gap. Go back, fix it and explain again.

Combine words with visuals too. A quick diagram, timeline or flowchart drawn from memory gives your brain two ways to find the information later. For beginners in a subject, studying a fully worked example step by step before trying problems alone saves a lot of frustration.

Technique 5, focus without your phone

Switching between your notes and your phone has a hidden cost. Each time you glance at a message, it takes a while to get your full attention back, so an hour of interrupted study can be worth far less than it looks.

  • Put your phone in another room or at least out of sight.
  • Turn off notifications on your computer and close unneeded tabs.
  • Study in the same tidy spot when you can, so it becomes a cue to focus.
  • Work on one task at a time.

Use timed focus sessions. Set a timer for 25 to 50 minutes, work on one task only, then take a 5 to 10 minute break away from screens: stretch, get water or look out of the window. After three or four sessions, take a longer break. If a thought pops up mid-session, write it down and return to the task.

Technique 6, beat procrastination and plan your week

Procrastination is usually about avoiding an unpleasant feeling, like boredom, confusion or fear of doing badly, not about laziness. The fix is to make starting smaller.

Use the two-minute start: commit to only opening the book and writing the first heading, or doing one flashcard. Starting is the hardest part, and you often keep going. Break big tasks into tiny steps, and decide in advance when and where you will do them, such as "Tuesday at 4, library, biology questions."

Then plan your week in a 30-minute session each Sunday. Choose priorities before hours, and give each day one main block plus one short review.

Technique 7, sleep and a calm exam countdown

Sleep is part of studying. Your brain consolidates what you learned while you sleep, so an all-nighter usually costs more marks than it gains. Aim for regular sleep, move your body most days, drink water and eat regular meals for steady energy.

WhenMain jobKey actions
4 weeks outCover and planLearn the exam format, list topics, rate them green, amber or red
2 to 3 weeks outPractice and fixPast papers, a mistakes log, weak topics
1 week outRehearseTimed papers under exam conditions, quick daily reviews
Day beforeLight reviewBrief recall, pack your bag, normal bedtime
Exam dayPerformBreakfast, arrive calmly, read every question carefully

Exam nerves are normal. Slow breathing with a longer out-breath and knowing you have practiced under real conditions both help.

If anxiety, low mood or trouble concentrating is seriously affecting your life or studies, talk to a doctor, a counselor or your school's student support service. Avoid so-called study drugs and supplements sold as memory boosters.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most effective way to study?

Testing yourself is one of the best-supported study methods. Instead of rereading, close your notes and recall what you know with a blank page, flashcards or practice questions, then check and fix the gaps. Combine this with spaced reviews over days and weeks, mix problem types, and explain ideas in your own words. Short, active sessions beat long, passive ones.

How many hours a day should I study?

There is no perfect number, and quality matters more than hours. Many students do well with one main focused block of 60 to 120 minutes a day plus a short review, built from focus sessions of 25 to 50 minutes with breaks. Increase the time as exams approach, but protect sleep. Two hours of active recall usually beats five hours of rereading.

How can I remember what I study?

Review new material the next day, then a few days later, then about a week later and again a few weeks later. Make each review active by recalling from memory before checking. Connect new ideas to what you already know, use concrete examples and draw simple diagrams. Good sleep also helps your brain store what you learned.

Is it better to study at night or in the morning?

Either can work. What matters most is studying when your energy is reasonably high, keeping a regular schedule and getting enough sleep. If you study late, stop in time for a normal bedtime, because staying up cuts into the sleep that helps memory. Try both for a week and notice when your focus and recall feel strongest.

Does cramming work?

Cramming can help you pass a test the next day, but much of the information fades quickly afterwards, which hurts in cumulative exams and later courses. Spreading the same hours across several short sessions usually gives better results with less stress. If you must cram, test yourself rather than reread, focus on likely questions and still get some sleep.

Can I use AI to help me study?

Yes, as a tutor rather than a shortcut. Ask an AI assistant to quiz you on a topic, explain an idea in simpler words or check your explanation for gaps. Do not use it to produce graded work if your school's rules forbid it, and always check facts against your course materials, since AI answers can be wrong.

Start with your very next study session: close your notes, take a blank page and write everything you remember about one topic for 10 minutes, then fill the gaps in a different color and book a quick review for tomorrow.
