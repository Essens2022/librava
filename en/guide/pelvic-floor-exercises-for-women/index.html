Pelvic Floor Exercises for Women: A Clear Step by Step Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you leak a little when you laugh, cough, sneeze or jump, or you sometimes have to rush to the toilet, you are in very good company, and it is not something you just have to live with. The short answer is that regular pelvic floor exercises help many women: squeeze and lift as if stopping wind and the flow of urine at the same time, hold for a few seconds, then fully relax, and do a set of slow and quick squeezes about three times a day for at least three months.

This guide explains how to find the right muscles, how to check you are doing it correctly, the mistakes to avoid, how to use your pelvic floor in daily life, the bladder habits that help, and a 12-week plan. It also covers when to see a doctor or pelvic health physical therapist.

What your pelvic floor does

Your pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles that runs from your pubic bone at the front to your tailbone at the back. It supports your bladder, bowel and womb (uterus), helps you control when you pee and pass wind, and works with your tummy and back muscles every time you cough, lift or move.

Many things can weaken or strain it: pregnancy and childbirth, lower estrogen (oestrogen) around menopause, getting older, a long-term cough, constipation and straining, regular heavy lifting and carrying extra weight.

The main types of bladder leaks are:

  • Stress leaks: a little urine escapes when you cough, sneeze, laugh, lift or jump.
  • Urge leaks (overactive bladder): a sudden strong need to go, sometimes leaking before you get there.
  • Mixed leaks: a combination of both, which is common.

Studies suggest pelvic floor muscle training is a first-line, well supported treatment for stress leaks, and it helps many women with mixed leaks too.

Check first. This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor (GP), nurse, midwife or a pelvic health physical therapist (UK: physiotherapist) about leaks at any stage, and see a doctor promptly if your symptoms are new, severe or worrying. You do not need to wait or feel embarrassed; help is available.

How to find your pelvic floor muscles

These muscles are inside, so you learn to feel them rather than see them. Sit on a firm chair with your feet flat, lie on your back with your knees bent, or stand. Let your thighs, buttocks and tummy go soft, and breathe normally.

Now imagine you are stopping yourself passing wind and, at the same time, stopping the flow of urine. Gently squeeze and lift inside, drawing those openings up and in. You should feel a tightening around the back passage and a lift inside toward the front. Then let go completely and feel the muscles soften.

Some women find a picture helps, such as an elevator rising gently from the ground floor or a drawstring bag being pulled closed and lifted.

Ways to check you have it right:

  • Sitting and leaning forward slightly, you should feel the area between your legs lift a little away from the seat.
  • Lying with knees bent, a hand mirror should show the area between the vagina and anus draw up and in, not bulge down.

Up to about a third of women find this tricky at first. If you cannot feel anything, or feel a pushing down instead of a lift, a pelvic health physical therapist can check your technique.

How to do pelvic floor exercises step by step

There are two kinds of squeeze, and you need both.

Slow squeezes for strength

  1. Get comfortable, with tummy, buttocks and thighs relaxed.
  2. Squeeze and lift your pelvic floor.
  3. Hold for a few seconds, breathing normally. Start with about 3 seconds and build up to about 10.
  4. Let go fully and rest for the same amount of time.
  5. Repeat, building up to about 10 slow squeezes.

Quick squeezes for sneezes and surprises

  1. Squeeze and lift quickly.
  2. Let go straight away, fully.
  3. Repeat, building up to about 10.

Aim for one set of slow and quick squeezes about three times a day. Start lying down if that is easiest, then progress to sitting, then standing and during everyday tasks. Linking sets to things you already do, such as after brushing your teeth, while the kettle boils or at red lights, makes the habit stick.

Common pelvic floor exercise mistakes

  • Holding your breath. Keep breathing normally throughout; counting out loud helps.
  • Squeezing your buttocks, thighs or tummy hard. These should stay relaxed. A gentle tightening low in the tummy is fine.
  • Pushing down instead of lifting. The feeling should be up and in, never bearing down.
  • Not letting go fully. Relaxing completely between squeezes matters as much as the squeeze itself.
  • Practicing stopping your urine flow on the toilet. You can do this once to identify the muscles, but not as a regular exercise, because it can stop your bladder emptying properly.
  • Giving up too soon. It takes regular practice for at least about three months to see the full benefit.

If the exercises cause pain, or you have pain with sex, pelvic pain or trouble emptying your bladder or bowel, your pelvic floor may be tense rather than weak. Stop and see a doctor or pelvic health physical therapist rather than doing more squeezes.

How to stop leaking when you cough or sneeze

Once you can find your muscles, start using them at the moments that matter. This is often called the knack: squeeze and lift just before and during a cough, sneeze, laugh or lift.

  • Practice with pretend coughs first: squeeze, cough, then relax.
  • When you feel a real sneeze coming, squeeze and hold until it passes.
  • When lifting a child, shopping or a laundry basket, breathe out and squeeze as you lift, and keep the load close to your body.
  • Avoid holding your breath and straining on effort, which pushes down on the pelvic floor.

While you build strength, lower impact activity such as walking, swimming and cycling is kinder than running and jumping. Return to high impact exercise gradually, and ease back if you notice leaks, heaviness or dragging.

Bladder habits that help reduce leaks

Exercises work best alongside good bladder and bowel habits.

  • Drink normally, around 6 to 8 glasses (about 1.5 to 2 liters, or 3 to 4 US pints) a day unless a doctor has advised otherwise. Cutting back makes urine more concentrated and can irritate the bladder.
  • Caffeine, fizzy drinks and alcohol can irritate the bladder for some people. Try cutting down gradually.
  • Most adults pee about 4 to 8 times in the day and up to about once at night. Avoid going just in case too often.
  • Sit fully on the toilet seat rather than hovering, relax, take your time and lean forward slightly to empty fully.
  • Avoid constipation with fluids, fiber and activity. A small footstool so your knees are higher than your hips helps, and never strain.

For sudden urges, try stop, squeeze, breathe, wait: stand still, do 5 or 6 quick squeezes, breathe calmly until the urge fades, then walk to the toilet. A 3-day bladder diary of drinks, toilet visits and leaks helps you and your doctor see patterns.

After birth, menopause and later life

After birth, gentle pelvic floor exercises can usually start soon after a vaginal or cesarean (caesarean) birth, once you are comfortable and peeing normally, if your midwife or doctor agrees. Leaks and heaviness are common early on and often improve over weeks to months. Mention any ongoing leaks or heaviness at your postnatal check, and wait until at least about 12 weeks, with no leaks, heaviness or pain, before running or high impact exercise.

Around menopause, lower estrogen can make bladder and vaginal tissues thinner and drier, which can add to urgency, leaks and repeated urine infections. A doctor can discuss treatments such as vaginal estrogen, which is local and suitable for many women. It is never too late to start pelvic floor exercises.

Bladder and pelvic symptoms that need a doctor

  • Blood in your urine (pink, red or brown), even once: see a doctor promptly.
  • Burning when peeing, needing to go urgently and often, or cloudy urine: see a doctor or pharmacist.
  • The above with fever, shivering, back or side pain or feeling very unwell, or an older adult who becomes confused: urgent same day care.
  • Being unable to pee with a full, painful bladder: urgent care the same day.
  • Sudden loss of bladder or bowel control with numbness around the genitals, buttocks or inner thighs, or new leg weakness: call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand).
  • Any bleeding after menopause, between periods or after sex, a bulge or heaviness, or pelvic pain: see a doctor.

Also see a doctor or pelvic health physical therapist if leaks are not improving after about three months of regular exercises, or you leak stool or wind.

Frequently asked questions

How long do pelvic floor exercises take to work?

Some women notice small improvements within a few weeks, but it usually takes regular practice for at least about three months to see the full benefit. Like any muscle, the pelvic floor gets stronger gradually. Keep doing your sets about three times a day, and keep a bladder diary at the start and after 12 weeks to see your progress.

Can you do pelvic floor exercises too much?

Yes, more squeezing is not always better. Doing far more than you need can leave the muscles tired or tense. Stick to about three sets a day and always relax fully between squeezes. If you feel pain, have pain with sex or find it hard to empty your bladder, stop and see a pelvic health physical therapist.

How do I know if I am doing pelvic floor exercises correctly?

You should feel a squeeze and lift inside, with your buttocks, thighs and tummy staying relaxed and your breathing steady. Sitting and leaning forward, the area between your legs should lift slightly off the seat. If you feel a pushing down or nothing at all, a pelvic health physical therapist can check your technique.

Is it ever too late to strengthen your pelvic floor?

No. The pelvic floor is muscle, and muscles can get stronger at any age. Women in their seventies and eighties can benefit from regular pelvic floor exercises and better bladder habits. Around and after menopause, a doctor can also discuss treatments for bladder and vaginal changes alongside your exercises.

Should I cut down on drinks to stop leaking?

Usually not. Cutting back on fluids makes urine more concentrated, which can irritate the bladder and make urgency worse. Aim for around 6 to 8 glasses a day unless your doctor has advised otherwise. It can help to cut down gradually on caffeine, fizzy drinks and alcohol, and to drink less in the hour or two before bed.

When should I see someone about bladder leaks?

Whenever leaks bother you, at any stage. You do not need to wait. See a doctor or pelvic health physical therapist if leaks are not improving after about three months of exercises, you have urgency or get up often at night, cannot find the muscles, leak stool or wind, or feel heaviness or a bulge.

Your first step today is to find your pelvic floor: lie down with your knees bent, relax your tummy and buttocks, then gently squeeze and lift as if stopping wind and urine, hold for 3 seconds and let go fully. Do 5 of these now, and link your next set to brushing your teeth tonight.
