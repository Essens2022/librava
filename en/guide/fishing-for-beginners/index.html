Fishing for Beginners: How to Catch Your First Fish From Shore

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You want to take a rod to the local pond, maybe with a child or grandchild, but the tackle aisle looks like a wall of secrets. The short answer: get a fishing license if your area requires one, buy a 6 to 7 foot medium-light spinning combo, tie on a size 8 or 10 hook, add a split shot and a small bobber, bait it with a piece of worm and fish close to weeds, docks or fallen trees early in the morning.

That simple setup catches bluegill, sunfish and perch in almost any pond. This guide covers licenses and rules, what to buy, one reliable knot, the bobber rig, bait, where to cast, how to hook and land a fish gently and how to stay safe.

Do you need a fishing license

Usually, yes. Check the rules before you wet a line, because fines are an expensive way to learn.

  • United States. Most anglers 16 and older need a state fishing license, though the age cutoff varies by state. Many states also sell one-day or short-term licenses, and resident fees are often roughly tabindex="-1"5 to $60 a year.
  • England and Wales. Anyone 13 or older needs a rod fishing licence from the Environment Agency for freshwater fishing. It is free for ages 13 to 16 but still required. You also need permission, a day ticket or a club permit from whoever owns the fishing rights.
  • Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own systems, so check locally.

A license is only part of it. Regulations also set seasons, minimum size limits and bag limits (how many fish you may keep per day), and some waters have special rules, such as catch and release only or restrictions on live baitfish. In England and Wales, the coarse fishing close season on rivers generally runs from 15 March to 15 June. Read the current regulations for the exact water you plan to fish.

The best beginner fishing setup

Your first rod should be cheap and forgiving. For most adults, that means a spinning combo, a rod and reel sold together and matched to each other.

ItemWhat to look for
RodAbout 6 to 7 feet, medium-light power
ReelSpinning reel sized for 6 to 10 lb monofilament
LineMonofilament, 4 to 8 lb for panfish
HooksSizes 6, 8 and 10
Weights and floatsSplit shot and a few small bobbers (floats)
ToolsNeedle-nose pliers and line clippers

For children under about ten, a spincast combo with a closed cover and push button is easier to cast and harder to tangle. A short 4 to 5 foot rod suits small arms. Skip the toy-style rods with cartoon pictures, as many have reels that jam.

Hook sizes confuse everyone at first: for sizes like 6, 8 and 10, a bigger number means a smaller hook. A complete beginner kit in the US runs roughly $70 to tabindex="-1"50, plus a license. Spend on the reel before the rod, because a smooth drag saves more fish than a fancy rod, and buy lures one or two at a time once you know what works where you fish.

One knot to tie first

Five knots cover almost everything in freshwater fishing, but you only need one to start. The improved clinch knot ties your line to a hook, lure or swivel and works well with monofilament.

  1. Pass about 6 inches (15 cm) of line through the hook eye.
  2. Wrap the loose end (the tag end) around the main line 5 to 7 times, using fewer wraps with heavier line.
  3. Push the tag end through the small loop just above the eye.
  4. Bring the tag end back through the big loop you just made.
  5. Wet the knot with water or saliva, pull the main line slowly until the coils snug down, then trim the tag end.
Always wet a knot before tightening it. A dry knot heats up from friction and weakens the line. Test every knot with a firm pull before you cast, and retie after every fish or snag.

When you are ready, add the Palomar knot (strong and simple, good with braided line), the uni knot, the surgeon's loop and the double uni for joining two lines. Practice at the kitchen table, not on the bank.

How to set up a bobber rig

The bobber rig, called a float rig in the UK, holds your bait at a set depth and shows you when a fish bites. It is the first rig nearly everyone learns.

  1. Tie a size 8 or 10 hook to the end of your line. A long-shank hook is easier to remove from a small mouth.
  2. Pinch one or two small split shot onto the line 6 to 12 inches above the hook.
  3. Clip a bobber onto the line 2 to 4 feet above the hook. Use the smallest one that floats your bait, about the size of a large grape or a ping pong ball.
  4. Thread on a piece of worm about an inch long so it covers the hook point.

The bobber should sit upright with only its top showing; if it lies flat, add a little more split shot. Cast near cover and wait. If nothing happens in five minutes, move the bobber up or down a foot or try a new spot.

Little twitches and rings mean a fish is pecking. When the bobber goes under and stays under, or slides steadily sideways, reel up the slack and lift the rod tip firmly about a foot or two.

What bait to use for beginners

Live bait is the easiest way to catch fish while you are learning, because the fish do the work of finding it.

  • Worms. The all-around champion for sunfish, perch, trout and catfish. Use small pieces for small mouths and whole nightcrawlers for bass and catfish.
  • Crickets, mealworms and wax worms. Great for bluegill and other panfish.
  • Minnows. Excellent for crappie and bass, but some places restrict live baitfish to prevent the spread of invasive species and disease, so check first.
  • Corn and bread. Work for some species where allowed.

Keep bait cool and shaded, never baking in a hot car. A cup of worms goes a long way for one trip.

Once bait fishing feels easy, add a few simple lures: a small inline spinner, a soft plastic worm and a small jig cover a lot of situations. A Texas-rigged plastic worm, with the hook point tucked into the plastic, lets you fish weeds and wood for largemouth bass without snagging.

Where to find fish in a pond or lake

Fish want food, shelter and comfortable water. In practice that means they gather near cover and changes in the bottom.

  • Weed edges and lily pads
  • Docks and the shady side of piers
  • Fallen trees, brush piles and logs
  • Rocky points and gravel bars, especially for smallmouth bass
  • Inlets where water flows in, and deeper holes in rivers

A very common beginner mistake is casting as far as possible every time. In ponds and small lakes, panfish and bass often sit within ten feet of the bank, so try the water near your feet first.

Timing matters too. Early morning and evening are usually best, especially in summer, and cloudy days can keep fish feeding longer. In late spring, when the water warms to around 70°F (21°C), sunfish gather in the shallows to spawn, and you can often see their nests as pale round patches on the bottom. Catfish bite well on warm evenings and at night.

Before your first trip, walk the shore for 20 minutes without a rod and note every dock, weed bed and fallen tree. That little map tells you where to start.

How to hook, land and release a fish

Losing fish at the last moment is normal at first. A few habits fix most of it.

Set your drag

The drag is the brake inside your reel that lets line slip so it does not break. Pull line off by hand: it should come out with a firm, steady pull. When a fish is taking line, stop turning the handle, or you will only twist the line.

Fight and land it

Keep the rod tip up with a good bend and the line tight. When the fish stops pulling, lift the rod slowly, then lower it while reeling in the slack. Small panfish can be swung in; for bigger fish, put a net in the water and lead the fish in headfirst.

Handle it gently

  • Wet your hands first to protect the fish's slime coat.
  • Support bigger fish under the belly and keep them horizontal.
  • Use pliers to back the hook out the way it went in.
  • If a fish you plan to release is hooked deep, cut the line close to the hook rather than tearing it out.
  • Hold it upright in the water until it swims off on its own.

Pinching the barbs flat with pliers makes releasing fish, and unhooking your own sleeve, much easier. If you keep fish, make sure they are legal size and within the bag limit, put them on ice straight away and check local fish consumption advisories.

Fishing safety for beginners

Put a properly fitted life jacket (PFD, or buoyancy aid in the UK) on young children near deep water, on docks and piers, and on everyone in a boat. At the first sound of thunder, reel in and get off the water into a hard-topped vehicle or building, because graphite rods conduct electricity.
  • Look behind and to both sides before every cast, and wear sunglasses or safety glasses to protect your eyes from hooks.
  • Wear a hat, sunscreen and a light long-sleeved shirt; water reflects sunlight.
  • Wear shoes with good grip and stay back from wet rocks and undercut banks.
  • Never fish alone in remote places, and tell someone where you are going.
  • Take your used line and litter home, since discarded line harms birds.
  • Clean, drain and dry your gear and boat when moving between waters to avoid spreading invasive species.

If a hook buries past the barb, cut the line, protect the hook and see a doctor or urgent care. Get emergency help for any hook in or near the eye. Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest fish to catch for beginners?

Bluegill and other sunfish are the easiest fish for beginners in the US. They live in almost every pond and lake, bite readily and fight hard for their size. Use a size 8 or 10 hook with a small piece of worm under a small bobber near weeds or docks. In the UK, roach and perch are friendly first targets on a simple float rig.

What time of day is best for fishing?

Early morning and evening are usually the best times, especially in summer when midday heat pushes fish deeper or into shade. Overcast days can keep fish feeding longer. Catfish often bite best in the evening and at night. If you can only fish at midday, focus on shady spots such as under docks, overhanging trees and thick weeds.

What size hook should a beginner use?

For panfish such as bluegill and sunfish, use a size 8 or 10 hook, because they have small mouths. Size 6 suits slightly bigger fish and baits. For crappie with minnows, a light wire size 4 or 6 works well. Remember that for these sizes, a larger number means a smaller hook, while sizes written as 1/0, 2/0 or 3/0 get bigger as the number grows.

Do kids need a fishing license?

In most US states, children under 16 can fish without a license, but the age cutoff varies by state, so check your state's rules. In England and Wales, children under 13 do not need a rod licence, while those aged 13 to 16 need one, though it is free. The adult helping them may still need a license, especially if also holding a rod.

How much does it cost to start fishing?

A beginner can be fully set up in the US for roughly $70 to tabindex="-1"50: a spinning combo, basic hooks, weights and bobbers, pliers and line clippers, and bait for a trip. Add the cost of a state license, which varies widely. Costs creep up mainly through lures and gadgets, so start with bait and buy lures one or two at a time.

Why am I not getting any bites?

Usually the bait is in the wrong place or at the wrong depth. Try casting closer to cover such as weeds, docks or fallen trees, move your bobber up or down a foot, and use a smaller hook and bait piece. If nothing happens after 20 minutes, change one thing at a time: depth, bait or spot. Early morning and evening often bring more bites.

Your first step today is to look up the fishing license rules for where you live, then practice casting a hookless practice weight at a bucket in the yard 20 times. Once your casts land close and you can tie an improved clinch knot without looking at the steps, pack a bobber rig, a cup of worms and a life jacket for any child, and head to the nearest park pond early in the morning.
