How to Start Freelancing: A Step-by-Step Plan for Your First Clients

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have a skill people pay for and you would like to earn from it on your own terms, you are in good company. To start freelancing, work out how many months of bills your savings cover, choose one clear niche and offer, set a minimum rate that includes tax and time off, make two or three samples, and tell the people who already know you that you are open for work.

Most people do this on the side first, keeping their job until freelance income covers a good share of their essential bills. This guide walks you through each step with worked numbers you can copy using your own figures.

Is freelancing right for you?

Freelancing means selling your skills to several clients instead of one employer. You choose your clients, your hours and often your location, and your income is not capped by a salary band.

The trade-offs are real. Income is uneven, with busy months followed by quiet ones. Nobody pays you for vacation (holiday in the UK), sick days or training. In the US you may need your own health insurance, and in both countries you plan retirement saving yourself. You also take on the hidden work: finding clients, writing proposals, sending invoices, chasing payments and keeping records. Plan for that unpaid admin to take a large slice of your week.

Check your contract first

If you are employed, read your contract and staff handbook before taking paid work. Some restrict outside work, working for competitors or using company equipment, and some give the employer ownership of work you create in certain situations.

Work out your financial runway

Your runway is how many months you could cover essential bills if freelance work went quiet. It is the most useful number for deciding when to go full time.

  1. Add up your essential monthly costs: rent or mortgage, utilities, food, transportation, insurance and minimum debt payments.
  2. Divide your available savings by that total.

Say your essentials come to $3,200 a month and you have $9,600 saved. That is three months of runway. Many freelancers aim for three to six months before leaving a job, but the right figure depends on your household and how steady your first clients are.

If your runway is short, freelance on the side until savings grow or freelance income covers a large part of your essentials. Keep tax money separate from your runway, because part of every payment will belong to the tax authorities.

Choose a niche people understand

Specialists usually find clients faster than generalists. When you say you write for anyone, nobody thinks of you. When you say you write email newsletters for independent bookshops, every bookshop owner knows you are for them, and everyone else knows who to send your way.

You can narrow down in three ways:

  • By industry, such as dental practices, cafes, nonprofits or real estate agents (estate agents in the UK).
  • By problem, such as rescuing slow websites or turning messy spreadsheets into monthly reports.
  • By deliverable, such as product photos, slide decks or podcast show notes.

Before you commit, look at ten people in your niche and talk to two or three about what eats up their week. If you hear the same frustration three times, you have something. Treat your first niche as a three-month test, and remember you can still accept good work outside it.

Write your offer statement

Use this template: I help [type of client] [get a result] by [what you do], so they can [benefit]. For example: I help self-employed tradespeople get their books in order before tax deadlines by catching up on overdue bookkeeping, so they can stop dreading paperwork. If a stranger could repeat it after hearing it once, it is ready.

How much to charge as a freelancer

Most new freelancers undercharge because they compare a freelance rate with their old hourly wage. As a freelancer you pay for your own time off, equipment, software, insurance and extra taxes, so the same hourly number leaves you worse off.

Start with a minimum hourly rate:

Minimum hourly rate = (target income + business expenses + tax set-aside) divided by billable hours per year.

Notice the 20 billable hours. Almost nobody bills 40 hours week after week, because admin, marketing and proposals take the rest. Plan on 15 to 25 billable hours for a full-time week.

Most clients prefer a fixed project price. Estimate the hours honestly, including calls and revisions, multiply by your rate and add a buffer. Track your real hours on every project so your estimates improve.

Build a portfolio with no clients

Clients want proof you can do the work, not a long history. If you have no freelance clients yet, you can still show that proof.

  • Use work from your job if you are allowed to share it, with private details removed.
  • Create two or three sample projects that look like real jobs in your niche, such as a redesigned menu for an imaginary cafe or a sample newsletter for a type of shop.
  • Do a small piece for a friend's business or a local charity, on clear terms you choose.

For each piece, write a short case study: the problem, what you did and the result. Put it all on a simple website or a professional networking profile with your offer statement at the top. Ask former managers or colleagues for a short testimonial about working with you.

Do not wait for a perfect site or logo before contacting anyone. A clear offer and a few solid samples are enough to start conversations.

How to find your first freelance clients

Your first clients rarely find you. You find them, and the first few usually come from people who already know you.

  1. Warm network. List at least 50 people: former colleagues, managers, friends, family and neighbors. Send each a short personal note saying what you do and who you help, and ask them to keep you in mind.
  2. Past employers. They know your work and may have a gap since you left. Check any non-compete or non-solicitation clauses first.
  3. Referrals. Make them easy by giving people a two-line description they can forward.
  4. Freelance marketplaces. Apply to fewer jobs, tailor each reply to the client's own words and keep payment on the platform.
  5. Cold and local outreach. Research a business, mention one specific thing you could improve, keep it to 80 to 120 words and follow up once.

Track every contact in a simple sheet with a next action date. Expect silence, which usually means busy rather than no. Steady outreach of 30 to 60 minutes a day for 30 days does more than one frantic weekend.

Contracts, getting paid and avoiding scams

Every project needs a written agreement, even a short one. It should cover scope and deliverables, deadlines, number of revisions, price and payment terms, a deposit, who owns the work (usually transferring on full payment), how either side can end the work and confidentiality. Having a lawyer review your template once is money well spent.

Ask for a deposit before you start, often a share of the fee, and use milestones on longer projects. Send clear invoices with a due date and chase late ones calmly, first with a friendly reminder and then with a firmer note.

Watch for these red flags:

  • Large unpaid test projects
  • Overpayments or checks (cheques) where you are asked to send money back
  • Pressure to move off a marketplace before any work is agreed
  • Job offers that ask you to pay for training or equipment
  • Clients who refuse to sign anything

Taxes and money when you freelance

Freelance payments usually arrive with no tax taken off, so set aside a share of every payment in a separate account the day it lands. Keep business and personal money apart, and log income and expenses as you go.

  • In the US, freelancers generally pay self-employment tax on top of income tax and may need to make quarterly estimated payments. Clients may send you a 1099-NEC form.
  • In the UK, you usually register as self-employed with HMRC, file a Self Assessment return and pay National Insurance, and may need to make payments on account. Making Tax Digital for Income Tax is being phased in from April 2026 for some people above an income threshold.

Build a buffer fund for quiet months, look into professional indemnity or liability insurance, and plan your own retirement saving.

This is general information, not tax, legal or financial advice. Rules and thresholds change, so check current official guidance and talk to an accountant about your situation.

Frequently asked questions

Can I start freelancing with no experience?

Yes, if you have a skill someone will pay for, even if you have never freelanced. Use relevant work from a job, school or volunteering, then create two or three sample projects aimed at your chosen niche. Start with small, clearly defined jobs from people who know you. You do not need to be the best in your field, just reliable, easy to work with and clear about what you deliver.

How long does it take to get your first freelance client?

It varies widely, from a few days to several months. People who message their warm network directly tend to land work sooner than those who wait for marketplaces or a website to bring clients. A steady daily routine of outreach, tracked in a simple sheet, usually gives you a clearer picture within about 30 days of which channels are working for you.

Should I quit my job to start freelancing?

Usually not right away. Many people freelance on the side for six to twelve months and switch once freelance income covers a good share of their essential bills and they have three to six months of runway saved. Going straight to full time can make sense with strong savings or clients already lined up, but the pressure to accept every job is much higher.

What skills are in demand for freelancers?

Common freelance skills include writing and editing, design, marketing, virtual assistance, bookkeeping, web development, translation, consulting, photography and tutoring. A skill sells best when you describe the problem it solves for a specific client, not just the skill itself. Some fields, such as finance, law and health, may need licenses, so check the rules before you offer them.

How do freelancers get paid?

Freelancers usually send an invoice after agreeing a price in writing, with clear payment terms such as a due date. Many ask for a deposit before starting and use milestone payments on longer projects. Clients typically pay by bank transfer or an online payment method. Keep copies of every invoice and payment for your tax records, and chase late payments promptly and politely.

Do I need a business to start freelancing?

In many cases you can start as a self-employed individual, called a sole proprietor in the US or a sole trader in the UK, without forming a company. You will still usually need to register or report your income for tax and keep records. Some people later set up a company for other reasons. An accountant can tell you which option suits you.

The best first step today is to add up your essential monthly costs and divide your savings by that number, so you know your runway. Then write your offer statement and send five personal messages to people who already know you.
