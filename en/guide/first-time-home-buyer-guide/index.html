First-Time Home Buyer Guide: Clear Steps From Savings to Keys

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you are tired of renting but the home buying process feels like a maze of jargon, you are exactly who this guide is for. As a first-time buyer, you work out a budget you are comfortable with, save a deposit (down payment) plus buying costs, get your credit ready, get pre-approved or an agreement in principle, then find a home, make an offer, have it inspected or surveyed and close or complete with professional help.

Most first-time buyers need months of preparation before they ever view a home, and that is normal. Below you will find each stage in plain words, with US and UK differences side by side, so you can plan your own timeline.

The home buying process step by step

  1. Check you are ready to buy.
  2. Set your own comfortable budget.
  3. Save your deposit or down payment, plus buying costs.
  4. Get your credit and paperwork ready.
  5. Learn how mortgages work.
  6. Get pre-approved (US) or an agreement in principle (UK).
  7. Build your team: agent, lawyer or conveyancer, inspector or surveyor.
  8. Search and view homes.
  9. Make an offer.
  10. Inspect and value the home.
  11. Close (US) or complete (UK) and collect the keys.

The early stages take the longest, often 6 to 24 months, and they make everything after them smoother. From an accepted offer to keys usually takes 1 to 3 months, and often longer in the UK because of searches and property chains.

This guide is general information, not financial, legal or tax advice. Rates, programs and tax thresholds change often, so check current figures and confirm decisions with a licensed mortgage lender or broker, a real estate attorney or title company (US) or a solicitor or licensed conveyancer (UK).

Are you ready to buy a home

Renting is not wasted money. It buys flexibility and freedom from repair bills, and it can be the right choice for a while. Buying usually makes more sense when your job and life feel stable and you plan to stay put for several years.

Keep an emergency fund that is separate from your deposit. New owners face surprise costs fast, and you do not want a broken boiler or water heater to land on a credit card in month two.

How much house can you afford

Lenders measure affordability differently in each country.

  • US: lenders look at your debt-to-income ratio (DTI), your monthly debt payments compared with gross monthly income. A classic rule of thumb is housing costs around 28 percent of gross income and total debts around 36 percent, though many loans allow more.
  • UK: lenders use an income multiple, roughly 4 to 4.5 times annual income is commonly quoted, plus a detailed check of your outgoings from bank statements.

A higher limit is permission, not a recommendation. Many people aim to keep total housing costs to about a quarter to a third of take-home pay.

Stress test your budget

Recalculate your monthly budget three ways: with a mortgage rate 2 percentage points higher, with 20 percent less household income and with a large repair bill in your first year. If one of these would sink you, look at a cheaper price, a bigger deposit or a longer saving period. Then write down your maximum purchase price and treat it as a ceiling.

How much deposit or down payment you need

Your savings target has three parts: the deposit or down payment, the buying costs and a moving buffer. First-time buyers often forget the last two.

In the US, down payments vary by loan type. Some conventional loans allow a small down payment, and government-backed loans (FHA, VA for eligible veterans and service members, USDA in eligible rural areas) can allow less. On a conventional loan, 20 percent down avoids private mortgage insurance (PMI). Closing costs are typically a few percent of the loan amount on top.

In the UK, most lenders want at least 5 to 10 percent, and a bigger deposit usually gets you better rates. Budget too for solicitor or conveyancer fees, searches, a survey and possibly Stamp Duty Land Tax (or the Welsh and Scottish equivalents), where first-time buyer relief may apply.

Gifts from family usually need a gift letter and a clear paper trail. First-time buyer programs exist in many US states, and the UK has government-backed schemes such as Lifetime ISAs, but rules change, so check current details.

Get your credit and paperwork ready

US lenders pull reports from the three major credit bureaus. UK lenders check your credit file with the main agencies and use their own scoring. Either way, check every report or file early, because fixing errors takes time.

  • Pay every bill on time, ideally by automatic payment.
  • Bring card balances down.
  • Avoid new credit applications in the months before you apply.
  • UK buyers: make sure you are on the electoral register at your current address.

Start a mortgage folder with ID, recent payslips or pay stubs, tax records and bank statements. Then keep your finances boring until you have the keys. No new car loans, no big furniture on credit and no job change without talking to your lender first.

Mortgages and pre-approval explained

A mortgage is a loan secured on your home, made up of the amount borrowed (principal), interest, a term and a rate. A fixed rate keeps your payment predictable. An adjustable rate (US) or a tracker or variable rate (UK) can move up or down. A longer term lowers the monthly payment but means more interest overall, which is why many US buyers compare 15 and 30 year terms.

Compare offers on the rate, the APR (APRC in the UK), the fees and the monthly payment together, never one number alone.

Pre-qualification, pre-approval and agreement in principle

In the US, pre-qualification is a rough estimate, while pre-approval involves checked documents and a credit pull. In the UK, an agreement in principle (also called a mortgage in principle) plays a similar role. None of these is a guarantee. Get one before serious viewing so you shop with a real number, and ask any broker how they are paid and how many lenders they search.

Find the right home and make an offer

Before viewing, write three columns: needs, wants and dealbreakers. Location affects daily life and resale more than almost any feature inside the home, so visit areas on a weekday evening and at the weekend.

In the US, a buyer's agent represents you, and you usually sign a written agreement before touring homes. Agent pay is negotiable. In the UK, the estate agent works for the seller. For condos with HOA fees (US) or leasehold flats (UK), check fees, reserves, lease length, ground rent, service charges and planned works.

Making the offer

Base your offer on recent sold prices for similar homes, not the asking price, and set your walk-away number before you start. US offers become binding contracts with contingencies for inspection, financing and appraisal, each with a deadline. UK offers go through the estate agent, subject to contract and survey, and are not binding until exchange of contracts (in Scotland, until missives are concluded), which is why gazumping can happen in England and Wales.

Inspections, surveys and closing day

An inspection (US) or survey (UK) checks the home's condition for you. An appraisal or valuation checks its value for the lender. In the UK, choose a survey level that fits the home: a basic condition report suits newer homes, while a full building survey suits older or unusual ones. Sort the report into safety issues, big-ticket repairs and normal wear before you react, then renegotiate on significant problems with evidence. Walking away from the wrong home is a sensible outcome.

In the US, closing follows underwriting, title work and a final walkthrough, and you must receive the Closing Disclosure at least 3 business days before closing. Compare it line by line with your Loan Estimate. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the deal becomes binding at exchange, and you get the keys at completion.

Payment redirection scams target home buyers. Always verify bank details by phone using a number you already know, and never trust an email that says payment instructions have changed.

Frequently asked questions

How much do I need to save to buy my first home?

Plan for three things: the deposit or down payment, buying costs and a moving buffer, plus a separate emergency fund. In the US, closing costs are typically a few percent of the loan amount. In the UK, most lenders want at least 5 to 10 percent down, plus legal fees, searches, a survey and any property tax. Get real figures from a lender or broker for your area.

Do I really need 20 percent down to buy a house?

No. In the US, 20 percent down on a conventional loan avoids private mortgage insurance, but smaller down payments are common, and some government-backed loans allow very little. PMI adds to your monthly cost and can usually be removed later once you build enough equity. In the UK, deposits of 5 to 10 percent are typical minimums, and more usually means better rates.

What is the difference between pre-qualification and pre-approval?

Pre-qualification is a quick estimate based on what you tell a lender. Pre-approval goes further: the lender checks your documents and pulls your credit, so the figure is more reliable and sellers take it more seriously. Neither is a final loan approval. In the UK, the agreement in principle plays a similar role before a formal mortgage offer.

How long does it take to buy a house?

Getting ready, with budgeting, saving and credit work, often takes 6 to 24 months. Pre-approval or an agreement in principle can take 1 to 4 weeks, searching 1 to 6 months, and accepted offer to keys about 1 to 3 months. The UK stage from offer to completion is often longer because of searches, surveys and chains of linked sales.

Is it better to rent or buy?

It depends on your stability, savings and plans. Buying tends to make more sense if you expect to stay several years, have an emergency fund separate from your deposit and can afford the full cost of owning, including maintenance and insurance. Renting buys flexibility and freedom from repair bills, and waiting until you are ready is always a valid choice.

Do first-time buyers pay stamp duty?

In England and Northern Ireland, Stamp Duty Land Tax may apply, but first-time buyer relief can reduce or remove it depending on the price. Wales has Land Transaction Tax and Scotland has Land and Buildings Transaction Tax, each with its own rules. Thresholds change, so check the current figures on official government sources and ask your conveyancer to confirm.
