Embroidery Stitches for Beginners: 10 Easy Stitches, Step by Step

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have a hoop, a pack of floss and a lovely design in mind, and then you stare at the needle wondering where it is supposed to go first. The short answer: start with five stitches, backstitch, stem stitch, satin stitch, lazy daisy and the French knot, using two or three strands of floss on tightly woven cotton held drum tight in a 6 inch (15 cm) hoop.

Those five will cover outlines, stems, solid shapes, petals and dots, which is most of what you see in simple hoop designs. Below you will find the setup that makes every stitch easier, clear step by step instructions in words, five more stitches to add once you feel steady and fixes for the problems almost every beginner runs into.

What you need to start embroidery

A small kit is easier to learn with than a big one. Here is everything you need for your first stitches:

  • One wooden or plastic embroidery hoop, 5 or 6 inches (13 or 15 cm) across
  • About half a yard (half a meter) of medium-weight cotton or cotton-linen in white or cream
  • A pack of embroidery needles, also called crewel needles, in mixed sizes
  • Six to eight skeins of six-strand floss (stranded cotton), including a dark outline color
  • Small sharp scissors for thread, plus larger fabric scissors
  • A water-soluble fabric marking pen

Choose fabric with a firm weave you cannot easily see through and no stretch. Quilting cotton is ideal. Prewash it in warm water so any shrinking happens before your stitches go in, then iron it while slightly damp. A quick naming note: the plain cotton called muslin in the US is called calico in the UK, where muslin means a much thinner cloth.

Needle sizes run backward: the higher the number, the finer the needle. Match the needle to your thread so it opens a hole the floss can pass through without dragging.

1 or 29 or 10Fine details, small lettering

How to set up your hoop and thread

Most early frustrations come from setup, not from the stitches themselves.

Hoop it drum tight

  1. Loosen the screw and lay the inner ring on a table.
  2. Place the fabric over it, design centered, and press the outer ring down on top.
  3. Gently pull the fabric edges outward all around, along the straight lines of the weave, then tighten the screw.
  4. Tap it. A good hoop makes a soft, dull thump and barely gives.

Check the weave still runs straight, and take the fabric out of the hoop at the end of each session so it does not keep a crease.

Cut and separate floss

Cut about 18 inches (45 cm), roughly fingertip to elbow. Longer pieces tangle and fray. Then pull the strands you need out one at a time, straight up, while the rest of the bundle bunches below your fingers. Lay them side by side again. Separating them individually gives a smoother, flatter stitch.

Start without a lumpy knot

A simple knot is fine for practice, but a waste knot gives a cleaner back. Knot the end, go down from the front about 3 inches (8 cm) along the line you are about to stitch, come up at your starting point and stitch toward the knot. Your stitches trap the tail on the back, and you snip the knot off when you reach it. To end a thread, slide the needle under four or five stitches on the back and trim.

Let each stitch settle snug against the fabric instead of pulling hard. Tight stitches pucker the cloth, and the puckers stay after you take it out of the hoop.

Backstitch, the best first line stitch

Backstitch makes a solid, continuous line and is the stitch most stitchers use for outlines and lettering. Aim for stitches about 1/8 to 1/4 inch (3 to 6 mm) long.

  1. Come up one stitch length in from the start of your line.
  2. Go down at the very start of the line. That is your first stitch, worked backward.
  3. Come up one stitch length ahead of where you first came up.
  4. Go down into the hole where the previous stitch began.
  5. Repeat to the end of the line.

The secret is sharing holes, so there are no gaps or overlaps. On curves, shorten your stitches to about 1/8 inch (3 mm); long stitches turn a circle into a lumpy polygon.

Running stitch is the even simpler cousin: come up, go down one stitch length along, then leave a small gap before coming up again. It gives a light dashed line, lovely for borders.

Stem stitch for smooth curves

Stem stitch makes a raised, rope-like line that looks beautiful on stems, vines and script letters. Work it from left to right, holding the working thread below the line the whole time.

  1. Come up at the start of the line.
  2. Go down one stitch length along, leaving a little slack.
  3. With the thread held below, come up exactly halfway back along that stitch and pull it flat.
  4. Go down half a stitch length past the end of the first stitch.
  5. Come up in the hole where the previous stitch ended.
  6. Repeat, each stitch overlapping the last by half its length.

On curves, turn the hoop as you go so the short section you are working always runs left to right in front of you. Never switch the thread to the other side partway, or the twist will break.

Satin stitch to fill small shapes

Satin stitch fills a shape with straight stitches laid side by side, giving a glossy, silky finish. It works best on shapes no wider than about 1/2 inch (12 mm), such as petals, small leaves and dots.

  1. Outline the shape first in split stitch, a short line stitch where each new stitch comes up through the middle of the one before.
  2. Start at the widest part, near the middle.
  3. Come up just outside the outline on one edge, and go down just outside it on the opposite edge.
  4. Come up right beside your first point, so close no fabric shows, and go down beside the second.
  5. Always come up on the same edge and go down on the other. Fill one half, slide under your stitches on the back to return to the middle, then fill the other half.

Use two or three strands rather than six for a smoother fill, and draw a few direction guide lines first, angling out from a leaf's center vein. For larger shapes, stitches longer than 1/2 inch sag and snag, so split the shape into sections or use long and short stitch. Seed stitch, a scatter of rice-sized stitches at different angles, is an easy, relaxing alternative fill for any size.

Lazy daisy and chain stitch

Both stitches are built from a loop, and the trick is holding that loop under the needle tip as you pull through.

Lazy daisy (detached chain)

  1. Come up where you want the base of the petal.
  2. Go down into the same hole or right beside it, leaving a loop on the front.
  3. Come up at the petal tip, inside the loop.
  4. Pull gently until the loop forms a rounded petal, not a thin line.
  5. Go down just outside the loop at the tip to tack it in place.

For a daisy, draw a center circle about 1/4 inch (6 mm) across and five to eight petal lines around it. One lazy daisy on its own makes a neat leaf.

Chain stitch

Chain stitch is a line of these loops linked together. Instead of tacking each loop, go down inside it, into the same hole you came up from, and come up one stitch length further along with the new loop under the needle. Finish with a tiny stitch over the last loop. Skinny links mean you are pulling too hard.

How to do a French knot

French knots make flower centers, eyes, berries and dots, and they become easy once you understand that tension is everything.

  1. Come up where you want the knot.
  2. Hold the working thread taut with your other hand, about 2 inches (5 cm) from the fabric.
  3. Hold the needle close to the fabric, in front of the thread, and wrap the thread around it twice.
  4. Keeping the thread taut, put the needle tip back into the fabric one or two threads away from where you came up, not into the same hole.
  5. Slide the wraps down so they sit against the fabric.
  6. Push the needle through to the back slowly, letting the thread slide through your fingers and holding it taut until the very last moment.

For a bigger knot, use more strands rather than more wraps; three or more wraps tend to topple over. Keep the fabric drum tight, since loose fabric is the most common reason knots vanish. If French knots keep failing, try a colonial knot, which uses a figure-eight wrap around the needle tip and gives a taller, firmer dot that many stitchers find more reliable.

When a piece is finished, rinse out pen marks with cool water, let it dry flat and press it face down on a thick towel so the stitches stay raised. Keep needles and small scissors in a closed tin, especially with young children or pets around.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest embroidery stitch for beginners?

Running stitch is the easiest: you simply come up and go down along a line, leaving even gaps. Backstitch is the most useful first stitch, because it gives a solid outline and works for lettering, shapes and details. Most beginners learn running stitch in minutes and feel confident with backstitch after one practice session of straight lines and gentle curves.

How many strands of embroidery floss should I use?

Two or three strands suit most beginner stitching. Use one or two strands for fine details and small lettering, three for most outlines, fills and knots, and four to six for bold lines and chain stitch. Separate the strands one at a time and lay them back together, which gives smoother stitches than pulling several out at once.

What fabric is best for beginner embroidery?

A tightly woven, medium-weight cotton or cotton-linen blend with no stretch is best. Quilting cotton is easy to find and holds stitches well. Choose white or cream for your first pieces, since designs are easier to transfer onto light fabric. Avoid stretchy knits and very thin cloth until you are comfortable using stabilizer behind them.

Why does my French knot keep pulling through?

Usually because the needle went back down into the exact hole it came up from, or the working thread went slack before the needle was through. Go down one or two fabric threads away, keep the thread taut with your other hand until the end and make sure the fabric is drum tight in the hoop.

Do you tie a knot when you start embroidery?

You can, and a small knot is fine for practice or wall hoops with a backing. For a neater, flatter back, use a waste knot that you snip off later, or a loop start when stitching with an even number of strands. On clothing and anything washed often, knot-free starts are more secure, because knots can work loose over time.

How do I transfer an embroidery design onto fabric?

For light, thin fabric, tape the printed design to a sunny window, tape the fabric over it and trace with a water-soluble pen. For dark or thick fabric, use light-colored dressmaker's transfer paper, or print the design on wash-away stabilizer, stick it on and stitch through it. Always test your pen on a scrap first.

Your first step today is to hoop a square of prewashed cotton until it thumps like a drum, draw three straight lines and two gentle curves with a water-soluble pen, thread two strands of floss and backstitch along each line. Once your stitches share holes neatly, add a stem stitch curve and one lazy daisy flower with a French knot center, and you will already have the beginnings of a sampler worth keeping.
