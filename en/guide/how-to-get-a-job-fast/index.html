How to Get a Job Fast: A Focused Plan That Gets You More Interviews

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have sent dozens of applications and heard almost nothing back, the problem is usually your method, not your worth. The fastest way to get a job is to aim at two or three closely related roles, send fewer but tailored applications, ask people you know for advice and referrals, follow up on a set schedule and prepare your interview stories in advance.

This guide turns that into a weekly routine you can start today, with resume fixes, message examples, follow-up timings and answers to the questions that trip most people up.

Why you are not getting interviews

Searches usually stall for the same few reasons:

  • Spray and pray. Sending one general resume to everything. A document that could fit any job rarely shows a strong match for one.
  • No clear target. Anything in marketing reads as unfocused to an employer.
  • A resume that fails the quick screen: duties instead of results, no numbers, missing keywords or a layout software cannot read.
  • Doing it all alone, only clicking apply on job boards.
  • No follow-up and no interview preparation.

Picture two weeks. In one you send 40 generic applications. In the other you send 8 tailored ones and message someone at three of those companies. They take about the same time, and the second week usually brings far more conversations.

So change what you measure. Count conversations, not applications. If applications rise while replies stay at zero, the method needs to change, not the volume.

Pick a clear job target

A clear target is two or three closely related roles that share most of the same skills, at a level that fits your experience. Operations coordinator or project coordinator in a logistics company is a target. Something in business is not.

A target makes every other step faster. You know which postings to read, which keywords to use, which people to contact and which stories to prepare.

Before applying to any role, run a quick fit check:

  1. Do you meet most of the core requirements? Roughly 60 to 70 percent is often enough.
  2. Are you genuinely interested in the work?
  3. Can you name at least two achievements that match what they are asking for?

If a role fails all three, skip it and spend that time on a better match.

Fix your resume or CV in an afternoon

A resume (called a CV in the UK) has one job: to get you an interview. Readers often skim it in well under a minute, so put your most relevant information first and make results easy to spot.

Use a standard structure

Contact details, a three or four line summary, skills, experience in reverse chronological order, then education. Keep it to one or two pages. Leave out a photo, date of birth and your full street address.

Turn duties into achievements

Use action verb + what you did + result. Responsible for customer complaints becomes Resolved about 25 complaints a week, raising satisfaction scores from 3.8 to 4.4 out of 5. No numbers? Look at volume, money, time, quality, people and rankings. An honest estimate is fine, but never invent a figure.

Make it readable by screening software

  • Single column, no tables, text boxes, icons or skill bars
  • Standard headings such as Summary, Skills and Experience
  • Your contact details in the main body, not the page header
  • Mirror true keywords from the job description

Quick test: paste your resume into a plain text file. If it comes out in a sensible order with nothing missing, most systems will read it fine. You can use AI tools to suggest stronger verbs or spot missing keywords, but check every line is true.

Use your network to skip the queue

Hiring is risky for employers, so they prefer candidates who come with some trust attached. A resume forwarded by a colleague usually gets read properly, and some roles are filled through contacts before a posting gets much attention.

List 30 warm contacts: former colleagues and managers, classmates, friends, neighbors and people from clubs or volunteering. Message the 10 you feel most comfortable with first. Ask for advice, not a job. For example:

Hi Daniel, it has been a while. I saw you moved teams last year, congratulations. I am looking for my next role in operations and would love to hear how you found the move. Would you be up for a quick 15 minute call in the next couple of weeks?

In those calls, ask what skills matter most and always finish with: is there anyone else you think I should talk to? When a contact works somewhere with a matching opening, make a referral easy by sending the job link, your tailored resume and two lines on why you fit, and give them a graceful way to say no.

How many jobs to apply for each week

Aim for 5 to 10 tailored applications a week if you are searching full time, or 3 to 5 if you are employed. Routes that tend to get the most replies, from strongest to weakest:

  1. A referral from someone inside the company
  2. A direct application on the employer's careers page
  3. A specialist recruiter with a real role to fill
  4. Niche job boards for your industry
  5. Large general job boards

A simple weekly rhythm keeps you moving:

DayFocus
MondayFind and shortlist new roles
TuesdayTailor and send applications
WednesdayNetworking messages and calls
ThursdayTailor and send applications
FridayFollow-ups, tracker update and review

Keep a tracker with four columns: company and role, date applied, route and contact, and status with a dated next step. Save each job description too, since postings vanish when roles close. Protect at least one full day off a week, because tired job seekers give flat answers.

How to follow up on a job application

A short, polite follow-up often moves your application back to the top of the pile.

  • After applying online: wait about a week, then send one short note if you can find the right person.
  • After a referral: tell your contact the same day, then check in after about a week.
  • After a recruiter gives a date: follow up the day after it passes, or after a week if they gave none.
  • After an interview: send a thank-you note within 24 hours, then follow up once if the decision date passes.
  • After two follow-ups with no reply: mark it inactive and move on.

Keep it to two to four sentences: who you are, which role, one line of genuine interest or a relevant update, and a friendly close. Never start with I still have not heard back.

Prepare for the toughest interview questions

Build a bank of short stories using situation, task, action and result, then practice them out loud. Most tough questions can be answered with one of them.

  • Tell me about yourself: 60 to 90 seconds using present, past, future. What you do now with one highlight, one or two past experiences that connect, and why this role.
  • Greatest weakness: a real but manageable one that is not core to the job, what you are doing about it and the progress so far.
  • Gap in your resume: brief and matter-of-fact, anything useful you did, and why you are ready now. You do not have to share private details.
  • Why are you leaving: focus on what you are moving toward and never criticize an employer. If you were laid off, say so plainly.
  • A time you failed: a real mistake with moderate stakes, with most of the answer on the fix and the lesson.
  • Salary expectations: research a range from several sources, ask for their range first and, if pressed, give a range whose bottom you would be happy with.

Rules on salary history questions vary by country, state and city, so check what applies where you live.

Negotiate the offer before you accept

Your strongest moment is after the offer and before you accept. Thank them warmly, ask for the details in writing and ask when they need an answer.

Before you reply, set three private figures from your research: a target, a walk-away point and a slightly higher opening ask. Make one clear, specific request based on the role and what you bring, not your personal costs, then stop talking. If base pay cannot move, look at the whole package: bonus, leave, flexibility, training budget, an early pay review or start date.

This is general career information, not legal advice. Do not resign from your current job until you have the final offer in writing and any conditions such as references are cleared. If a contract term is unclear, ask the employer or a qualified professional.

Career Change Guide

Before rewriting your resume for a career change, compare three real job descriptions for the same target role. Make two lists: skills you can demonstrate from previous work and requirements you still need to learn. Speak to someone who understands the role and test one small learning project before paying for a long course. Frequently asked questions

How long does it usually take to get a job?

It varies a lot by field, level, location and the job market. Some people land a role within a few weeks, while for others it takes several months. You can usually shorten it by narrowing your target, tailoring each application, using referrals and following up on a schedule. Track your conversations each week so you can see what is working and adjust quickly.

Why am I not hearing back from job applications?

Silence often comes from crowded inboxes, but if you hear nothing at all, check three things. Is your target clear? Does your resume show results with numbers and the keywords from the posting? Are you applying only through big job boards? Referrals and direct applications usually get far more replies than cold online applications, so add warm contacts to your routine.

Should I apply for jobs if I do not meet all the requirements?

Often, yes. Many job descriptions list more than the employer strictly needs. If you meet most of the core requirements, are genuinely interested and can point to at least two achievements that match what they ask for, it is usually worth applying. Use your cover letter or summary to show how your experience fits the most important parts of the role.

Is it okay to use AI to write my resume?

AI tools can help you draft faster, suggest stronger verbs and spot keywords you missed. Treat every suggestion as a draft, because these tools sometimes invent skills, numbers or qualifications. Check every line against the truth and rewrite it in your own voice. Remove personal details before pasting text into an online tool, and never claim anything you could not explain in an interview.

Should I send a thank-you email after an interview?

Yes. Send a short thank-you note within 24 hours to the people who interviewed you. Thank them for their time, mention one specific thing you discussed and briefly restate why you are interested in the role. Keep it to a few sentences. It is a small courtesy that keeps you in mind while they decide, and it gives you a natural point to follow up later.

How do I get a job fast after being laid off?

Start by telling your warm contacts, including former colleagues, that you are looking and what kind of role you want. Update your resume around four or five results with numbers, set a clear target and follow a weekly routine of tailored applications and networking. In interviews, say plainly that your role was cut in a restructure. It is common and rarely held against you.

Start today by writing down your target in one sentence, then message three warm contacts asking for a short chat about their work.
