Easiest Fruit to Grow at Home: 10 Beginner Picks and How to Start

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You would love to step outside and pick your own berries, but you are not sure what will actually grow in your yard, patio or balcony. The short answer: the easiest fruits for beginners are strawberries, fall-fruiting raspberries, blueberries in pots, currants, gooseberries, rhubarb, figs and a self-fertile apple or plum on a dwarfing rootstock, planted in a spot with at least 6 hours of direct sun.

Strawberries and fall raspberries can crop in their first year, while bushes and trees take 2 to 5 years, so mixing quick and slow crops keeps you picking. This guide covers how to choose, where to plant, what grows in pots, how to plant well and the simple care that matters most.

Start with fruit your household loves to eat, then think about value. Raspberries, ripe figs, currants and blueberries are expensive or hard to buy fresh, so they repay your space well. For a first season, two or three crops is plenty. Teresa, a beginner with a sunny 10 by 6 foot patio, chose two potted blueberries, a trough of strawberries and a dwarf apple trained on her fence, and by her second summer she was picking from June to October.

Check sun, climate and soil first

Fruit is made from sunlight, so sun is the first thing to check. Most fruit needs at least 6 hours of direct sun a day in the growing season, and 8 or more is better. Watch your yard every two hours on a clear day and shade the shadowy parts on a quick sketch. Remember that spring shadows are longer than midsummer ones, just when blossom opens.

Next, climate. In the US, find your USDA hardiness zone; Canada, the UK, Australia and New Zealand have their own rating systems, and plant labels list what each fruit suits. Many fruit trees, blueberries included, also need a certain amount of winter cold, called chill hours, to flower well. In mild-winter areas, choose low-chill varieties. Your local nursery or extension service (regional agricultural advice service) will tell you which varieties crop reliably near you.

Then soil. Dig a hole about 12 inches (30 cm) deep, fill it with water, refill it the next day and time it. If it drains within a few hours, you are fine. If water still stands after a day, use raised beds or pots. Most fruit likes a pH of about 6 to 7, while blueberries need acid soil of about pH 4.5 to 5.5.

Cold air sinks and collects in low spots, called frost pockets. Keep early-flowering trees such as peaches, apricots and some plums out of them, and use those spots for currants or rhubarb instead.

Strawberries, the quickest win

Strawberries fruit within months of planting and fit beds, troughs, pots and hanging baskets. Choose summer-fruiting (June-bearing) types for one big crop over 2 to 3 weeks, or everbearing (perpetual) types for smaller pickings until the first frosts. A mix of both works well.

  • Plant 12 to 18 inches (30 to 45 cm) apart in full sun and well-drained soil.
  • Set the crown exactly level with the soil. Buried crowns rot; crowns set too high dry out.
  • Avoid spots where strawberries, potatoes, tomatoes, peppers or eggplants (aubergines) grew in the last 3 or 4 years.
  • Water at the base in the morning and tuck clean straw under the plants as fruit forms.
  • Feed with a high-potassium liquid feed, like tomato feed, every 1 to 2 weeks once flowers appear.

A 12 inch (30 cm) pot holds 3 plants. Net against birds as berries color, collect slugs after dark and replace plants every 3 or 4 years in a fresh spot.

Easy berry bushes and canes

Raspberries

Raspberry canes live for two years, which makes pruning simple once you know your type. Summer raspberries fruit on last year's canes, so cut the fruited canes out after harvest. Fall (autumn) raspberries fruit on this year's canes, so you cut every cane to the ground in late winter. Fall types are the easiest for beginners and crop in their first year.

Blueberries

Blueberries need acid soil, so in most gardens they are easiest in a pot at least 16 inches (40 cm) across, filled with ericaceous (acid) compost and watered with rainwater where tap water is hard. Plant two varieties of the same type for bigger crops.

Currants and gooseberries

These cope with 4 to 5 hours of sun, which makes them useful for shadier corners. Each winter, remove about a third of the oldest blackcurrant stems. Grow redcurrants and gooseberries as open goblet shapes and check gooseberries weekly in spring for sawfly larvae stripping the leaves. Net all bush fruit against birds.

Easy fruit trees for small gardens

The secret to fruit trees in small spaces is the rootstock. Almost all fruit trees are grafted onto roots that control their size. Dwarfing rootstocks give small trees that fruit in 2 to 3 years and suit small gardens and pots, but they need a stake for life. Vigorous rootstocks give big trees that may take 5 years or more.

Pollination is the other thing to check. Most apples and pears need a partner variety that flowers at the same time within about 50 to 100 feet (15 to 30 m); a neighbor's tree or a crabapple often does the job. If you have room for only one tree, buy a self-fertile variety. Many European plums, sour cherries, most peaches and common figs crop on their own.

  • Apples and pears: prune in winter, and thin fruit after the early summer drop.
  • Plums and cherries: prune in spring or summer in dry weather, never in winter, to reduce disease.
  • Figs: self-fertile and happiest with restricted roots, which is why they do so well in pots. Wear gloves when pruning, because the milky sap can irritate skin and burn in sunlight.

Sunny walls and fences can hold trees trained flat as fans, cordons or espaliers, which take up only a foot or so of depth.

Bigger pots dry out more slowly, so they are easier. Every pot needs drainage holes. Use a soil-based potting mix for trees and bushes, and stand pots on small feet so water drains freely.

Watering is the biggest job. Push a finger into the mix and water until it runs out of the bottom when the top inch (2 to 3 cm) is dry, which can mean every day in hot weather. From spring to late summer, give a high-potassium liquid feed every 1 to 2 weeks. Each spring, replace the top 2 inches (5 cm) of mix with fresh mix. Citrus such as lemons, limes and kumquats must come indoors to a bright, cool, frost-free spot before the first frost.

How to plant a fruit tree or bush

Bare-root plants are cheaper and go in during the dormant season (winter). Container plants can go in most of the year if you keep them watered.

  1. Soak bare-root roots in water for about 20 minutes, or water a potted plant an hour before.
  2. Dig a hole wide enough to spread the roots out, and no deeper than the root system.
  3. If the tree needs a stake, drive it in before planting, on the side the wind usually comes from.
  4. Set the tree so the graft union, the knobbly bump near the base, sits at least 2 inches (5 cm) above the final soil level.
  5. Backfill, firm gently and water well.
  6. Tie the tree to the stake with a soft, flexible tie and check it each spring.
  7. Spread a 2 to 3 inch (5 to 8 cm) layer of compost or wood chip mulch in a circle about 3 feet (1 m) across, keeping it a hand's width from the trunk.

Never bury the graft union. If soil covers it, the fruiting variety can grow its own roots and lose the size control of the rootstock. In the first dry season, water deeply about once a week rather than a little every day.

Simple care that keeps fruit healthy

Most fruit problems are prevented rather than cured. A weekly walk around your plants catches trouble early.

  • Birds: put taut, fine netting over a frame as fruit starts to color, and check it daily so nothing gets trapped.
  • Aphids: squash them or blast them off with a jet of water before reaching for insecticidal soap.
  • Slugs on strawberries: straw mulch, night patrols and raised containers.
  • Fungal problems: space plants for airflow, water at the base, clear fallen leaves and fruit and remove moldy berries straight away.

When pruning, start with the 3 Ds: dead, damaged and diseased wood. Use sharp pruners and clean them between plants. Feed with a balanced fertilizer in early spring and favor potassium over nitrogen, since too much nitrogen gives lots of leaves and little fruit.

Rhubarb is an easy extra: plant crowns about 3 feet (1 m) apart, leave them unpicked in the first year and then pull no more than a third to a half of the stalks at once. Its leaves are poisonous to people and pets, so eat only the stalks. Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest fruit to grow at home?

Strawberries are the fastest, fruiting within months of planting, often in their first summer. Fall-fruiting raspberries usually crop in their first year too, because they fruit on canes that grow that same season. Summer raspberries, currants and gooseberries crop from year two, while most fruit trees take 2 to 5 years, depending on the rootstock and type of fruit.

Can I grow fruit trees in pots?

Yes. Choose trees on very dwarfing rootstocks, columnar apples, common figs or citrus on dwarfing rootstocks. Start in a pot about 12 to 16 inches (30 to 40 cm) across and move up a size every couple of years to 18 to 24 inches (45 to 60 cm). Use soil-based potting mix, water faithfully and feed regularly in the growing season.

What fruit grows well in shade?

No fruit crops well in deep shade, but a few cope with about 4 to 5 hours of direct sun. Currants, gooseberries, rhubarb and sour cooking cherries are the best choices for the shadier side of a yard, and alpine strawberries tolerate part shade. Expect smaller crops than in full sun, and keep the sunniest spots for apples, plums, figs and strawberries.

Do I need two fruit trees to get fruit?

It depends on the fruit. Most apples and pears and many sweet cherries need a partner variety that flowers at the same time nearby, though a neighbor's tree or a crabapple often works. Many European plums, sour cherries, most peaches, common figs and most citrus are self-fertile. If you have room for only one tree, buy a self-fertile variety.

When is the best time to plant fruit trees?

Bare-root trees and bushes are planted in the dormant season, roughly late fall to late winter, when they are leafless. Container-grown plants can go in most of the year as long as the ground is not frozen or baking and you water them well. Remember that seasons are reversed in the southern hemisphere, so follow the season rather than the month.

Why is my fruit tree not producing fruit?

Common causes are that the tree is still too young, it lacks a pollination partner, a late frost hit the blossom, it is in too much shade or it has had too much nitrogen fertilizer or hard pruning. Be patient with young trees, add a partner variety if needed, feed potassium rather than nitrogen and prune lightly.

Your first step today is to make a simple sun map: sketch your yard, patio or balcony and check it every two hours on the next clear day to find the spots with 6 or more hours of direct sun. Then write down the two or three fruits your household loves most, match them to those sunny spots or to a big pot, and ask a local nursery which varieties crop well where you live before you buy.
