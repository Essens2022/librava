How to Make Candles at Home: An Easy Step-by-Step Soy Candle Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have probably stood in a shop holding a pricey scented candle and thought you could make one yourself. You can. To make a candle at home, you melt container wax in a double boiler, stir in candle fragrance at about 6 to 10 percent of the wax weight, let it cool to the pour temperature, and pour it into a heat-safe jar with a centered wick.

The details are what make it look and smell professional, and most of them are about patience and a thermometer rather than skill. This guide takes you through a first soy container candle from start to finish, then shows you how to fix the problems nearly everyone meets on batch one.

What you need to make candles at home

You do not need a studio. A kitchen table, a covered surface and a short list of supplies will do.

  • Container wax, such as soy or a coconut blend, plus the supplier's data sheet
  • A heat-safe glass jar or candle tin, clean, dry and free of cracks
  • Pre-tabbed wicks sized for your container, and wick stickers
  • A wick holder or centering bar (two clothespins, or clothes pegs, work too)
  • A metal pouring pitcher and a saucepan to make a double boiler
  • A thermometer and a digital kitchen scale
  • Fragrance oil made for candles (optional for a first try)
  • Paper towels (kitchen roll), oven mitts and a metal lid
  • A fire extinguisher or fire blanket within reach

Avoid thin glass, decorative drinking glasses with cracks or chips, and anything not made to handle heat. A straight-sided jar about 3 inches (7.5 cm) across is the easiest shape for a first candle.

Which wax is best for beginners

For your first container candles, choose a single soy container wax or a coconut blend. Both are forgiving and clean up easily with warm, soapy water once the wax is scraped off.

WaxBest forWhat to expect
SoyJars and tinsCreamy look, beginner friendly, may frost
Coconut blendJars and tinsSmooth, good scent, costs more
BeeswaxPillars, tapers, rolled candlesGolden, needs a larger wick
ParaffinJars and pillarsStrong scent and bright color, many grades
Rapeseed (canola)Jars and tinsSimilar to soy

Container wax is soft and made to stick to glass. Pillar wax is harder so it can stand on its own. Using the wrong type is a common reason first candles crack or slump, so check the label says container wax.

How to make a soy candle step by step

Read all the steps once before you start, then set every item out before you turn on the stove.

  1. Measure your wax. Put the empty jar on the scale, press tare and fill it with water to about half an inch (1 cm) below the rim. Multiply the water weight by about 0.86 to get the wax weight. A jar that holds 240 g of water needs roughly 206 g of wax, so melt about 220 g to allow for a little left in the pitcher. Dry the jar completely.
  2. Prepare the jar and wick. Make sure the jar is at room temperature, warming it with a hair dryer if your kitchen is chilly. Stick the wick tab to the exact center of the base and hold the wick upright with your holder.
  3. Melt the wax. Put the wax in the pouring pitcher, set it in a saucepan with a few inches of water and heat on low to medium. Stir now and then and watch the thermometer.
  4. Add fragrance. When the wax reaches your supplier's fragrance temperature (often around 185°F or 85°C for soy), take it off the heat and stir in the weighed fragrance slowly for about two minutes.
  5. Cool to pouring temperature. For soy container wax this is often 120 to 140°F (50 to 60°C). Check the data sheet and write down the number you use.
  6. Pour. Pour slowly in one smooth stream, aiming near the wick, and keep a tablespoon or two back for a top-up. Recenter the wick if it has drifted.
  7. Let it set and cure. Leave it untouched overnight at steady room temperature, away from drafts. Then let it cure for 1 to 2 weeks so the scent reaches full strength.
  8. Trim and burn test. Trim the wick to about 1/4 inch (6 mm) and do the first burn as described below.
Never leave melting wax unattended and never heat it directly over high heat. If wax catches fire, turn off the heat and cover the pitcher with a metal lid or baking tray. Never use water on a wax fire, because it turns to steam and throws burning wax into the air.

How much fragrance oil to add to a candle

Fragrance is measured by weight, not by drops or spoonfuls. A typical fragrance load is about 6 to 10 percent of the wax weight, and you should never go above the maximum your wax manufacturer lists. More oil does not always mean more scent, and too much can cause sooting, sweating or a fragrance that pools on top.

For a first candle, 8 percent is a sensible middle point if your wax allows it. The sum is simple:

Use only fragrance oils made for candles or essential oils that have been properly tested for candle use. Wear gloves when you pour fragrance and wash any splashes off your skin promptly. Candle dyes made for wax are the safe way to add color. Crayons, food coloring and cosmetics do not dissolve well and can clog the wick.

Keep a notebook. Write down the wax, jar, wick, fragrance, load, fragrance temperature and pour temperature for every batch, so a candle you love can be made again.

How to choose the right wick size

The wick decides how your candle burns, and the right size depends on three things: the inside diameter of the container, the type of wax and how much fragrance or dye you used. Diameter matters most, because the flame has to melt wax all the way to the edge.

Inside diameterWicks usually needed
Under 2 in (5 cm)One small
2 to 3 in (5 to 7.5 cm)One small to medium
3 to 4 in (7.5 to 10 cm)One medium to large
Over 4 in (10 cm)Two or more

Your supplier's wick chart is a starting point, not a final answer. When a jar falls between two sizes, make one test candle with each. Buying small packs of two or three neighboring sizes and testing them side by side tells you far more than testing one and hoping.

How to burn test a homemade candle

A burn test shows whether your wick, wax and fragrance work together before you give a candle away. Place it on a heat-safe surface away from drafts, curtains, children and pets, and stay in the room.

  • Let the first burn continue until the melt pool reaches the edge of the jar, about 1 hour per inch of diameter. A 3-inch jar needs around three hours.
  • Note how long it takes to reach a full melt pool and how tall the flame gets.
  • Trim the wick to about 1/4 inch (6 mm) before every later burn.
  • Watch for black smoke, a flickering or very tall flame, or a jar that gets very hot.

A good result is a calm, steady flame, a full melt pool within the expected time and very little smoke. If the pool never reaches the edge, try the next wick size up. If the flame is tall and smoky, go down a size.

Common candle making problems and fixes

Your first candle may not be perfect, and that is normal. Change only one thing at a time next batch, such as pour temperature or jar warmth, so you know what made the difference.

Tunneling

The candle burns a hole down the middle and leaves a wall of wax. The usual causes are a first burn that was too short or a wick that is too small. For a mild tunnel, wrap a collar of aluminum foil (aluminium in the UK) around the top of the jar with a wide opening over the flame, stay with it, and remove the hot foil with an oven glove once the wax is level.

Sinkholes

A dip near the wick forms because wax shrinks as it cools. Smooth a shallow one with a heat gun on low, or pour a thin top layer of reserved wax. Avoid pouring too hot and never cool fresh candles in the refrigerator.

Wet spots and frosting

Wet spots are patches where wax has pulled away from the glass, and frosting is a white crystal bloom common on soy. Both are cosmetic. Clean, dry, slightly warm jars and slow cooling away from fans help with each.

Weak scent

Check cure time first, since many beginners test too soon. Then check that fragrance went in at the right temperature, that you weighed it, and that the melt pool is wide enough.

Soot and black smoke

This usually means an untrimmed or oversized wick, too much fragrance or a draft. Trim before every burn, move the candle and resize the wick if the flame stays too large.

Safe workspace habits for candle making

Hot wax behaves like hot cooking oil, so a calm routine matters more than any special gear. Before every pour, run through a short check.

  • Cover the work surface and clear away paper, cloths and anything flammable.
  • Keep children and pets out of the room while wax is hot.
  • Have the metal lid, oven mitts and a fire extinguisher or fire blanket in reach, and know how to use them.
  • If you must answer the door, turn off the heat first.
  • Never pour leftover wax down the drain. Let it harden in the pitcher, warm the outside and wipe it clean.
  • Store wax, fragrance and wicks somewhere cool and dry, out of reach of children.

Frequently asked questions

What do I need to make candles for the first time?

You need container wax, a heat-safe jar, a pre-tabbed wick and wick sticker, a wick holder, a pouring pitcher in a saucepan as a double boiler, a thermometer and a kitchen scale. Fragrance oil made for candles is optional. Keep a metal lid and a fire extinguisher or fire blanket nearby every time you melt wax.

How much fragrance oil do you put in a candle?

Most candles use about 6 to 10 percent fragrance by weight of the wax, and you should never exceed the wax manufacturer's maximum. For a first candle, 8 percent is a good starting point. With 220 g of wax, that is about 18 g of fragrance oil. Always weigh it on a scale rather than measuring by drops or spoons.

What temperature do you pour soy wax?

Many soy container waxes pour well somewhere between about 120 and 140°F (50 and 60°C), with fragrance added at around 185°F (85°C). Every wax is a little different, so follow your supplier's data sheet and write down the exact temperatures you use. Pouring too hot can cause sinkholes, and too cool can leave rough tops.

How long should homemade candles cure before burning?

Let the candle set overnight, then give it time to cure so the wax and fragrance fully bond. Many soy candles smell best after one to two weeks. You can safely burn a candle sooner, but you will not get a fair sense of the scent, so wait before judging how strong your fragrance is.

Why is my homemade candle tunneling?

Tunneling usually comes from a first burn that was too short or a wick that is too small for the jar. Wax tends to follow the melt pool of its first burn, so let it burn about one hour per inch of diameter until the pool reaches the edges. If it still tunnels after a proper first burn, test the next wick size up.

Is making candles at home safe?

It is safe when you follow a few firm rules. Melt wax in a double boiler, never directly over high heat, use a thermometer and never leave wax unattended. Use only heat-safe containers and candle-grade fragrance. If wax catches fire, turn off the heat and cover it with a metal lid, and never use water.

Your first step today is to choose one straight-sided heat-safe jar, weigh it full of water to work out your wax amount, and order a small amount of soy container wax with two or three neighboring wick sizes. One careful test batch will teach you more than any amount of reading.
