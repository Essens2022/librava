How to Fix a Running Toilet: Find the Cause and Fix It in 30 Minutes

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

That quiet hiss from the bathroom, or the tank that refills by itself in the middle of the night, is one of the most common small problems in any home. In most cases a running toilet has one of two causes: a worn rubber seal (the flapper) letting water leak into the bowl, or a fill valve set too high so water spills into the overflow tube. Both are cheap, beginner-friendly fixes that usually take 20 to 30 minutes.

This guide shows you how a toilet works, a simple test to find which problem you have, and step-by-step fixes for each, including notes for UK cisterns. It also tells you clearly when to stop and call a plumber.

How a toilet tank works

Lift the lid off the tank (the cistern in the UK) and you can see almost everything that matters. When you press the handle or button, a seal at the bottom lifts and lets the stored water rush into the bowl. Once the tank empties, that seal drops back into place. A fill valve then refills the tank until a float rises high enough to tell it to stop.

In most US toilets the seal is a rubber flap called a flapper, joined to the handle by a small chain. Many UK toilets use a push-button flush valve instead, a plastic tower with a rubber washer at its base, and older UK cisterns use a siphon.

Whatever the design, a running toilet comes down to the same two faults:

  • Water is leaking past a tired seal at the bottom of the tank.
  • The fill valve is not shutting off at the right level, so water flows down the overflow.

Running toilets are worth fixing quickly. A steady leak can waste a startling amount of water and add to your bill, even when you can barely hear it.

What you need and how to turn off the water

You do not need much for this job:

  • Rubber gloves and an old towel
  • A sponge and a small bucket
  • Food coloring
  • A flat screwdriver and an adjustable wrench
  • A replacement flapper, washer or fill valve, once you know which part you need

Before you touch anything inside the tank, find the small shutoff valve on the pipe behind or beside the toilet. In the UK this is often called an isolation valve. Turn a handle clockwise until it stops. On a UK valve with a slot, turn the slot a quarter turn with a flat screwdriver so it sits across the pipe. Then flush once to empty the tank.

If the shutoff valve is stuck or will not turn with moderate hand pressure, do not force it with a big wrench. Old valves can snap or start leaking at the stem. Use your home's main shutoff instead (the stopcock or stop tap in the UK), and call a plumber about the stuck valve. Renters should report the problem to their landlord and ask before replacing any parts.

Put the tank lid on a folded towel on the floor. A cracked lid is the most common casualty of toilet repairs, and porcelain is heavier and more fragile than it looks.

Find out why your toilet keeps running

A two-minute look tells you which fix you need, so you do not buy the wrong part.

  1. Wait for the tank to fill completely after a flush.
  2. Look at the open overflow tube in the middle of the tank (in most US toilets).
  3. If water is spilling over the top of that tube, the fill valve is set too high or is faulty.
  4. If the water sits below the top of the tube but the toilet still runs or refills on its own every so often, the seal at the bottom is leaking.

The food coloring test

To confirm a leaking seal, add a few drops of food coloring to the water in the tank and do not flush for 30 minutes. If color appears in the bowl, water is creeping past the flapper or flush valve washer. A good time to do this is last thing at night, checking the bowl in the morning before anyone flushes.

A toilet that refills by itself for a few seconds every so often (sometimes called a phantom flush) is almost always a slow seal leak, not a ghost.

Check the chain and clean the flapper

Before buying any parts, check the simple things. Sometimes the fix costs nothing.

  • The chain. If the chain from the handle to the flapper is too short, it holds the flapper slightly open. If it is too long, it can get trapped under the flapper. You want about half an inch (12 mm) of slack when the flapper is closed.
  • The handle. If the handle sticks down after a flush, the flapper may not drop properly. Check that the nut holding the handle inside the tank is snug, not overtightened.
  • The rim. Wipe the rim of the drain opening that the flapper sits on with a cloth or soft scourer. Grime and mineral scale stop the seal from sitting flat.

Now run a gloved finger around the flapper itself. If your finger comes away black, or the rubber feels stiff, warped or slimy, it needs replacing. Flappers wear out over the years, especially in hard water areas, and a soft, misshapen one will never seal well again.

How to replace a toilet flapper

This is the most common running toilet fix, and one of the best first repairs for any beginner. Flappers usually come in 2 inch and 3 inch sizes, so take the old one to the store to match it, or check the size printed on the toilet if you can find it.

  1. Turn off the water at the toilet shutoff valve and flush to empty the tank.
  2. Unhook the chain from the handle arm.
  3. Slide the flapper's ears off the two pegs on the sides of the overflow tube, or unclip the ring if your flapper has one.
  4. Wipe the rim of the drain opening clean.
  5. Fit the new flapper the same way the old one came off.
  6. Reattach the chain with about half an inch (12 mm) of slack when the flapper is closed.
  7. Turn the water back on, let the tank fill and flush two or three times.

You are done when the tank refills once, stops and stays quiet. Repeat the food coloring test after 30 minutes to make sure no color reaches the bowl.

If the new flapper still leaks, check that it is sitting straight and that the chain is not catching. A cracked or rough drain rim can also stop it sealing, and that is a job for a plumber.

How to adjust or replace the fill valve

If water is running into the overflow tube, start by lowering the water level. The aim is a level about 1 inch (25 mm) below the top of the overflow tube, or at the water line marked inside the tank.

  • Modern float cup valve. The float rides up and down the valve body. Look for a plastic screw or clip that lets you lower it, then let the tank refill and check the level.
  • Older ball float on an arm. Gently bend the arm down slightly, or turn the adjusting screw where the arm meets the valve. Make small changes and test each one.

If adjusting does not help, the valve's internal seal is probably worn. Fill valves are designed to be replaced by homeowners. Before you buy, photograph your valve and note whether the water pipe enters from the bottom or the side of the tank, as this affects which replacement fits.

To replace it, turn off the water, flush, and soak up the last water in the tank with a sponge. Put a towel and bucket under the tank, then disconnect the supply pipe and undo the nut under the tank that holds the old valve. Fit the new one, set its height as the packet says, and hand-tighten the connections before a gentle extra turn. Always follow the instructions that come with your new valve over any general advice, then turn the water on slowly and check underneath for drips.

Fixing a running cistern in UK toilets

Many UK toilets have a push-button flush instead of a handle and flapper, but the idea is the same. If water trickles into the pan all the time, the rubber washer at the base of the flush valve is usually worn.

On many designs you can turn off the water, flush, then twist the valve tower out of its base, swap the rubber washer and twist it back in. Take the old washer with you to buy a matching one. Other designs require taking the cistern off the pan, which is a bigger job.

Older UK cisterns with a lever often use a siphon, and the fill valve is a float valve on an arm. You can adjust the water level in the same way as above. If the siphon itself needs removing, that is often the point where a plumber is the better call.

When to call a plumber

A running toilet is a beginner job, but some toilet problems are not. Call a professional if you notice any of these:

  • Water leaking around the base of the toilet, a sewage smell or stains on the ceiling below
  • A crack in the tank or bowl, even a hairline one
  • A shutoff valve that will not turn or leaks itself
  • More than one sink, tub or toilet draining slowly or gurgling at the same time
  • Sewage backing up into a tub, shower or toilet, or water coming through a ceiling (call right away)

A toilet that rocks on the floor deserves attention too. If it moves and there is any sign of leaking, the seal under the toilet may have failed, and lifting and resetting a heavy toilet is best left to a plumber. A seat that slides around is different: just tighten its two bolts until snug, and stop there, because porcelain cracks if you overdo it.

Never pour chemical drain cleaner into a toilet. It rarely clears a toilet clog, can damage the rubber seals and makes plunging or a plumber's visit risky because of splashes.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my toilet keep running after I flush?

Usually because the seal at the bottom of the tank is not closing properly or the fill valve is set too high. Look inside the tank: if water spills into the overflow tube, lower the fill valve. If not, the flapper or flush valve washer is probably worn or held open by a chain that is too short. Both are inexpensive parts you can fix yourself.

Why does my toilet run for a few seconds by itself?

A toilet that briefly refills on its own every so often has a slow leak from the tank into the bowl. Water seeps past the flapper or flush valve washer, the level drops and the fill valve tops it up. Do the food coloring test to confirm, then clean the rim the seal sits on or replace the flapper or washer.

How do I know if my toilet flapper is bad?

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 30 minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. With the water off, run a gloved finger around it. If the rubber feels stiff, warped or slimy, or leaves black residue on your finger, replace it with one of the same size.

Is a running toilet an emergency?

Usually not, but it is worth fixing soon because it wastes water and can raise your bill. If the toilet is overflowing onto the floor, turn off the shutoff valve behind it straight away. It becomes urgent if water leaks from the base, comes through a ceiling below or sewage backs up, and then you should call a plumber.

Can I fix a running toilet without turning off the water?

You can adjust the float and the chain with the water on, since you are only changing settings inside the tank. To replace a flapper, washer or fill valve, turn off the toilet's shutoff valve and flush to empty the tank first. If the valve is stuck, use the main shutoff for the house rather than forcing it.

How long does a toilet flapper last?

It varies with water quality and the cleaning products used, but flappers are rubber and wear out over the years. Hard water and strong chemicals in the tank can make them stiffen or warp sooner. If your toilet starts running or refilling by itself, check the flapper first, since it is the most common cause and the cheapest part to replace.

Your first step today is the simplest one: put a few drops of food coloring in each toilet tank tonight and check the bowls in the morning before anyone flushes. If you see color, you have a worn seal and a 20-minute fix ahead of you. If not, look at the overflow tube next time the tank fills, and you will know whether the fill valve needs adjusting.
