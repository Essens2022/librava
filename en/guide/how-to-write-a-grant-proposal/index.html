How to Write a Grant Proposal: A Clear Step-by-Step Beginner Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have been asked to write a grant for your charity, community group or club and you are staring at a blank page, you are in good company. To write a grant proposal, you pick a funder whose priorities truly match your project, read their guidelines closely, plan the project first, then answer their questions in their order with clear evidence of need, measurable outcomes and a budget that matches your story.

Clever writing matters far less than fit, honesty and clarity. This guide takes you through each stage, with a sample needs statement, a budget layout and the checks experienced writers use before pressing submit. Rejection is a normal part of the process, so treat each application as practice for the next.

The grant writing process in eight steps

Most successful proposals follow the same path, whatever the size of the grant.

  1. Get grant ready: confirm your legal status, accounts and basic documents.
  2. Find funders that fit your mission, area and size.
  3. Read the guidelines and scoring criteria line by line.
  4. Plan the project: problem, activities, people, timeline and rough costs.
  5. Write the needs statement with local evidence.
  6. Set outcomes and a simple evaluation plan.
  7. Build the budget and budget narrative.
  8. Draft, edit, check attachments and submit early.

Notice that writing the full proposal comes near the end. Proposals go wrong most often because a group skipped the planning and applied to a funder that was never going to fund that kind of work.

Get grant ready before you write

Funders need to trust that your group can handle money and deliver what it promises. Before you apply anywhere, check that you have the basics in place.

  • Legal status that the funder accepts, such as a registered charity or nonprofit. If your group is not registered, you may be able to apply through a fiscal sponsor (sometimes called a host or umbrella organization) that receives the grant on your behalf under a written agreement.
  • A governing document, a board or committee, and minutes of meetings.
  • Simple, up-to-date financial records and your most recent accounts.
  • A separate bank account in the organization's name.
  • Key policies a funder may ask for, such as safeguarding for work with children or vulnerable adults.
  • A one- or two-sentence mission statement.

Then start a boilerplate bank: a document of reusable, regularly updated text about your organization, history, the people you serve, past results and team. It saves hours on every application, though each piece still needs tailoring to the funder.

Find funders that fit your project

Start close to home and widen out. Local government community grants, your nearest community foundation, local trusts, businesses with community giving programs and funder databases (often free through public libraries) are all good starting points. Ask similar groups who has funded them, and read funders' annual reports and lists of past grants to see what they really support.

Run every prospect through a quick fit test before you spend time on it.

AreaQuestion to ask
EligibilityDo we meet their rules on legal status and type of group?
GeographyDo they fund in our area?
MissionDoes our work match their current priorities?
SizeIs our request within their usual grant range?
CostsWill they pay for the kind of costs we need?
TimingDo the deadline and decision date suit our project?

Eligibility and geography must be a clear yes, or move on. A small local trust that funds exactly what you do is worth far more than a large foundation that never will. Keep your prospects in a simple tracking sheet with deadlines, amounts and notes.

Read the funder guidelines like a checklist

Guidelines tell you exactly what the funder wants, and reviewers score against them. Print them or copy them into a document, then mark up:

  • eligibility rules and anything the funder will not pay for
  • stated priorities, and the exact words they use to describe them
  • the deadline, including the time and time zone
  • word or character limits for every answer
  • required attachments, such as accounts, policies or letters of support
  • scoring criteria, if published
  • whether they want a short letter of inquiry first

If something is genuinely unclear, contact the program officer or grants officer with a short, specific question, such as whether your project's costs fall within their capital grants. Many funders welcome this, and it saves both sides time.

How to write a needs statement that convinces

The needs statement explains why your project should exist, and reviewers often read it most closely. A strong one answers four questions: what the problem is, who is affected and where, how you know, and why your group is well placed to respond.

Local evidence beats national statistics. Combine public data from your local council or statistics office, your own records such as waiting lists or turned-away enquiries, a short survey of the people you serve and, with written consent, a brief quote. Note the source and date of every figure.

Describe people with respect. "Young people facing challenges at home or school" reads better than "at-risk youth," and many funders look for this approach. Avoid crisis language, inflated numbers and claims that you are the only group doing this work unless you have checked.

Here is the difference in practice. A weak version says children in the area are struggling and the club desperately needs funding. A stronger version says that in a survey of 35 parents last spring, 22 said their child had no quiet place for homework, and that the pilot club's two weekly afternoons had a waiting list of 15 by week four. End with a bridge to your project: what you will do about it.

Stories belong to the people who tell them. Ask permission in writing, let people choose how they are named, remove identifying details, and get consent from a parent or guardian for children. If anyone could be harmed by being recognized, leave the story out.

Set outcomes and objectives you can measure

Outputs count what you did. Outcomes describe what changed for people. Funders want both, and many proposals list plenty of outputs with very few outcomes.

OutputOutcome
40 cooking sessions heldFamilies cook more meals at home
12 volunteers trainedVolunteers gain skills that help them find work
200 meals servedOlder residents feel less isolated

Turn each outcome into a SMART objective: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. Instead of "help young people become more active," write "by the end of the 12-month project, at least 30 young people aged 11 to 16 will attend weekly sessions for at least 20 weeks, and at least two thirds will report being more active than when they started."

Base targets on your past results plus a sensible increase. Small projects rarely need complex evaluation. Sign-in sheets, short before and after surveys, observation notes and consented feedback are usually enough, as long as you say who collects them and when.

Build a grant budget that matches your story

Every activity in your proposal should appear in the budget, and every budget line should be explained in the narrative. Here is a simplified example for a one-year family cooking project, using round illustrative figures in no particular currency.

Label other funding as confirmed or pending, and include in-kind support such as donated space or volunteer time if the funder allows it. Include a fair share of overheads where permitted, because projects cannot run without rent, insurance and bookkeeping.

The most common budget mistake is totals that do not add up. Use a spreadsheet with formulas, check the totals by hand, and make sure every figure matches the summary and the form exactly. Ask your treasurer or bookkeeper to review it separately.

Put the proposal together and submit

Most full proposals include a summary, organization background, need, project description, outcomes and evaluation, budget and sustainability. Always follow the funder's order and headings, and answer every part of every question.

Draft the project description first, because it is the heart of the proposal, and write the summary last. A reviewer should be able to picture the project exactly: "20 two-hour Saturday workshops at the community center, each led by a local artist and a trained volunteer, for up to 15 young people aged 12 to 16" tells them far more than "engaging workshops." Use short sentences and everyday words, and echo the funder's phrases only where they honestly describe your work.

Then edit in rounds, each with one focus:

  1. Does it answer every question in order and cover the scoring criteria?
  2. Do dates, numbers and amounts match everywhere?
  3. Can you trace every statistic, and is consent recorded for every story?
  4. Is it clear, correct and within every word limit?

Ask someone outside the project to read it and explain it back in two sentences. Check every attachment against the guidelines, and submit a few days early, since portals slow down near deadlines. What to do after you hear back

If the answer is yes, read the grant agreement carefully before signing, note the payment schedule and reporting dates, and track spending and results from day one. Grant money is usually restricted to the purpose agreed, so ask in writing before changing how you use it.

If the answer is no, thank the funder and politely ask for feedback. A no often reflects competition or timing rather than a poor proposal. Use what you learn, update your boilerplate bank and look at whether that funder would welcome a revised application in a later round. Build relationships over time by sending short updates, and avoid relying on a single funder for most of your income.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a grant proposal be?

As long as the funder allows and no longer. Many small community grants use online forms with short answer boxes, sometimes only a few hundred words or characters each, while larger foundations may ask for several pages plus attachments. Always follow the stated word, character and page limits exactly. A focused answer that addresses every part of the question beats a long one.

What are the main parts of a grant proposal?

Most proposals include a summary, background on your organization, a statement of need, a project description, outcomes and evaluation, a budget with a budget narrative, and a section on sustainability after the grant ends. Some funders also want a cover letter and attachments such as accounts or letters of support. Always use the funder's own order and headings.

Can an individual or unregistered group apply for a grant?

Sometimes. Some funders offer grants to individuals, such as artists, and some small local schemes accept informal community groups. Many foundations, though, fund only registered charities or nonprofits. If you are not registered, look for funders that accept unregistered groups or ask an established organization to act as your fiscal sponsor under a clear written agreement.

Why do grant proposals get rejected?

The most common reasons are poor fit with the funder's priorities, missing eligibility rules, not answering the question asked, vague outcomes, budgets that do not add up or do not match the narrative, and missing attachments. Strong proposals are also turned down simply because funders receive more good applications than they can support. Ask for feedback and apply what you learn.

How long does it take to write a grant proposal?

A short local application might take a few days of work once your basics are ready, while a large foundation proposal can take several weeks, especially if you need partner letters or board sign-off. Start as early as you can, aim to finish a draft at least a week before the deadline, and leave time for a fresh reader and attachments.

Can I use writing tools or AI to help write a grant?

They can help you get unstuck or tidy wording, but check every fact yourself, follow any funder rules on their use, and never paste in confidential details about participants. Rewrite the result so it sounds like your organization, with your real numbers and examples. Reviewers often recognize generic text, and you remain responsible for every claim in the proposal.

Your first step today is to write one paragraph describing the problem your project solves, with one local figure and its source, then list three funders that fund in your area. Run each through the fit test above. You will finish the day knowing who to write for and what to say first, which is most of the battle.
