How to Start a Short-Term Rental: A Legal, Calm, Step-by-Step Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have a spare room, an annex or a second home sitting empty, hosting travelers can feel like an obvious next step. To start a short-term rental, check your local rules and get every permission in writing first, tell your insurer and your tax authority, make the space safe and comfortable, price it from your real costs, and set up a simple system for messages, cleaning and reviews.

The order matters. Plenty of new hosts buy bedding and take photos before learning that their city, building or lease does not allow short stays. This guide takes you through each step in the right order, honestly, with no income promises, because demand is seasonal, costs add up and rules change.

Decide which kind of hosting fits you

Short-term hosting usually takes one of three shapes, and each changes your privacy, your workload and which rules apply.

TypeYour privacyHands-on work
A room in your homeLowerDaily contact
A private space with its own entrance, such as an annex or granny flatMediumRegular contact
A whole property, such as a cottage, cabin or apartment (flat)FullTurnovers and remote help

Every stay involves answering questions, sending check-in details, cleaning, laundry, restocking and updating your calendar. Take a normal week from your diary and pencil in where a checkout, a two to four hour clean, a laundry load and a check-in would fit. If no day works, you may need a co-host or cleaner, which changes your costs.

Talk to everyone you live with, and to the neighbors. Give them your number and ask them to call you directly if anything bothers them. If you are still unsure, invite a friend for a two-night practice stay and treat them exactly like a guest.

Check short-term rental rules first

This is the most important step. Rules vary by country, state, province, city and even neighborhood, and they change often. Breaking them can mean fines, an order to stop, a broken lease or a refused insurance claim.

Check each layer that applies to you:

  • Local government: registration, permits or licenses, limits on nights per year, rules that you must live there, guest caps and safety requirements.
  • Zoning or planning: whether short stays are allowed in your zone, and whether an annex or converted garage is approved for sleeping.
  • Building, condo, strata or homeowners association rules, which may ban short stays or set long minimum stays.
  • Your landlord, if you rent. Subletting without permission can break your lease.
  • Your mortgage lender, if you have a mortgage.

Search your local government website for terms like short-term rental, short-term let, holiday let or vacation rental. Then email the planning or licensing department describing your address and space, and ask what applies. Keep every answer, permit and certificate in one rules file, with renewal dates in your calendar.

If the answer is no, do not list quietly. Look for legal alternatives instead, such as longer stays or taking a lodger, where local rules allow it.

Insurance and taxes for hosts

A standard home, renter's or contents policy may not cover paying guests. If you do not tell your insurer, they may refuse a claim, even one unrelated to guests. Call before you host, describe your plans and ask about:

  • Damage and theft by guests
  • Liability if a guest or visitor is injured
  • Loss of income after damage
  • Any safety conditions you must meet

If your insurer will not cover hosting, look at specialist cover, often called home sharing, holiday let or vacation rental insurance. Booking platform guarantees and protection programs have limits and exclusions, so treat them as an extra, not a replacement.

Hosting income is usually taxable. Occupancy, tourist or lodging taxes may apply per stay, and sales tax, VAT or GST may apply in some places. Some platforms collect certain local taxes, but not all of them everywhere. Open a separate bank account, log every payment and cost, and photograph receipts on the day you get them.

This is general information, not legal, tax or insurance advice. Confirm everything with your local government, your insurer and an accountant or your tax authority, and get answers in writing.

Make your rental safe before guests arrive

Guests arrive tired, often in the dark, in a place they do not know. Safety is your job.

  • Smoke alarms on every level and in or near every sleeping area.
  • Carbon monoxide alarms wherever there is a fuel-burning appliance, such as a gas boiler or furnace, fireplace or wood stove, or an attached garage.
  • Test every alarm before each stay, or at least monthly, and replace batteries and units as the manufacturer says.
  • A fire extinguisher and fire blanket in the kitchen, and a stocked first aid kit.
  • Clear hallways and exits, with doors that open easily from inside.
  • Gas appliances serviced and checked by a qualified professional, and wiring checked by an electrician.
  • Secure handrails, good stair lighting and non-slip bath mats.
  • Window restrictors, secure balcony railings and blind cord cleats if children may stay.
  • No space heaters near bedding or curtains, and pools or hot tubs fenced and covered as local rules require.

Post emergency information inside the front door: the full address, your number and the local emergency number (911 in the US and Canada, 999 or 112 in the UK, 000 in Australia, 111 in New Zealand). Never place cameras in bedrooms, bathrooms or sleeping areas, and disclose any security cameras or noise monitors in your listing.

Set up a space guests love

Wait until the rules check is done before you spend much. Then focus on the things guests notice most: a comfortable bed, a clean bathroom and a good night's sleep.

  • A good mattress with a protector, and at least three full sets of bedding and towels per bed: one on the bed, one in the wash and one on the shelf.
  • Blackout curtains or blinds, bedside lamps near a socket and somewhere to put a suitcase.
  • Kitchen basics that match the kind of stays you expect.
  • A short house manual with Wi-Fi details, appliance instructions, house rules and local tips.

Lock away valuables and personal documents. Then write a listing that is honest as well as appealing: describe stairs, shared spaces, step-free access and bathroom layout accurately, and show the real space in photos taken in natural light.

How to price a short-term rental

Nobody can tell you what to charge without knowing your area, space and costs. Use a method instead.

  1. Build a cost sheet. List per-stay costs (cleaning, laundry, toiletries), per-night costs (heating, electricity, water) and fixed costs (insurance, permits, internet, platform fees, taxes, replacing linens). Spread yearly costs over a cautious estimate of nights.
  2. Research comparable listings nearby by searching as a guest for different dates. Note weekday and weekend prices, cleaning fees, minimum stays and reviews.
  3. Set a minimum nightly price that covers your costs and makes the work worthwhile.
  4. Adjust for high, shoulder and low seasons, weekends and local events, without extreme jumps guests will resent.
  5. Decide on minimum stays, a cleaning fee based on real costs and discounts for weekly stays.

Automatic pricing tools can help, but set your own minimum and maximum limits and check them weekly at first. Review your prices once a month.

Cleaning and turnovers made simple

Cleanliness is one of the first things guests notice and comment on. A repeatable system keeps you thorough and fast.

  1. Check for damage before you clean, and photograph anything you find with the date.
  2. Open windows, strip the beds and start the first wash.
  3. Clean top to bottom and from the back of each room toward the door, leaving floors until last.
  4. Disinfect high-touch surfaces every time: handles, switches, remotes, faucets (taps), the toilet flush and any keypad.
  5. Check beds and cupboards for signs of pests.
  6. Restock from a list taped inside your store cupboard.
  7. Run your safety check and reset the space.

Wash bedding and towels on the hottest setting the fabric allows and dry them fully. Store cleaning products safely and never mix them. If you hire a cleaner, give them your checklist, pay fairly and on time, and check local employment or contractor rules.

Messages, problems and reviews

Most good reviews come from clear communication. Prepare short templates for the booking confirmation, a pre-arrival message, check-in instructions, a first-morning check, a checkout reminder and a thank-you with a review request. Reply quickly and kindly, even to questions your listing already answers.

Write clear, reasonable house rules on noise, parties, parking and trash (rubbish), and treat every guest fairly, since anti-discrimination laws apply. Keep payments and messages on the booking platform, and watch for fake payment messages and overpayment refund scams.

When a review comes in, reply to good ones with thanks and to critical ones calmly, without arguing, saying what you have fixed. Then set a monthly routine to check prices, supplies and safety equipment, and a yearly one to renew permits and review the rules.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to run a short-term rental?

In many places, yes. Cities, councils and regions often require registration, a permit or a license, and some limit how many nights a year you can rent a whole home or require you to live there. Some areas ban short stays in certain zones. Search your local government website and email the planning or licensing department to confirm what applies to your address before you list.

Can I rent out a room if I am a tenant?

Only with your landlord's permission. Most leases say something about subletting, lodgers or paying guests, and subletting without permission can break your lease and put your tenancy at risk. Ask in writing, explaining which room, how often and how you will handle safety and insurance. If they agree, keep the signed permission in your rules file. If they refuse, do not host.

Does home insurance cover paying guests?

Often not fully. Many standard home, renter's and contents policies exclude business use or require you to report paying guests. If you do not tell your insurer, they may refuse claims. Call before your first guest, describe your plans and get the answer in writing. You may need specialist home sharing or holiday let cover, including liability for guest injuries.

How much can I earn from a short-term rental?

Nobody can say honestly without knowing your area, space and costs. Earnings vary with location, season, local rules, reviews and how much you spend on cleaning, supplies, fees, insurance and taxes. Some months will be quiet. Build a cost sheet, research comparable listings for different dates and work out your minimum price before deciding whether hosting is worth it for you.

What safety equipment does a short-term rental need?

At a minimum, working smoke alarms on every level and near sleeping areas, carbon monoxide alarms near any fuel-burning appliance, a fire extinguisher and fire blanket in the kitchen, a first aid kit, clear exits and posted emergency information. Gas and electrical checks by qualified professionals are often required. Your local rules and insurer may add more, so check both.

How long does a turnover take between guests?

It depends on the size of the space and your system. A single room may take a couple of hours, while a whole home with several beds takes longer. Time your first few turnovers with a written checklist, then add a cushion. If cleaning keeps running close to check-in, move check-in later, set a minimum stay or block a gap day.

Your first step today is simple: search your local government website for short-term rental rules, then send one email to the planning or licensing department asking what applies to your address.
