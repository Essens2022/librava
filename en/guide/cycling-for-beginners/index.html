Cycling for Beginners: How to Start Riding Safely and Enjoy It

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe you have not ridden since you were a kid, or you have a bike in the shed and a nagging worry about traffic, hills and flat tires. The short answer: choose a bike that suits the riding you will actually do, set the saddle so your knee is slightly bent at the bottom of the pedal stroke, practice in a quiet place, then build up from three easy 20 to 30 minute rides a week to a 90-minute ride over about 8 weeks.

This guide covers choosing and fitting a bike, the gear worth buying first, handling skills, riding in traffic, a one-minute safety check, fixing a flat, and how hard to push. It works at any age.

What bike should a beginner buy?

Start with how you will ride, not what looks fast. Most beginners ride on roads, paths and around town, and for that a hybrid is the easiest choice.

TypeBest forWatch out for
HybridRoads, paths, town, upright positionLess fast on long rides
RoadFast rides on pavementStretched position, narrow tires
GravelMixed surfacesDrop bars take practice
MountainOff-road trailsSlow and heavy on pavement
E-bikeHills, commutingWeight, cost, local rules

Frame size matters more than brand or price. Use the maker's size chart for your height and inside leg, then test ride. You should be able to stand over the top tube with both feet flat, reach the bars without locking your elbows, and feel neither cramped nor stretched. Between two sizes, the smaller is usually easier to handle.

Very cheap department store bikes often have weak brakes and parts that never adjust well, which puts people off. Buy the best bike you can comfortably afford from a bike shop, or a good used one, and keep money back for a helmet, lights, a lock and a pump. When buying used, check for frame cracks, straight wheels, firm brakes and smooth gears, and ask to see the frame number so you can check it against stolen bike registers where they exist.

How to set your saddle height

Most aches in new riders come from poor fit, and saddle height is the biggest single fix. Too low and your knees ache at the front; too high and your hips rock side to side.

  1. Lean the bike against a wall or ask a friend to hold it.
  2. Sit on the saddle in your riding shoes and put your heel on the pedal.
  3. Pedal backward until the crank points straight down.
  4. With your heel on the pedal, your leg should be straight. Raise the saddle if your knee is bent; lower it if you rock your hips to reach.
  5. Ride with the ball of your foot over the pedal. You will now have a slight bend in the knee at the bottom of the stroke.

Adjust in small steps of about 5 mm (a quarter inch), and never raise the seat post above the minimum insertion mark printed on it. Keep the saddle level, or tipped down very slightly at the nose, and set the bars high enough that your elbows stay softly bent.

At the right height, you usually cannot put both feet flat on the ground while seated. That is normal. When you stop, slide forward off the saddle and stand over the top tube. If that feels nervous at first, start a little lower and raise it as your confidence grows.

Essential cycling gear for beginners

You do not need special clothing or expensive extras to start. Spend first on the things that keep you safe and moving.

  • A helmet that fits snugly and sits level, about two fingers above your eyebrows. Helmets are required by law in Australia and New Zealand and recommended elsewhere; check your local rules.
  • A white front light and red rear light, plus reflectors. Laws vary, but lights are wise in any low light.
  • A good lock. Lock the frame to a fixed object, not just a wheel, and register your frame number where you can.
  • A pump, spare tube, tire levers and a small multi-tool.
  • A water bottle and cage.

Padded shorts, cycling gloves and clip-in pedals can wait until you know what you enjoy. Comfortable everyday clothes work fine for short rides, with a layer you can remove as you warm up.

Practice basic bike handling first

Before you ride on roads, spend a session or two in an empty parking lot, a quiet park path or a school playground out of hours. Practice these until they feel easy:

  • Starting smoothly. Raise one pedal to about 2 o'clock, push down and sit as you roll.
  • Stopping. Squeeze both brakes evenly and progressively, slide forward off the saddle and put a foot down.
  • Shifting. Change to an easier gear before a hill or a stop, while pedaling lightly.
  • Looking ahead. Look where you want to go, not at the pothole you want to miss.
  • Riding slowly in a straight line, and weaving around a few water bottles or cones.
  • Looking over your shoulder without swerving, and riding one-handed for signals.

Most of your stopping power comes from the front brake, but grabbing it hard can tip you forward. Use both, and brake before corners rather than in them.

How to ride safely in traffic

Know your local rules first. In the US and Canada you ride on the right; in the UK, Australia and New Zealand you keep left. Ride predictably, in a straight line, and make your intentions obvious.

Hugging the curb feels safe but often is not. You meet drains, glass and potholes there, and drivers tend to squeeze past. On roads where traffic can pass safely, ride about 3 feet (1 meter) out from the curb. Take the middle of the lane when the lane is too narrow for a car to pass safely, when approaching junctions or pinch points, and when traffic is moving at your speed. This is legal and recommended in many places, though details vary.

  • Stay at least a door's width, about 3 to 4 feet (1 meter or more), from parked cars.
  • Look behind, then signal for two or three seconds: arm straight out for a turn, arm out and down for slowing.
  • Return both hands to the bars before you turn.
  • Never ride up the inside of a truck (lorry) or bus at a junction. If you cannot see the driver's mirrors, the driver cannot see you.
  • Use lights and bright clothing in low light.

Build up gradually: quiet streets first, then busier roads at quieter times. A weekend test run of a commute route takes the stress out of the first weekday ride.

The ABC bike check and fixing a flat

This one-minute check before every ride prevents most problems.

  • A is for air. Tires should feel firm and sit within the pressure range printed on the tire sidewall. As a rough guide, hybrid tires often run about 45 to 70 PSI (3.1 to 4.8 bar) and road tires 60 to 100 PSI, but the sidewall has the final word.
  • B is for brakes. Both levers should stop the wheels firmly and stop well short of the handlebar.
  • C is for chain. It should be clean, lightly oiled and run smoothly when you turn the pedals backward.

Also check that wheel levers or axles are closed tight. Clean and lube the chain every few weeks or after wet rides, and see a mechanic for brakes that feel spongy, gears that keep slipping, or a wobbly wheel.

Fixing a flat in brief

  1. Move well off the road.
  2. Shift to the smallest rear gear, open the brake if needed and remove the wheel.
  3. Use tire levers to lift one side of the tire off the rim and pull out the tube.
  4. Run your fingers carefully around the inside of the tire to find and remove the glass or thorn.
  5. Put a little air in the new tube, fit it, and push the tire back on with your thumbs.
  6. Inflate, check the tire sits evenly, refit the wheel and test the brakes.

Practice once at home so the roadside version is calm.

An 8-week beginner cycling plan

Ride at a pace where you can still talk, about 3 to 5 on a 1 to 10 effort scale, and take at least two rest days a week. Repeat any week that feels too hard.

WeekRidesLongest rideFocus
13 rides, 20 to 30 min30 minABC check, easy pace
23 rides, 25 to 35 min35 minSmooth shifting
33 rides, 30 to 40 min40 minOne gentle hill, signals
42 to 3 rides, 30 min35 minEasy week, practice a flat fix
53 rides, 30 to 45 min45 minTraffic skills
63 to 4 rides55 minDrinking and eating on the bike
73 to 4 rides65 minA social ride
83 to 4 rides80 to 90 minYour first longer ride

Spin a lighter gear a little faster rather than grinding a big one. Many riders feel best around 70 to 90 pedal turns a minute: count your right foot at the bottom of the stroke for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. Shift down before a hill, not halfway up it.

Sip water every 15 to 20 minutes. A rough guide is one bottle, about 500 to 750 ml (17 to 25 fl oz), per hour on moderate rides, more in heat. On rides over about 90 minutes, eat something small, such as a banana or an oat bar, every 30 to 45 minutes to avoid a sudden energy crash.

Health check. Talk to your doctor before starting if you have a heart or lung condition, high blood pressure, diabetes, joint problems or have been inactive for a long time. Stop and get urgent help (911 in the US, 999 in the UK) for chest pain, severe breathlessness, dizziness or fainting. After any fall involving a blow to the head, get checked, and replace your helmet.

Frequently asked questions

How far should a beginner cyclist ride?

Start with time rather than distance: 20 to 30 minutes at an easy, chatty pace, three times a week. For many beginners that is roughly 3 to 6 miles (5 to 10 km), depending on the bike and terrain. Add about 5 to 10 minutes to your longest ride each week, with an easier week every month, and most people can ride 80 to 90 minutes comfortably after about 8 weeks.

Is cycling good exercise for beginners?

Yes. Cycling is low impact on the joints, builds leg strength and endurance, and is easy to fit into daily life through commuting and errands. You control the effort, so it suits most fitness levels and ages. E-bikes count too: riders still get real exercise, partly because they tend to ride longer and more often. Check with your doctor first if you have a health condition.

Why does my bum hurt when cycling?

Some saddle soreness is normal for the first few weeks while your body adapts. Persistent pain often comes from saddle height or angle, a saddle that does not suit your shape, or riding too long too soon. Keep the saddle level, check the height, build up gradually, and consider padded shorts worn without underwear. Numbness or pain that does not settle is a reason to see a bike fitter.

Do I need to wear a helmet when cycling?

It depends on where you live. Helmets are required by law in Australia and New Zealand, and some US states, Canadian provinces and cities require them for certain ages. In the UK they are recommended but not required by law. A well-fitted helmet that sits level, about two fingers above your eyebrows, with the straps snug under your chin, is a sensible choice everywhere.

How do I get better at cycling up hills?

Shift to an easier gear before the hill starts, not halfway up. Stay seated, keep a steady pedal rhythm and let your speed drop while your effort stays manageable. Look a little ahead rather than at the top. Start with gentle slopes and repeat them over a few weeks. Walking a steep hill is perfectly fine, and your legs will get stronger with regular riding.

How often should I pump up my bike tires?

Check them before every ride by squeezing them, and use a pump with a gauge about once a week, since tires slowly lose air even without a puncture. Narrow road tires lose pressure faster than wider hybrid or mountain bike tires. Always inflate within the minimum and maximum printed on the tire sidewall. Firm, correctly inflated tires roll more easily and help prevent pinch flats.

Your first step today: check your bike's saddle height with the heel method, do the ABC check of air, brakes and chain, and take a 20-minute easy ride somewhere quiet to practice starting, stopping and shifting.
