How to Compost at Home: A Simple Beginner Guide for Any Space

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You want to stop throwing peelings in the trash, but you are worried about smells, rats or getting it wrong. To compost at home, collect kitchen and garden scraps (greens), mix them with about 2 to 3 times as much dry material like cardboard and leaves (browns), keep the pile as damp as a wrung-out sponge, let air in, and leave out meat, dairy and pet waste.

That is genuinely most of it. This guide shows you how to choose a method for your space, build your first heap, know when compost is ready and fix the common problems that put people off.

How composting works

A compost heap is full of tiny workers: bacteria, fungi, worms, beetles and woodlice (pill bugs or sow bugs). They need four things from you: a mix of greens and browns to eat, moisture, air and time. Give them those and they turn scraps into dark, crumbly soil.

StageWhat happensTypical length
WarmingBacteria multiply and the heap heats up1 to 3 days
HotSoft material breaks down fastA few days to a few weeks
CoolingFungi and larger creatures move inSeveral weeks
CuringMaterial darkens and settles1 to 3 months or more

A well-built heap can reach about 130 to 160°F (55 to 70°C) in the middle. Most home heaps never get that hot, and that is fine. A cool heap still makes excellent compost; it just takes longer, often six months to a year or more. Do not give up on a heap because it never steamed.

Times below are rough, since weather, ingredients and effort all matter.

For most beginners, a closed bin added to bit by bit is the easiest start. In an apartment (flat), a worm bin or a bokashi bucket handles kitchen scraps. Worm bins need red wigglers (tiger worms), not earthworms dug from the garden. Bokashi ferments scraps in a sealed bucket, and the fermented contents still need burying in soil or adding to a bin to finish. Many households combine methods, such as a worm bin for the kitchen and a slow heap for garden waste.

Greens and browns made simple

Greens are moist, rich in nitrogen and rot quickly. They are the fuel that gets the heap working.

  • Fruit and vegetable scraps, peels and cores
  • Coffee grounds and loose tea leaves
  • Grass clippings and soft plant trimmings
  • Young weeds without seeds
  • Manure from plant-eating animals such as chickens or rabbits, with their straw or shavings bedding

Browns are dry, rich in carbon and slow to rot. They soak up moisture and hold air spaces open.

  • Fallen leaves, ideally shredded
  • Plain cardboard, egg boxes and toilet roll tubes, torn up
  • Newspaper and plain paper, shredded
  • Straw, and sawdust or shavings from untreated wood
  • Woody prunings and old stalks, chopped small

Color is not the test. Coffee grounds look brown but act as a green. Most homes make plenty of greens and never enough browns, so keep a bag of leaves or a box of torn cardboard beside the bin.

What is the right compost ratio

A common starting guide is about 2 to 3 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume. In practice, every time you tip in a caddy of kitchen scraps, add two or three caddies of cardboard, leaves or straw. You will not get it exact and you do not need to, because the heap tells you how you are doing.

  • Slimy, smelly or full of flies? Add more browns.
  • Dry, dusty and nothing happening? Add greens and some water.

Aim for the dampness of a wrung-out sponge. Scrap-heavy bins rarely need watering; heaps of dry leaves and straw usually do. Smaller pieces break down faster too, so tear cardboard into strips, snap woody stems and cut big stalks into chunks. A quick stir with a fork each time you add scraps keeps greens and browns in close contact.

Grass clippings go slimy fast in a thick layer. Mix them with at least as much brown material as you add them, or let them wilt in a thin layer for a day first.

What not to put in compost

Leave outWhy
Meat, fish and bonesSmell, attract rats and other pests
Dairy productsSmell, attract pests
Oily or greasy foodSlows breakdown, attracts pests
Dog and cat wasteCan carry harmful parasites and germs
Diseased plantsDisease can survive and spread
Weeds with seeds or tough rootsMay spread around your garden
Ash from coal or treated woodCan contain harmful substances
Glossy or coated paper, plastics, stickersDo not break down well

Some items are gray areas. Bread, pasta and rice will compost but tempt rats, so keep them to small amounts in a sealed bin or use bokashi, which can also take meat and dairy. Eggshells are fine if crushed. Many tea bags contain a little plastic, so loose tea or bags labeled plastic-free are safer. Compostable cups and packaging are often made for hot commercial sites and can sit in a home bin for years unless they say home compostable.

Tough weeds such as bindweed and couch grass (quackgrass) can regrow from tiny root pieces. Drown them in a lidded bucket of water for a few weeks until they turn to sludge, then add that to the heap.

How to start a compost bin step by step

  1. Pick a level spot with good drainage, ideally on bare soil so worms can move in. Partial shade suits most climates. Keep it away from shared fences and windows, and close enough that you will actually walk there in the rain.
  2. Put a lidded caddy by the kitchen sink and empty it every few days, or daily in hot weather.
  3. Lay 4 to 6 inches (10 to 15 cm) of coarse twiggy material or straw on the bottom so air can rise and water can drain.
  4. Add greens and browns, mixing them as you go. A shovelful of garden soil or finished compost adds a starter population of microbes.
  5. Water lightly if the materials are dry, aiming for wrung-out sponge dampness.
  6. Finish each addition with a layer of browns to cover fresh scraps and keep flies away.
  7. Turn or stir the heap every few weeks if you can, or push a fork into it to open air channels.

If the only space is paving or concrete, spread a few shovelfuls of soil or old compost on the base first. To keep rodents out, stand the bin on fine wire mesh (hardware cloth) with holes no bigger than about 1/4 inch (6 mm), and use a secure lid.

Why does my compost smell and other fixes

A healthy bin smells earthy or of very little. Most problems come down to too wet, too dry, or not enough air.

ProblemLikely causeFix
Rotten egg smellToo wet, no airTurn and mix in dry browns
Ammonia smellToo many greensAdd cardboard, leaves or paper
Slimy and drippingToo wet or rain getting inAdd browns, use a lid
Dusty, nothing happeningToo dryWater while turning, add greens
Fruit fliesExposed sweet scrapsBury scraps, cover with browns
AntsPile too dryWater and turn
Very slowPieces too big, pile too small or coldChop smaller, add greens, be patient

For rats, stop adding food scraps for a while, turn the pile thoroughly, fix any gaps and keep the pile moist, since rats prefer dry nesting spots. If the problem continues, your local council or municipality can usually advise. Fat grayish grubs in a wet pile are often soldier fly larvae, which are harmless.

Wear gloves when handling compost materials, especially manures, and wash your hands well afterward. Never put dog or cat waste in a heap used for food plants. If you suspect rats, do not touch droppings with bare hands and keep children and pets away until it is sorted.

How to tell when compost is ready

Finished compost is dark brown, crumbly, cool to the touch and smells like a woodland floor after rain. A few twigs, eggshell flecks or avocado pits are normal; pick them out and throw them back in the next batch.

Not sure? Put a few handfuls in a pot and sow quick seeds such as cress or radish. If they sprout and grow normally over a week or so, the compost is mature. If they struggle or yellow, give it a few more weeks. Nearly ready compost that is still a bit coarse works well as a mulch spread on top of beds, while your finest compost is best saved for pots and seed sowing.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to make compost at home?

It depends on the method and how much effort you put in. A well-managed hot heap that you turn regularly can be ready in 1 to 3 months. A closed bin added to bit by bit usually takes 6 to 12 months or more. Worm bins take about 2 to 4 months, and leaf mold 1 to 2 years. Cold weather slows everything down.

Can I compost in an apartment without a yard?

Yes. A worm bin fits on a shelf, in a cupboard or on a balcony and turns kitchen scraps into rich castings, as long as you use red wigglers rather than garden earthworms. A bokashi bucket fits under the sink and can take cooked food, but the fermented contents need burying in soil or adding to a compost bin to finish, so you may need a garden, planter or community site.

Should compost be wet or dry?

Compost should feel like a wrung-out sponge: damp all the way through, but not dripping when you squeeze a handful. Too wet and it goes slimy and smelly because air cannot get in, so add browns. Too dry and the microbes stop working, so water it as you turn and add some fresh greens.

Do I need to turn my compost?

No, but turning speeds things up. Turning lets air in, mixes greens and browns and helps a heap stay hot. If you never turn it, a cold heap will still become compost, just more slowly, often in a year or so. A quick stir when you add scraps, or poking air holes with a fork, is a good middle ground.

Will a compost bin attract rats?

A well-run bin is far less attractive to rats than many people fear. Keep meat, fish, dairy, oily food and cooked scraps out of open bins, bury fresh scraps in the middle, keep the pile moist and turn it regularly. A secure lid and a base of fine wire mesh with holes no bigger than about 1/4 inch (6 mm) help keep them out.

Can you put citrus peel and onions in compost?

Yes, in small amounts in an outdoor heap. Citrus peel and onion skins break down slowly and are acidic, but a little mixed into a large bin does no harm. Worms dislike them, so keep them out of a worm bin or add only a tiny amount. Chop peels into smaller pieces to help them break down faster.

Your first step today: put a lidded caddy by the sink and fill one bag or box with torn cardboard or dry leaves. Every time you empty the caddy into your bin, throw a couple of handfuls of those browns on top, and you have already solved most of the smell, fly and slime problems before they start.
