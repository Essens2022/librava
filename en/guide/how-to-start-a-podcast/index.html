How to Start a Podcast: A Simple Beginner's Plan From Idea to Launch

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe you have a phone full of half-recorded voice memos and a show idea you have been talking about for years. To start a podcast, you choose a narrow topic and one specific listener, record in a quiet, soft room with simple gear, edit in free software, then upload your episodes to a podcast hosting service that sends them to every major listening app through an RSS feed.

You do not need a studio, a radio voice or a big budget. With a few hours a week, most beginners can have a first episode recorded, edited and published within about 30 days. This guide walks you through each step, with checklists and the settings you will actually need.

How to start a podcast in 8 steps

  1. Get clear on why you want a show and who it is for.
  2. Pick a topic that can fill at least 50 episodes and write a one-sentence show promise.
  3. Choose a format, an episode length and a schedule you can keep for six months.
  4. Gather the smallest kit that works, starting with what you already own.
  5. Make your room sound soft and quiet, then run a quick sound check.
  6. Plan, record and edit your first three episodes.
  7. Create cover art, a show description and an intro, using only music you have rights to.
  8. Sign up with a podcast hosting service, publish a trailer, submit your feed and launch.

Before you buy anything, spend ten minutes answering a few questions in a notebook. Who is it for, what should they gain from an episode, and how many hours a week can you honestly give it? Keep the answer somewhere you will see it on the day your motivation dips.

Find a podcast idea you can talk about for years

Most new shows stop before episode ten, often because the topic was too wide or too thin. A good idea is narrow enough to describe in one breath and deep enough to fill dozens of episodes.

Try the 50-episode test. Set a 30-minute timer and list 50 possible episode titles: questions people ask you, mistakes you have made, guests you would love to interview. If you stall at 15, widen the topic a little (from sourdough to home bread baking) or plan a limited series instead.

Pick one listener and an angle

Picture one real-sounding person, such as Priya, 34, who just bought a house with a small garden and listens on her commute. Then choose what makes your show different. Angles usually come from the listener you serve (gardening for renters), the format (five-minute daily tips), a place (local history of one town), your perspective (a nurse talking about healthy habits) or your tone.

Now write your show promise with this simple formula: a [format] show for [specific listener] that helps them [result]. For example, "a weekly 20-minute show for first-time gardeners with small spaces that helps them grow food without wasting money."

Listen to a few similar shows to spot gaps, never to copy them. Choose a name that is easy to say and spell, and check podcast directories, a web search and your country's free public trademark database before you commit.

Choose your podcast format and episode length

Solo, co-hosted and interview shows are the easiest formats for beginners. Narrative storytelling and audio drama are rewarding but take far more time per episode.

LengthWorks well forWatch out for
5 to 10 minutesDaily tips, short storiesMust be very focused
15 to 30 minutesSolo teaching, commute showsEasy to pad if unplanned
30 to 60 minutesInterviews, co-hosted chatsNeeds strong structure
Over 60 minutesDeep conversationsOnly if listeners ask for it

Make episodes as long as they need to be and no longer, and keep lengths roughly consistent. Consistency matters more than frequency. A show that comes out every two weeks without fail builds more trust than a weekly show that disappears for a month. Pick the slowest schedule you are sure you can keep for six months, or plan in seasons of about ten episodes with breaks in between.

What equipment do you need to start a podcast

Far less than you think. Good content and a quiet room matter more than expensive gear. Here are three kits, from almost free to mid-range.

KitWhat it includesGood for
Almost freeYour phone or laptop, wired earbuds, free audio editing software, a soft roomTesting your idea
Starter (low-cost)A dynamic USB microphone, closed-back headphones, a foam windscreen or pop filter, a desk standSolo shows and remote guests
Upgrade (mid-range)A dynamic XLR microphone per person, an audio interface, boom armsCo-hosts and frequent recording

Dynamic or condenser, USB or XLR

Dynamic microphones are less sensitive and reject more room noise, which makes them the safer choice in an ordinary spare bedroom. Condenser microphones capture lovely detail in a treated room, but in a normal home they also catch the echo, the traffic and the neighbor's dog. Look for a cardioid pickup pattern, which hears mainly from the front.

A USB microphone plugs straight into your computer and is perfect for a solo show. An XLR microphone needs an audio interface but makes it easy for two people to record on separate tracks. Some microphones offer both, a sensible middle path.

Use wired, closed-back headphones so sound does not leak into the microphone. Hold off on mixers, pricey condensers and paid software until you have about ten episodes out.

How to record a podcast at home

Echo comes from hard, flat surfaces such as bare walls, wooden floors and windows. Soft, uneven things absorb it: curtains, rugs, cushions, bookshelves and a duvet hung behind you. A closet (wardrobe) full of clothes makes an excellent low-cost vocal booth.

Before every session, turn off fridges and fans where you can, settle pets, close windows and put phones in airplane mode (flight mode). Keep the microphone about a fist or a hand-width from your mouth, angled slightly to the side so your breath passes it rather than hitting it.

Your ten-minute sound check

  1. Run through the noise checklist and put soft furnishings in place.
  2. Position the microphone and put on headphones at a moderate volume.
  3. Speak at full energy and set the input level (gain).
  4. Record 30 seconds of speech and 10 seconds of silence.
  5. Listen back for echo, hum, hiss and pops, then fix one problem or start.

Settings that work

Record in WAV at 44.1 or 48 kHz, 24-bit if possible, with each voice on its own track. Leave headroom so your loudest peaks sit comfortably below the maximum. Run a backup recording and copy your files to a second place straight after the session.

When you stumble, pause, then repeat the whole sentence. That makes the mistake easy to spot and cut later. For remote guests, ask each person to record on their own device where possible, which gives you clean audio even if the internet connection wobbles.

Always tell guests and callers they are being recorded and get clear permission before you start. In the US some states require every person in a conversation to consent, and consent and data protection rules also apply in the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. A simple written guest release (permission to record, edit and publish) is a sensible habit. This is general information, not legal advice.

Plan episodes people keep listening to

A simple five-part structure keeps listeners with you.

  • Hook: the first 30 seconds, a question, story or surprising fact that shows why this episode matters.
  • Intro: show name, your one-sentence promise and who is speaking.
  • Main segments: two or three points, each with a story or example.
  • Recap: two or three takeaways.
  • Call to action: one clear next step, not five.

Choose bullet outlines, a full script or a mix. Bullet notes keep you sounding natural; scripts help with tricky facts or nervous starts. Either way, write for the ear: short sentences, everyday words and a little helpful repetition.

Plan a season of ten episodes before you launch. Give each a working title, a one-sentence promise and three main points.

How to edit a podcast with free software

Free audio editing software is enough for a clean, professional-sounding show. Follow the same four steps every time.

  1. Cut: remove false starts, long tangents, technical problems and anything a guest asked you to remove.
  2. Clean: take out only distracting ums and keep natural pauses. Use noise reduction gently, because heavy settings make voices sound underwater.
  3. Level: add a high-pass filter around 80 to 100 Hz to remove rumble, a little EQ and gentle compression so quiet and loud parts even out.
  4. Export: aim for about -16 LUFS for stereo or -19 LUFS for mono, with true peak no higher than about -1 dBTP, then export an MP3 at about 96 to 128 kbps for mono speech or 128 to 192 kbps for stereo.

These are common guidelines, so check your hosting service's recommendations. Keep the WAV master and do a final listen on a different device.

Use only music, sound effects and clips you have permission or a license for. Giving credit, using under 30 seconds or writing "no copyright intended" does not make it legal, and royalty-free still comes with license terms. The simplest route is to create or license your own intro music.

How to upload a podcast and launch it

A podcast is not uploaded to each listening app separately. You upload your MP3 files to a podcast hosting service, which stores them and creates an RSS feed (a web address listing your episodes). You submit that feed once to each directory, and from then on new episodes appear automatically. Avoid putting audio on an ordinary website server, which is not built for many large downloads.

Choose a host by features rather than price alone: clear statistics, enough storage, scheduling, transcripts and, above all, an easy way to redirect your feed if you ever move. Check what happens on a free plan, such as ads in your episodes or older episodes being removed.

Your launch checklist

  • Cover art: square, commonly 3000 x 3000 pixels (minimum often 1400 x 1400), JPEG or PNG, readable as a tiny thumbnail.
  • Show description of about 100 to 200 words, category, language and the correct explicit content setting.
  • A one to three minute trailer, published one to two weeks early so directories can approve your feed.
  • Three full episodes released together on launch day, with episodes four to six already planned.
  • Show notes with a short summary, guest details, timestamps and any sponsor disclosures, plus a corrected transcript.
  • Personal messages to friends, family and guests, and one clip or story to share each day of launch week.

Grow your audience and keep going

Most podcasts grow slowly, so give your show at least six months of steady publishing before you judge it. Tell people personally rather than posting the same link everywhere. Swap guest appearances or trailers with similar shows, share short captioned clips, and ask happy listeners for reviews.

Compare each episode's first-week downloads and do more of what works. If you earn money later through sponsors, listener support, products or services, disclose sponsors, free products and affiliate links clearly, both out loud and in show notes, and keep simple records because podcast income may be taxable.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to start a podcast?

It can cost almost nothing. You can record your first episodes with a phone, wired earbuds, a soft room and free audio editing software, and many podcast hosting services have a free or low-cost starter plan. Most people later add a low-cost dynamic USB microphone, headphones and a pop filter. Delay bigger purchases until you have about ten episodes.

Can I start a podcast with just my phone?

Yes. A modern smartphone can sound surprisingly good if you treat it like a proper microphone. Prop it on books about a hand-width from your mouth, slightly to one side, switch on airplane mode and record in a high-quality format if your recording app allows it. Do not hold it in your hand, since finger movements create noise.

How long should a podcast episode be?

As long as it needs to be to keep your promise, and no longer. Tight 15 to 30 minute episodes suit solo teaching and commutes, while interviews often run 30 to 60 minutes. A focused 15-minute episode beats a padded 45-minute one. Keep your lengths roughly consistent so listeners know what they are signing up for.

How often should I release new episodes?

Choose the slowest schedule you are sure you can keep for six months. Every two weeks is a sensible starting point for many beginners because it leaves time for editing and life. Weekly is a strong rhythm if you can sustain it. You can also publish in seasons of about ten episodes, as long as you tell listeners before a break.

How do I get my podcast onto listening apps?

Upload your episodes to a podcast hosting service, which creates your RSS feed. Then submit that feed once to each major podcast directory, either through each directory's submission page or a one-click option your host offers. Approval can take from minutes to a couple of weeks, so publish a short trailer early and submit before launch day.

Can I use popular music in my podcast?

Not without permission or a proper license. Giving credit, using a short clip or adding a "no copyright intended" note does not make it legal. Fair use in the US and fair dealing elsewhere are narrow and judged case by case. Use music you created, music licensed for podcasts, or royalty-free tracks whose license terms you follow.

Your first step today is small: write down why you want a podcast and who it is for, then list as many episode ideas as you can in 30 minutes. If that list reaches 50, you have a show worth recording, and everything else (gear, editing and launch) can be learned one evening at a time.
