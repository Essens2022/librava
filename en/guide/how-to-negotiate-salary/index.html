How to Negotiate Salary: A Calm 7-Step Plan With Exact Scripts

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have a job offer in your inbox, the number is a little lower than you hoped, and part of you just wants to say yes and be done with it. To negotiate salary well, research a realistic range, decide your walk-away point before money comes up, thank them, ask for a day to review, then counter with one specific number and a short reason tied to the value you bring.

That is the whole method in two sentences. The rest of this guide shows you how to do each step calmly, with exact words you can borrow, so you can ask for more without sounding greedy or risking the offer.

Why you should negotiate a job offer

The offer stage is the strongest position you will have with this employer. They have spent weeks interviewing, they have probably told other candidates no, and they want this finished as much as you do. Many employers build a little room into a first offer precisely because they expect some discussion.

There is also a long tail. Every future percentage raise, bonus and retirement (UK: pension) contribution is usually calculated from the base you agree now. If you start 5 percent lower than you could have, that gap compounds, and over ten years it can add up to the price of a car.

A polite, well-supported counter rarely costs anyone an offer. What it usually costs is a day or two and one slightly nervous phone call. That is a small price for a raise that keeps paying for years.

Research your salary range first

You cannot negotiate well with a number you invented on the drive home. Before any talk of money, spend about an hour building a realistic range from several sources and look for where they overlap:

  • Job listings for similar roles in your area, especially ones that post pay ranges.
  • Salary comparison websites and industry or professional association surveys.
  • Government labor statistics, which often publish pay by occupation and region.
  • Recruiters who place people in your field.
  • People in similar roles, asked tactfully: "What range would you expect for a role like this?" rather than "What do you earn?"

From that research, write down three numbers. The low end is what people with less experience than you get. The middle is typical for someone like you. The high end is what strong candidates with your skills earn at well-paying employers.

Pay transparency rules are changing fast. Some US states and cities, parts of Canada and several European countries now require employers to post a salary range or share one on request, and some places limit questions about your salary history. Check your government's employment or labor department website for the rules where the job is based.

Set your target, opening and walk-away point

Three numbers do most of the work in any salary negotiation. Write them down before the conversation, because under pressure, with a friendly recruiter on the line, limits tend to slide.

NumberWhat it meansExample
Walk-away pointThe lowest figure you would accept62,000
TargetA result you would be pleased with and can justify68,000
OpeningYour first ask, a little above target71,000

Your walk-away point comes from your best alternative: what you will do if this offer does not work out. That might be another offer, or staying in your current job at your current pay. Strengthen that alternative before you talk, for example by keeping other applications moving. A stronger alternative makes you calmer, and the other side can usually sense it.

Your target usually sits between the middle and the high end of your research. Your walk-away point stays private. Keeping it to yourself is perfectly honest; just never invent an alternative you do not have.

What to say when asked for salary expectations

The question "What are your salary expectations?" often arrives early, sometimes on the first screening call. You have a few honest options, and none of them require bluffing.

  • If it is very early, ask to learn more about the role first, and ask what range they have budgeted.
  • If they posted a range, respond to it.
  • If they press, give a researched range where the bottom is a number you would be genuinely happy with.

That last point matters. People tend to remember the bottom of any range you give, so never say a figure you could not live with.

A script you can borrow

You: "I'd love to learn a bit more about the responsibilities before I put a number on it. Could you share the range budgeted for this role?"

Them: "We really need a figure from you to move forward."

You: "Based on what I've seen for similar roles in this area, I'm looking at something in the range of 68,000 to 75,000, depending on the full package. Does that fit with what you had in mind?"

Ending with a question keeps the conversation moving and invites them to share their side. The ideal time to discuss exact numbers is after they decide they want you and before you say yes.

How to counter a salary offer step by step

  1. Thank them warmly. Show you are excited about the role and the team.
  2. Ask for the offer in writing and a day or two to review it. That is normal and reasonable.
  3. Compare the offer to your target, walk-away point and any other options.
  4. If it is below target, counter with one specific number. A precise figure like 74,500 sounds researched; a round number can sound like a guess.
  5. Give one or two reasons tied to value, not your personal costs. "My rent went up" is true for everyone. "I bring five years with the exact system your team is moving to" is about them.
  6. Stop talking and let them think.
  7. If they come back lower, consider trading rather than simply accepting.

What the counter can sound like

"Thank you so much, I'm really excited about this role. I've looked over the offer carefully. Given my experience leading similar projects and the market range for this position, I was hoping for a base salary of 74,500. Is there flexibility there?"

If they reply that the best they can do is 70,000, you might say: "I appreciate you checking. If the base is fixed at 70,000, could we look at a signing bonus or an earlier salary review, at six months instead of twelve?"

Do not accept on the spot and then try to negotiate later. Once you say "yes, I accept," reopening the deal can feel like a broken promise. Try "Thank you, I'm excited. Could I have until Thursday to review the details?" instead.

Negotiate the whole package, not just pay

When a hiring manager says the base is fixed, that is often true. Pay bands, budgets and internal fairness rules are real. What is less fixed is everything around the salary, and some of it can be worth more to you than a small raise.

ItemWhat to ask
Signing bonusA one-time payment to close a gap
Start dateA break between jobs or a later start
FlexibilityRemote days, hours, compressed weeks
Vacation (UK: holiday)Extra days or credit for experience
TrainingCourses, certifications, conference budget
Review timingAn earlier pay review with clear goals
Job titleA title that matches your level

Pick the two or three items that matter most to you. A list of twelve requests reads like a shopping trip, while a short list shows you have thought about what you need.

Benefits such as health insurance, retirement or pension contributions, bonuses and stock vary enormously by country and employer. Ask for the full details in writing and compare total value, not just the headline figure.

Use a competing offer honestly

A second offer, or a counteroffer from your current employer, strengthens your walk-away point. Both call for honesty. You can say plainly: "I've received another offer with a higher base, but I'd genuinely prefer to join your team. Is there anything you can do on salary or the package to make this work?"

If they ask who the other offer is from, it is fine to keep that confidential and share only the figure and the type of role. Never claim an offer you do not have. Recruiters talk, and a bluff that gets called ends badly.

If your current employer counters, think about why you started looking. If it was only pay, the counter may solve it. If it was the manager, the work or a lack of growth, more money often postpones the problem by about a year.

Stay calm when they push back

Objections are a normal part of the process, not a sign you overreached. A simple pattern works almost everywhere: acknowledge what they said, ask a question, then restate what you need or offer a trade.

  • "That's above our budget." Try: "I understand. What flexibility is there elsewhere in the package?"
  • "We need an answer today." Try: "I want to give this the attention it deserves. Could I come back to you by tomorrow afternoon?"
  • "Everyone at this level gets the same." Try: "That makes sense. Could we agree an earlier review once I've hit clear goals?"

Silence helps too. The pause after your counter feels long, but it is where the other person does their thinking. Resist the urge to fill it by lowering your own number.

If the final offer is below your walk-away point, it is fine to decline politely and keep the door open: "Thanks for your time. It doesn't work for me right now, but I'd be glad to talk again."

Get the final offer in writing

Before you resign from your current job, ask for the final offer in writing. It should list your salary, start date, title, benefits and anything special you negotiated, such as a signing bonus or an early review date.

Read it carefully. Verbal promises like "we'll look at your pay in six months" are easy to forget when managers change. If something is missing, ask politely: "Could we add the six-month review we discussed to the letter?"

Employment law, notice periods and contract rules differ by country and state. If anything in the contract worries you, such as limits on future work, check official local guidance or get advice before you sign. This guide is general information, not legal or financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

Is it rude to negotiate a job offer?

No. Most employers expect some discussion, and many leave a little room in the first offer for exactly that reason. What matters is tone. Thank them, show you are excited about the role, then ask with one specific, researched number and a short reason. A polite counter rarely costs anyone an offer, while accepting too quickly can cost you for years.

How much should I ask for when negotiating salary?

Base your ask on research, not a gut feeling. Find the typical range for the role in your area, set a target between the middle and high end, then open a little above that target. Asking far above the market rate damages your credibility. A counter of roughly 5 to 10 percent above the offer is common when your research supports it.

Should I give a salary range or a single number?

A single specific number works best when you counter an offer, because it sounds researched and gives them something clear to answer. A range can help early in the process, when you know little about the role. If you give a range, make sure the bottom is a figure you would be genuinely happy with, because people tend to anchor on the lowest number they hear.

Can an employer withdraw an offer if I negotiate?

It can happen, but it is rare when you ask politely and stay within a researched range. Offers are more often withdrawn after aggressive demands, bluffs about other offers or long silences. Keep your tone warm, give a reason tied to the value you bring and ask an open question such as "Is there flexibility there?" so the conversation stays collaborative.

What if they say the salary is not negotiable?

Believe them on the base and ask about the package instead. A signing bonus, a later start date, extra vacation days, remote work, a training budget or an earlier pay review can all be easier for an employer to approve. Choose the two or three that matter most to you, ask for them clearly and get anything agreed added to the written offer.

How long can I take to respond to a job offer?

A day or two is normal and reasonable for most roles, and asking for it shows you take the decision seriously. Say something like "Thank you, I'm excited. Could I have until Thursday to review the details?" If they push for an answer on the spot, a calm reply is that you want to give the offer the attention it deserves.

Your first step today is simple: spend one hour building a salary range for the role you want from at least three sources, then write down your walk-away point, target and opening before anyone asks you for a number. With those three figures on paper and one script ready, the next offer conversation becomes a calm exchange rather than a guess.
