How to Go Vegan: A Gentle Step by Step Guide for Beginners

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have decided to go vegan, or someone in your house just announced they are done with meat, the hardest part is often knowing what to do first. The short answer: change gradually, one meal or one day at a time, replace the protein in meals you already love with beans, lentils or tofu, and sort out a reliable source of vitamin B12 from day one.

This guide walks you through a simple four week plan, the balanced vegan plate, the nutrients that need a little planning, a beginner pantry, the hidden animal ingredients to watch for on labels and the mistakes almost every new vegan makes in the first month.

What going vegan actually means

A vegan diet leaves out all foods that come from animals: meat, poultry, fish and seafood, and also eggs, dairy (milk, butter, cheese, yogurt and cream), honey and gelatin. Many people extend the idea to clothing and household products, but this guide is about your plate.

Vegetarian is different. Most vegetarians still eat eggs and dairy, so a vegetable lasagna with ricotta, or an omelet, is vegetarian but not vegan. If you are cooking for a vegan friend, that difference matters.

People go vegan for many reasons, and most have more than one: the way animals are raised, the environmental impact of food, wanting to eat more vegetables and beans, or simply cost, because beans, lentils and oats are some of the cheapest foods in the shop. Every reason is a fair one.

On health, studies suggest that well-planned vegan diets can be healthy for adults and are linked with eating more fiber, vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains. A vegan diet built mostly on white bread, fries (chips) and processed snacks can be unhealthy, though, and going vegan does not cure anything or guarantee weight loss. Planning is what makes the difference.

Go vegan gradually with a four week plan

You can switch overnight, and some people do it happily. Most beginners find a gradual change easier. It gives your taste buds time to adjust, your gut time to get used to more fiber and your cooking skills time to catch up.

WeekWhat changesNew habit
Week 1Vegan dinnersChoose a B12 source and start it
Week 2Add vegan lunchesSwitch to a fortified unsweetened plant milk
Week 3Add savory vegan breakfastsRead labels for hidden animal ingredients
Week 4Fully plant-basedBatch cook and freeze two meals

Other gradual routes work too. Try one fully vegan day a week, then two, then more. Or change one food group at a time: plant milk first, then meat at dinner, then cheese last, since cheese is the one many people miss most.

Do not throw out every non-vegan food in the house on day one. It wastes money and leaves you with an empty cupboard. Use up what you have, or pass it on, and restock with vegan staples as you go. Swap the protein in meals you already love

The easiest vegan meals are versions of the ones your household already eats. You do not need dozens of new dishes, just a few familiar ones with the protein replaced.

Instead ofTry
Spaghetti bologneseLentil bolognese
Chili con carneThree-bean chili
Shepherd's pieLentils under the mash
Chicken stir-fryTofu or edamame stir-fry
Meat curryChickpea, lentil or potato curry
Beef tacosBlack bean or tempeh tacos
Scrambled eggsTofu scramble with turmeric and spinach
Mac and cheeseButternut squash and nutritional yeast sauce

The key is to replace the protein, not just remove it. Leaving the meat off and piling on extra pasta or salad leaves most people hungry again within an hour.

Two quick ideas to start with. For a tofu scramble, crumble firm tofu into a hot pan with a little oil, add turmeric, garlic powder, a splash of soy sauce and a handful of spinach, and serve on toast. For a fast chickpea curry, simmer a can of chickpeas with canned tomatoes, curry powder, onion and spinach for 15 minutes and serve with rice.

Build a balanced vegan plate

Picture your plate in rough sections:

  • About half vegetables and fruit.
  • About a quarter protein foods: beans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu, tempeh, edamame or seitan.
  • About a quarter whole grains or starchy foods: brown rice, whole wheat pasta, oats, potatoes or whole grain bread.
  • A small amount of healthy fat: nuts, seeds, avocado, or olive or canola (rapeseed) oil.
  • A fortified unsweetened plant milk or yogurt somewhere in your day.

Protein worries most new vegans, and the answer is reassuring. A variety of beans, lentils, peas, soy foods, seitan, nuts, seeds and whole grains provides enough. You do not need to combine proteins at every meal, because variety across the day is enough. A simple habit is a protein food at every meal: tofu scramble at breakfast, a chickpea sandwich at lunch, lentil bolognese at dinner. Protein powders are not needed.

Go easy on processed vegan sausages, burgers and cheeses. They are handy sometimes, but many are high in salt and fat. Use them as a bridge, not a foundation.

Vitamin B12, iron, calcium and other nutrients

A few nutrients need a little planning on a vegan diet. This is the part beginners most often skip.

Vitamin B12 comes first. It is not reliably found in plant foods, so vegans must get it from fortified foods (some plant milks, yogurts, breakfast cereals, nutritional yeast and yeast extract spreads; check each label) or from a B12 supplement, every day or as advised. Seaweed, mushrooms, tempeh and spirulina are not reliable sources. Long-term lack of B12 can cause anemia and nerve damage, and symptoms can take years to show, so do not wait for them. Ask a pharmacist which option suits you.

A few practical tips. Eat iron-rich foods with peppers, tomatoes, citrus or broccoli, and drink tea and coffee between meals rather than with them. Shake fortified plant milk cartons, because calcium settles at the bottom, and note that organic versions are often not fortified. Do not rely on seaweed for iodine, as some types can give far too much. One or two Brazil nuts now and then is plenty for selenium.

Who should get advice before going vegan

General advice suits most healthy adults, but some people need a plan made for them. Talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before or when you change to a vegan diet if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, feeding babies, children or teenagers, an older adult, living with a condition such as kidney disease, osteoporosis, diabetes or a thyroid condition, have a history of eating disorders, or take regular medicines.

Never put a baby on a homemade or plant milk diet in place of breast milk or infant formula. See a doctor if you have ongoing tiredness, pins and needles, a sore tongue, memory problems, pale skin, shortness of breath or simply feel unwell for more than a week or two, and mention that you eat vegan so they can consider checking B12 and iron. Ask a doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement, and do not take iron tablets unless a doctor advises it.

Many people find a yearly check-up a good moment to ask about B12, iron and vitamin D, especially in the first year.

Set up a cheap beginner vegan pantry

A vegan diet can be one of the cheapest ways to eat, or one of the most expensive. Meals built on beans, lentils, grains and vegetables cost little. Meals built on specialty cheeses and ready meals add up fast.

  • Canned (tinned) chickpeas, black beans, kidney beans, white beans and butter beans
  • Dried red lentils, green or brown lentils and yellow split peas
  • Rice, egg-free pasta, rolled oats, couscous, barley and noodles
  • Canned tomatoes, tomato paste (purée), coconut milk, peanut butter and tahini
  • Soy sauce, miso, nutritional yeast, smoked paprika, cumin, curry powder and garlic powder
  • Pumpkin seeds, ground flaxseed and walnuts
  • Fridge: firm tofu, fortified unsweetened plant milk and yogurt, lemons, ginger and garlic
  • Freezer: peas, spinach, broccoli, corn and shelled edamame

Choose store brand (own label) staples, cook in batches and freeze portions. Red lentils cook to a soft purée in 15 to 20 minutes, and canned beans are ready to use straight away.

Raw or undercooked kidney beans are toxic. If you cook dried kidney beans, soak them, then boil hard for at least 10 minutes before simmering until tender, and never cook them from raw in a slow cooker. Canned kidney beans are already cooked and safe.

Hidden animal ingredients on food labels

Plenty of breads, chips (crisps), sauces and stock cubes are accidentally vegan, and plenty of others are not. Read the ingredients list each time you buy, because recipes change.

  • Dairy: whey, casein, caseinates, lactose, milk powder, butter and ghee
  • Egg, including egg in fresh pasta and some noodles
  • Gelatin, lard, suet and honey
  • Anchovies in some Worcestershire sauce, Caesar dressing, pesto and curry pastes
  • Fish sauce and shrimp paste, common in Thai curry pastes
  • Chicken or beef stock in soups, sauces and seasonings
  • Carmine (cochineal), a red coloring, and shellac, a glaze
  • Some vitamin D3, and some wines and beers fined with animal products

A vegan label is helpful, but it is not a guarantee against cross-contact if you have an allergy. When eating out, ask plainly: "Could you tell me if this is made without butter, cream or egg?" Kitchens are usually happy to help.

Common mistakes when you first go vegan

  1. Not eating enough. Plant foods are often less dense in energy than meat and cheese, so the same-looking plate can leave you hungry. Eat generous portions, include protein at every meal and add nuts, seeds or avocado.
  2. Living on pasta and toast. Comforting, but not a full meal. Add beans, lentils or tofu and plenty of vegetables.
  3. Skipping B12. This is the big one. Sort it out from the first week.
  4. Adding fiber too fast. Increase beans gradually, rinse canned beans and drink plenty of water to reduce wind (gas) and bloating. Red lentils and tofu are often gentler at first.
  5. Expecting perfection. You may find milk powder in your favorite chips or eat something at a party with butter in it. Learn from it and carry on.

If you share a home with meat eaters, cook a vegan base that everyone eats, like a curry, chili or pasta sauce, and let others add their own extras. Build-your-own meals such as tacos, burrito bowls and baked potatoes keep everyone happy.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest way to go vegan?

For most beginners, the easiest way is gradual. Start with vegan dinners for a week, then add lunches, then breakfasts. Replace the protein in meals you already like, for example lentil bolognese instead of beef bolognese, and set up a vitamin B12 source in the first week. Small, steady steps tend to last longer than an overnight clear-out.

Do vegans need to take B12?

Yes, vegans need a reliable source of vitamin B12, because it is not reliably found in plant foods. That means fortified foods such as some plant milks, cereals and nutritional yeast, checked on the label, or a B12 supplement taken every day or as advised. Ask a doctor or pharmacist which option suits you, and do not wait for symptoms.

Where do vegans get protein?

From beans, lentils, chickpeas, peas, tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk, seitan, nuts, seeds and whole grains. You do not need to combine proteins at each meal; eating a variety across the day is enough. A simple habit is to include one protein food at every meal and snack. Protein powders are not needed for most people.

What do vegans eat for breakfast?

Savory options include tofu scramble with spinach and peppers, a chickpea flour omelet, smashed avocado and white bean toast, breakfast burritos with black beans and potatoes, or savory oatmeal (porridge) made with fortified plant milk. Adding a protein food and a fortified plant milk at breakfast is an easy way to cover several nutrients early in the day.

Is a vegan diet expensive?

It can be very cheap if you build meals on beans, lentils, rice, pasta, oats, frozen vegetables and store brand staples. It becomes expensive when meals rely on specialty vegan cheeses, ready meals and meat alternatives. Dried beans cost least, canned beans save time, and batch cooking with freezing stops costly takeout on busy nights.

Why do I feel bloated after going vegan?

A vegan diet usually contains much more fiber than most people are used to, and a sudden jump can cause wind and bloating. Increase beans and lentils gradually, rinse canned beans well and drink plenty of water. Red lentils and tofu are often easier at first. Most people settle within a few weeks; see a doctor if symptoms are severe or do not improve.

Today, choose your vitamin B12 source and plan three vegan dinners for this week by swapping the protein in meals your household already enjoys. Once those feel easy, add lunches next week and keep going one step at a time.
