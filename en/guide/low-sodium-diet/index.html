Low Sodium Diet: How to Eat Less Salt Without Bland Food

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your doctor has just told you to cut down on salt, you may be staring at the shaker wondering how food will ever taste good again. The short answer: a low sodium diet usually means keeping sodium under about 2,300 mg a day (roughly 1 teaspoon of salt from everything you eat), or lower if your doctor advises, and most of that sodium comes from packaged and restaurant food, not the shaker.

This guide shows you how much sodium is too much, where it hides, how to read labels in the US and UK, how to make food taste big without salt, how to eat out and a sample day of meals. Your taste buds adjust faster than you might expect.

Why a low sodium diet matters

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against your blood vessel walls. When it stays too high for too long, it strains your heart and blood vessels and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney problems. High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms, which is why it is often found at a routine checkup.

When you eat a lot of sodium, your body holds on to extra water to keep the balance in your blood steady. That extra fluid increases the volume of blood your heart has to push, which can raise the pressure. People respond to salt differently, but for many people with high blood pressure, eating less salt is one of the simplest changes they can make, and it costs nothing.

Cutting salt is not a cure, and it does not replace medication. It works alongside staying active, limiting alcohol, not smoking and the rest of your doctor's advice.

How much sodium per day

Salt is sodium chloride, and sodium makes up about 40 percent of it by weight. US labels usually list sodium in milligrams, while UK labels list salt in grams.

MeasureAbout how much
Sodium in 1 teaspoon of salt2,300 mg
General adult limitUnder 2,300 mg sodium a day
Lower target for many with high blood pressureAbout 1,500 mg sodium a day
UK adult limit6 g salt (about 2.4 g sodium) a day

To turn sodium into salt, multiply by about 2.5. To turn salt into sodium, divide by about 2.5. So 1 g of sodium is about 2.5 g of salt.

These are general figures. Your own doctor's target is the one that counts, so ask for your number and write it down. Sea salt, rock salt, flaky salt and pink salt all contain roughly the same sodium by weight as table salt, and garlic salt and celery salt are mostly salt too.

High sodium foods to watch

For most people, the bulk of daily sodium arrives in packets and takeaway boxes. The usual suspects:

  • Bread, rolls and wraps, which are not always salty per slice but are eaten often.
  • Ham, bacon, sausages, salami, hot dogs and deli slices.
  • Cheese, especially hard, aged and processed types.
  • Canned soups, sauces, gravies and stock cubes (bouillon cubes).
  • Pizza, burgers and other takeaway meals, plus ready meals and frozen dinners.
  • Chips (crisps), crackers and salted nuts.
  • Soy sauce, ketchup, pickles and many salad dressings.

That is good news, because it means the biggest wins come from cooking more of your own food. Some easy swaps:

Instead ofTry
Canned soupHomemade soup, or low sodium canned
Stock cubesNo-salt-added broth (stock) or homemade
Jarred pasta sauceNo-salt-added canned tomatoes with garlic and herbs
Deli ham or turkeyHome-roasted chicken or turkey, sliced thin
Salted snacksUnsalted nuts, herb popcorn, roasted chickpeas
Bottled dressingOil with vinegar or lemon, mustard powder and herbs

How to read sodium on food labels

In the US and Canada

Check the serving size first, then the % Daily Value. 5% DV or less per serving is low, 20% or more is high. A can of soup listing about 2 servings doubles every number if you eat the whole can.

  • Low sodium means 140 mg or less per serving.
  • Reduced sodium means at least 25% less than the regular product, which can still be very salty.
  • No salt added means none was added in processing, but the food is not always sodium free.

In the UK, Australia and New Zealand

UK traffic light labels show red (high) for more than 1.5 g of salt per 100 g and green (low) for 0.3 g or less per 100 g, with amber in between. Aim for green and amber, and treat red as an occasional food. Australian and New Zealand labels show sodium per serving and per 100 g, so compare similar products per 100 g.

Wherever you shop, scan the ingredients list. It runs from most to least by weight, so salt in the first few ingredients means a salty product. Watch for monosodium glutamate, sodium bicarbonate and other "sodium" words too.

How to make food taste good without salt

Most of what we call flavor comes from smell, which is why food tastes of nothing when you have a heavy cold. If you turn salty down, turn the other tastes and aromas up.

  • Acid: a squeeze of lemon or lime or a splash of vinegar at the end of cooking does a surprisingly good impression of salt.
  • Aromatics: onions, garlic, celery, carrots, leeks and ginger, cooked slowly until soft and golden.
  • Herbs: dried oregano, thyme and rosemary early in cooking; fresh parsley, basil, cilantro (coriander), dill and chives at the end.
  • Toasted spices: stir cumin, paprika or curry powder into hot oil for 30 seconds before adding liquid.
  • Umami and browning: browned mushrooms, tomato paste (purée) cooked until it darkens, seared meat and well-roasted vegetables.
  • Crunch: toasted unsalted nuts or seeds, crisp raw vegetables or roasted chickpeas.

When a dish tastes flat, work through this order before reaching for salt: a squeeze of acid, a pinch more spice or pepper, a handful of fresh herbs, then something crunchy. Taste after each step.

A simple all-purpose blend: mix garlic powder, onion powder, dried oregano, smoked paprika and black pepper, and keep it by the stove. Check spice blends before you buy them, since many list salt as the first ingredient.

How to cut salt gradually

Going from a heavy hand to none overnight is hard. Your taste buds usually adjust to less salt over a few weeks, so step down:

  1. For the first week or two, halve the salt in your usual recipes and take the shaker off the table.
  2. Halve it again and lean harder on lemon, garlic and herbs.
  3. Move to recipes with no added salt or just a pinch for a whole dish.
  4. Meanwhile, swap one packaged or takeaway food at a time for a homemade version.

Expect the first two weeks to taste a bit bland. That is normal and it passes. Around the fourth week, many people find an old salty favorite tastes far too salty.

If you do use a little salt, sprinkle a few grains on the surface of food just before eating rather than stirring it into the pot. It hits your tongue directly, so a tiny amount tastes saltier. And watch out for replacing salt with salty sauces: a splash of soy sauce or a stock cube can add more sodium than the salt you left out.

A sample low sodium day

Low sodium eating fits naturally into the mainstream heart-healthy pattern: plenty of vegetables and fruit, whole grains, beans and lentils, unsalted nuts, low-fat dairy, and fish and poultry more often than red and processed meat.

MealIdea
BreakfastSavory oats with mushrooms and scallions, or a herby vegetable omelet
LunchSmashed chickpea salad sandwich with lemon and herbs, plus fruit
SnackUnsalted nuts or herb popcorn
DinnerLemon garlic herb roast chicken with roasted broccoli and brown rice

For the sandwich, mash rinsed no-salt-added chickpeas with a spoon of plain yogurt, lemon juice, chopped celery, dill and black pepper. Rinsing regular canned beans and vegetables under cold water removes some sodium if no-salt-added versions are not available. Eating out on a low salt diet

Restaurant food is often very salty because salt is cheap and makes food taste good fast. You can still eat out with a few habits:

  • Check the menu online and pick something simple.
  • Choose grilled, roasted, steamed or baked dishes over fried, cured, smoked or breaded ones.
  • Ask for sauces, gravies and dressings on the side.
  • Ask whether your dish can be cooked without added salt.
  • Swap fries (chips) for a plain baked potato, salad or steamed vegetables.
  • Go easy on soup, pizza, burgers, cheese plates and anything in soy or teriyaki sauce.

A short, friendly request works in most places: "I'm cutting down on salt for my blood pressure. Could the chef cook my fish without added salt and bring the sauce on the side?" One salty meal will not undo your progress, so get back to your usual way of eating at the next meal, without guilt.

Safety notes and when to get help

Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before changing your diet, and tell them you are cutting salt, since it can change your blood pressure readings if you take medication. Never stop or change a medicine on your own. Check before using any salt substitute: many contain potassium chloride, which can be dangerous with kidney disease or with medicines that raise potassium. People with low blood pressure, heavy sweating, some heart failure or kidney plans, or pregnancy should follow their own care team's sodium target.

Call 911 in the US or 999 in the UK straight away for a very high blood pressure reading, around 180/120 or higher, together with chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness or weakness, a change in vision or difficulty speaking. Do the same for signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech) or a heart attack (chest pain or pressure spreading to the arm, jaw or back, breathlessness, cold sweat or nausea).

If you get a high reading but feel well, sit quietly for a few minutes, recheck and contact your doctor as advised.

Frequently asked questions

How much sodium should I have a day?

Most adults are advised to keep sodium under about 2,300 mg a day, which is roughly 1 teaspoon of salt from all sources. Many people with high blood pressure are advised to aim lower, at about 1,500 mg. In the UK the advice is no more than 6 g of salt a day, about 2.4 g of sodium. Ask your doctor for your own target.

What foods are highest in sodium?

The biggest sources are usually processed and restaurant foods: cured and deli meats, bacon, sausages, cheese, canned soups, stock cubes, sauces, pizza, burgers, ready meals, salty snacks and condiments such as soy sauce, ketchup and pickles. Bread adds up too because it is eaten often. Cooking more meals from fresh ingredients is the quickest way to cut sodium.

Is sea salt or pink salt better than table salt?

Not for your blood pressure. Sea salt, rock salt, flaky salt and pink salt are all still sodium chloride, with roughly the same sodium by weight as table salt. Larger crystals can mean slightly less per teaspoon, but the difference is small. Garlic salt and celery salt are mostly salt too, so use garlic powder and celery seed instead.

Are salt substitutes safe?

Many salt substitutes and lite salt products replace sodium with potassium chloride, which can be dangerous for people with kidney disease or who take certain blood pressure, heart or other medicines that raise potassium. Check with your doctor or pharmacist before using one. Even when they are safe, they keep you used to a strong salty taste, so herbs, spices and acid often work better long term.

How long does it take to get used to less salt?

Most people find their taste buds adjust over a few weeks. The first two weeks can taste a little bland, which is normal. Cutting salt gradually, halving it, then halving it again, makes the change easier. By about the fourth week, many people notice that foods they used to love now taste too salty.

Does a low sodium diet lower blood pressure?

For many people with high blood pressure, eating less sodium helps lower it, though people respond differently depending on age, genes, kidney health and other conditions. It works best alongside a heart-healthy eating pattern, regular activity, limiting alcohol and any medication your doctor prescribes. It is not a cure and should not replace treatment.

Your first step today: ask your doctor or pharmacist for your personal daily sodium target, then look at the labels on three foods you eat often, such as bread, canned beans and broth (stock), and choose the lower sodium option next time you shop. Keep a lemon and a jar of salt-free seasoning by the stove so the low salt choice is also the tasty one.
