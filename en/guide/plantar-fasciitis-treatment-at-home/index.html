Plantar Fasciitis Treatment at Home: A Step by Step Heel Pain Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your first steps out of bed feel like stepping on a stone, and the pain eases a little as you move only to come back after a long day on your feet, you are dealing with one of the most common foot problems there is. The short answer is that most people get better with simple home measures: stretch your calf and foot before your first steps, strengthen your calves gradually, wear supportive cushioned shoes, avoid going barefoot on hard floors, and ease back (without stopping) on whatever flares it.

Recovery often takes several months, so this guide gives you a plan you can stick with: the morning routine, step by step stretches and strength exercises, shoe tips, a simple way to judge how much to do, a 6-week plan and the warning signs to watch for.

What is plantar fasciitis?

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue under your foot that runs from your heel to your toes and supports your arch. Plantar fasciitis (also called plantar heel pain) is irritation where this band attaches to the heel.

The typical signs are pain under the heel that is worst with the first steps in the morning or after sitting, often easing a little as you get going, then aching again after a long day. Common contributors include:

  • A sudden increase in standing, walking or running
  • Hard floors and long shifts on your feet
  • Worn, flat or unsupportive shoes
  • Tight calf muscles
  • Extra body weight and getting older

Heel spurs often show up on X-rays, but they are common in people without any pain and are usually not the cause. Most people do not need a scan.

Check first. This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor (GP), podiatrist (chiropodist), physical therapist (UK: physiotherapist) or pharmacist before starting new exercises or medicines, especially if your symptoms are severe or new. If you have diabetes, check your feet daily and speak to your doctor or podiatrist before trying ice or new exercises.

Ease the morning heel pain before your first steps

Your first steps are usually the worst moment of the day, so a small routine here pays off fastest. Keep a towel, scarf or long belt by your bed. Before you get up:

  1. Sit up with your legs out straight in front of you.
  2. Loop the towel around the ball of your sore foot, holding one end in each hand.
  3. Keep your knee straight and gently pull, so your toes come back toward your shin. Feel the stretch in your calf and under your foot.
  4. Hold for about 20 to 30 seconds, breathing normally. Repeat 3 times on each foot.
  5. Draw 10 slow circles with each foot in each direction, then point and flex 10 times.

Put supportive slippers or shoes right where your feet land, so you never step onto a bare floor. Push up from the bed with your hands and take the first few steps slowly. A quick version of the same routine helps after sitting during the day too.

Score your first steps each morning from 0 to 10 and jot it down. This number becomes your best guide to how much activity your heel can handle.

Plantar fasciitis stretches step by step

Hold each stretch for about 20 to 30 seconds and repeat 3 times, a few times a day. Move gently, breathe normally and stop if you feel sharp pain, numbness or tingling.

  • Calf stretch with a straight knee. Face a wall with your hands on it. Step the sore foot back, keep that leg straight and the heel down, and lean in until you feel a stretch in the calf.
  • Calf stretch with a bent knee. In the same position, bend both knees while keeping the back heel down. You will feel it lower in the calf.
  • Plantar fascia stretch. Sit and cross the sore foot over the other knee. Hold your toes and pull them back toward your shin until you feel a stretch along the arch.
  • Rolling the arch. Sit and roll your foot slowly over a ball or a frozen water bottle, from heel to toes, for 1 to 2 minutes. Keep the pressure comfortable.
  • Ankle circles. Lift one foot and draw slow circles at the ankle, 10 each way.

A 5-minute morning routine might be the towel stretch in bed, ankle circles, then the two wall calf stretches once you are up in your shoes. Repeat the calf stretches at lunchtime and in the evening.

Strengthening exercises for heel pain

Stretching eases pain, but strength helps your foot and calf cope with the load of daily life. Do these every other day, holding a counter or sturdy chair for anything standing, and build up slowly.

  1. Seated heel raises. Sit with feet flat, lift your heels, then lower slowly. Start with 2 sets of 10.
  2. Towel scrunches. Sitting, curl your toes to pull a towel toward you, 2 or 3 times each foot.
  3. Toe pick ups. Pick up small objects such as marbles or socks with your toes and drop them into a bowl.
  4. Short foot exercise. With your toes relaxed, gently draw the ball of your foot toward your heel so your arch lifts a little. Hold 5 seconds, 10 times.
  5. Standing heel raises. Holding the counter, rise up on both feet and lower slowly, 2 or 3 sets of 8 to 15.
  6. Single leg heel raises. When standing raises feel easy, rise on one foot at a time, starting with 5.
  7. Balance on one foot. Stand beside a counter and lift one foot for up to 30 seconds.

Later on, slow heel raises on a step with a rolled towel under your toes can add more challenge, holding the rail. The main rule is to progress only when your morning score is steady.

Best shoes for plantar fasciitis

Shoes make a big difference to how your heel feels by the end of the day. Look for:

  • Good cushioning under the heel
  • Arch support that feels comfortable rather than pushy
  • A slightly raised heel, rather than completely flat
  • A firm heel counter (the back of the shoe should not squash flat when you press it)
  • Room for your toes and a secure fastening such as laces or a strap

Avoid walking barefoot on hard floors, flimsy flats, flip-flops and very worn shoes. At home, wear supportive slippers or indoor shoes. Off the shelf heel pads or insoles from a pharmacy (chemist) help some people, and a podiatrist can advise on custom insoles if needed. There is no single miracle insole, so judge any change by your morning score over a couple of weeks.

How much walking and standing is okay?

Resting completely rarely helps, but neither does pushing through. The aim is to reduce, not stop, the activities that flare your heel, then build back up in small steps. For a while, you might swap some walking or running for cycling or swimming.

A simple guide is the next-morning rule. Discomfort during activity of up to about 3 or 4 out of 10 is acceptable. Then check your first steps the next morning:

Next morningWhat to do
Same as usual or betterKeep going, or add a little more
A bit worse, settles within a dayHold steady for now
Clearly worse, or worse for 2 daysEase back to a lower level

Heels often react the next day rather than during activity, so a walk can feel fine at the time and still cause a worse morning. If you stand at work, shift your weight often, do calf pumps, take short sits when you can and stretch on breaks.

A 6-week plantar fasciitis plan

This is a general guide, not a promise. Repeat a week or slow down whenever your mornings get worse.

2Add arch rolling, toe pick ups, short footHold steady and score mornings

Heel pain red flags and when to see a doctor

Get urgent help for any of these:

  • You cannot walk or put weight on the foot after an injury, the foot looks deformed, or you felt a snap at the back of the ankle as if kicked: urgent care the same day.
  • A foot that is suddenly cold, pale, blue or very painful: call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand).
  • A hot, red, swollen foot or joint, especially with fever or feeling unwell: see a doctor urgently.
  • Calf pain, swelling, warmth or redness in one leg: urgent same day care, emergency if you also have chest pain or breathlessness.
  • Numbness, tingling or burning in both feet, a sore that is not healing, or pain at night or at rest: see a doctor.

See a doctor, podiatrist or physical therapist if your heel pain has not improved after a few weeks of home measures, is getting worse or stops you doing daily things. If you have diabetes, any wound, blister, color change or new swelling needs the same day attention of your doctor, diabetes team or podiatrist.

Frequently asked questions

How long does plantar fasciitis take to heal?

Most people get better with simple home measures, but it often takes several months and sometimes longer. Progress is usually gradual, with good and bad mornings along the way. Tracking your first-step pain score helps you see the trend. See a professional if there is no improvement after a few weeks of steady home care.

Is walking good for plantar fasciitis?

Some walking is fine and helps keep you active, but long walks on hard surfaces can flare it. Cut back to shorter walks in supportive shoes, find a level your heel tolerates, then add about 5 minutes at a time if your next-morning pain stays steady. Swap in cycling or swimming while you build back up.

Should I ice plantar fasciitis?

Many people find ice soothing. Use a wrapped cold pack or bag of frozen peas on the heel for about 10 to 15 minutes, or roll your foot over a frozen water bottle. Never put ice straight on skin or on numb skin. If you have diabetes or reduced feeling, ask your podiatrist before using ice.

Why does plantar fasciitis hurt more in the morning?

Overnight, your foot rests with the toes pointing down, so the plantar fascia and calf tighten up. Your first steps then stretch that sore tissue suddenly. Doing a towel stretch and ankle circles in bed before you stand, and stepping straight into supportive slippers, can take much of the sting out.

Can I keep running with plantar fasciitis?

You may not need to stop completely, but most runners need to cut back for a while. Reduce distance and speed, avoid hills and hard surfaces, and use the next-morning rule to judge each run. If mornings get clearly worse, swap to cycling or swimming for a spell, then return gradually by small steps.

Do heel spurs cause plantar fasciitis pain?

Usually not. Heel spurs are bony growths that often show up on X-rays, but many people have them without any heel pain. The pain of plantar fasciitis comes from the irritated tissue under the heel, which is why stretching, strengthening, good shoes and load management are the main treatments.

Your first step today is to put a towel by your bed and supportive slippers where your feet land. Tomorrow morning, do three towel stretches on each foot before you stand, score your first steps out of 10, and add the wall calf stretches later in the day.
