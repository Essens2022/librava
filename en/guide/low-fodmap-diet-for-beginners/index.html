Low FODMAP Diet for Beginners: The 3 Phases Explained Step by Step

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your doctor has diagnosed IBS and mentioned a low-FODMAP diet, you are probably staring at a long list of banned foods and wondering how anyone cooks dinner without onion or garlic. The short answer: a low-FODMAP diet is a short-term learning tool with three phases. You swap high-FODMAP foods for low-FODMAP ones for 2 to 6 weeks, test food groups one at a time, then bring back everything your gut tolerates.

It is not a forever diet, and it does not help everyone, but many people with IBS find it calms bloating, pain, wind (gas), diarrhea or constipation. Below you will find what FODMAPs are, each phase in plain steps, the swaps that make cooking easy and the warning signs that need a doctor.

Get a diagnosis before you start

IBS symptoms overlap with other conditions, including celiac (coeliac) disease and inflammatory bowel disease. So the first step is a proper diagnosis from your doctor, not a diet.

One detail trips people up. Celiac testing only works while you are still eating gluten regularly, and the low-FODMAP diet cuts down on wheat. Get tested before you start, or your results may look falsely normal.

Ideally, follow the diet with a registered dietitian trained in the low-FODMAP approach. They can tailor it to you, keep your diet balanced and guide you through reintroduction, which is the part most people find hardest alone.

Check with your doctor first. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor or dietitian before changing your diet, especially if you are pregnant, have diabetes, are underweight, are under 18 or have had an eating disorder. Never change or stop medicines because of the diet, and ask your pharmacist if a medicine contains fillers such as sorbitol or lactose.

What are FODMAPs in plain words

FODMAPs are short-chain carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed in the small intestine. They draw water into the gut and are fermented by gut bacteria, which makes gas. In a sensitive gut, that can mean pain, bloating and changes in bowel habit.

LetterGroupEveryday examples
OOligosaccharides (fructans and GOS)Wheat, onion, garlic, beans, lentils
DDisaccharides (lactose)Cow's milk, soft cheese, regular yogurt
MMonosaccharides (excess fructose)Honey, mango, apples
PPolyols (sorbitol and mannitol)Avocado, blackberries, mushrooms, cauliflower

The F stands for fermentable, and the A simply means and. These are not bad or unhealthy foods. Onions, garlic, beans and wheat feed the helpful bacteria in your gut, which is exactly why the strict phase is meant to be temporary.

Habits to try first or alongside

Before or alongside a low-FODMAP diet, many people try simple everyday changes. Some find these are enough on their own.

  • Eat regular meals and avoid skipping meals or eating very late
  • Eat slowly and chew well
  • Limit caffeine, alcohol and fizzy drinks
  • Cut back on very fatty or very spicy food
  • Change fiber gradually rather than all at once
  • Drink enough water through the day
  • Stay active and find ways to manage stress

Food intolerance tests sold directly to the public are not recommended for diagnosing IBS. A symptom diary and a dietitian will tell you far more.

Phase one, elimination for 2 to 6 weeks

For about 2 to 6 weeks you swap high-FODMAP foods for low-FODMAP alternatives. The key word is swap. You are not cutting out food groups, you are choosing different foods within each group so your symptoms settle and you have a clear baseline.

Keep fiber up with oats, potatoes in their skins, brown rice, quinoa, carrots and a little chia or flaxseed. Plan your dinners before you shop and start a symptom diary from day one.

If you have followed elimination carefully for about 6 weeks with no improvement, stop and talk to your doctor or dietitian. Do not cut out more foods on your own. The diet does not work for everyone, and staying strict longer will not change that.

Why portion size matters so much

Many foods are low FODMAP at a small portion and high FODMAP at a larger one. Canned, rinsed chickpeas and lentils, zucchini (courgette), broccoli, sweet potato, coconut milk, almonds and avocado all fall into this group.

That is why a dish can be perfectly fine as one serving and cause trouble as two. During elimination, stick to one measured serving rather than going back for seconds of the borderline foods.

FODMAP information is updated as foods are retested, so use a reliable, regularly updated low-FODMAP food guide, such as one developed by a university research team, to check exact portions. Printed lists from a few years ago can be out of date.

Phase two, reintroduction one group at a time

This is where the real learning happens. You keep eating your low-FODMAP base diet and add just one test food, so if symptoms appear you know where they came from.

  1. Day 1: eat a small portion of the test food with a meal.
  2. Day 2: if you feel fine, eat a medium portion.
  3. Day 3: if you still feel fine, eat a normal-sized portion.
  4. Stop the test as soon as you get clear symptoms.
  5. Leave 2 to 3 symptom-free washout days on your base diet before the next test.

Common test foods include wheat bread for wheat fructans, garlic and onion tested separately, lentils or chickpeas for GOS, milk or yogurt for lactose, honey or mango for fructose, avocado or blackberries for sorbitol, and mushrooms or cauliflower for mannitol. Your dietitian will help you choose.

Take Maria. She eats one slice of wheat bread on Monday and feels fine, two slices on Tuesday and feels fine, then a full bowl of pasta on Wednesday and wakes up bloated. Her result is useful: two slices of bread suit her, a big serving does not, at least for now.

Look for patterns, not single bad days. Stress, poor sleep, illness and your menstrual cycle can all affect IBS. If a result is unclear, repeat the test later.

Phase three, personalization for the long term

In this phase you bring back every food you tolerated and keep only your personal triggers limited, at amounts you can handle. The goal is the widest, most varied diet you can enjoy, not the strictest one.

Make a simple list with three columns: foods you eat freely, foods you enjoy in smaller amounts and foods you keep limited for now. Stick it inside a cupboard door. If onion turned out fine, add it back to your stews. If lactose bothers you only in large amounts, a splash of ordinary milk in tea may be fine.

Every few months, try a little of a food you once reacted to. Tolerances can change, and many people find their limits grow over time.

How to cook without onion and garlic

Fructans dissolve in water but not in fat. That means garlic and onion flavor moves into oil without the fructans, as long as every piece is removed. Store-bought garlic-infused oil is the safest and easiest choice.

Homemade garlic oil safety. If you make your own, gently heat sliced garlic in oil, strain out every piece and use it straight away, or refrigerate and use within 4 days, or freeze in small portions. Never keep homemade garlic oil at room temperature, because of the risk of botulism.

Other easy flavor builders:

  • The green parts of scallions (spring onions) and leeks, plus chives
  • Fresh ginger, lemon zest and juice
  • Fresh herbs such as basil, dill, parsley and cilantro (coriander)
  • Smoked paprika, cumin and other single spices
  • A pinch of asafoetida, checked to be wheat free (optional)

On labels, watch for onion or garlic powder hiding in stock cubes, sauces, sausages and seasoning mixes, sometimes listed as spices or natural flavors. Also check for inulin, chicory root fiber, honey, fruit juice concentrates and sweeteners ending in -ol. Low FODMAP is not the same as gluten free, so gluten-free products still need checking.

Cooking for a family? Make one low-FODMAP base meal, then add sautéed onion or garlic for others at the end. Dinners like chicken stir-fry with ginger and scallion greens or salmon with dill and baby potatoes suit everyone. Red flags that need a doctor

See your doctor promptly, before or during the diet, if you notice any of these:

  • Bleeding from the bottom, blood in your stools or black, tarry stools
  • Weight loss you cannot explain, or anemia
  • New symptoms or a lasting change in bowel habit after age 50
  • A family history of bowel cancer, ovarian cancer, celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease
  • A lump in your abdomen or bottom, fever, or diarrhea that wakes you at night
  • Severe or worsening pain, or persistent vomiting
  • For women, persistent bloating on most days

Call emergency services (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) for severe sudden abdominal pain, heavy bleeding or fainting.

Frequently asked questions

How long should you stay on the low FODMAP diet?

The strict elimination phase usually lasts 2 to 6 weeks. After that you move on to reintroduction, then personalization, where you bring back every food you tolerate. The strict phase is not meant to be followed long term, because many high-FODMAP foods feed helpful gut bacteria. If nothing improves after about 6 weeks, stop and talk to your doctor or dietitian.

Can you eat garlic-infused oil on a low FODMAP diet?

Yes. The fructans in garlic dissolve in water but not in fat, so the flavor moves into oil while the fructans stay in the garlic pieces. Use oil with every piece of garlic removed. Store-bought garlic-infused oil is the easiest choice. Homemade versions must be refrigerated and used within 4 days, or frozen, because of the botulism risk.

Is low FODMAP the same as gluten free?

No. The low-FODMAP diet limits wheat because of its fructans, not because of gluten. A small amount of wheat, such as a splash of soy sauce or a little breadcrumb, is usually fine. Gluten-free products can still contain high-FODMAP ingredients like onion, honey or inulin, so read the label. If you might have celiac disease, get tested before cutting down on wheat.

What can I eat for breakfast on a low FODMAP diet?

Plenty. Savory oatmeal (porridge) topped with spinach and a fried egg, an omelet with cheddar and scallion greens, a potato and spinach hash, or eggs on slow-fermented spelt sourdough toast all work during elimination. Use lactose-free milk or yogurt, and keep fruit portions moderate. Oranges and kiwifruit are handy low-FODMAP choices.

Does the low FODMAP diet work for everyone with IBS?

No. Many people with IBS find it reduces symptoms such as bloating, pain and changes in bowel habit, but results vary and it does not help everyone. It also does not cure IBS, which is a long-term condition. If you see no improvement after about 6 weeks of careful elimination, your doctor may revisit the diagnosis or suggest other approaches.

Why test onion and garlic separately during reintroduction?

Both contain fructans, but many people tolerate one far better than the other. Testing them separately can show that you handle garlic fine but not onion, or the reverse. Since these two flavor so much family cooking, many dietitians suggest testing them early. Each test still follows the small, medium, larger portion pattern with washout days in between.

Your first step today: if IBS has not been confirmed by a doctor, book that appointment and ask about celiac testing before you change your diet. If it has, start a simple symptom diary, swap onion for scallion greens and garlic for garlic-infused oil in tonight's dinner, and plan your first week of low-FODMAP meals before you shop.
