How to Decorate on a Budget: 8 Simple Steps to a Cozy Home

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your home feels bare, mismatched or not quite yours, and the budget is tight, you are in good company. The short answer is that you decorate on a budget by getting the free basics right first (a clear style, a simple color plan, warm layered lighting and furniture in the right place), then filling gaps slowly with secondhand finds and small DIY projects.

A beautiful room rarely comes from one expensive piece. It comes from good decisions that make inexpensive things look intentional. This guide gives you those decisions in order, with real measurements you can use this weekend.

Start with free changes before you spend

Before you buy anything, take before photos of each room from the doorway and from two corners. They show you what the room really looks like, and in a month they will remind you how far you have come.

Then do the free work:

  1. Clear the room of anything that does not belong there or that you no longer like.
  2. List the real jobs the room does, such as watching TV, working, kids' play or reading.
  3. Measure the room, doors, windows, radiators or vents and the furniture you own.
  4. Try a new furniture layout using what you already have.

You will be surprised how often a room improves with nothing more than a clear-out and a rearrange. Keep every measurement in one note on your phone so you can check sizes while you shop.

Spend your money where your body touches the room every day, such as your mattress, sofa cushions and the lamp you read by. Save on things you mostly look at, like side tables, frames and decorative objects, which are easy to find secondhand.

Find your style so you stop buying regrets

Impulse buys are the biggest budget leak in decorating. A clear sense of style works like a shopping filter, so each thing you bring home fits with the rest.

Start with what you already love: a cushion, a print, a scarf, a mug. Gather photos of rooms that make you feel something and look for what repeats, such as warm wood, soft greens, curved shapes or black metal. Then describe your style in two or three words plus one sentence, for example "relaxed coastal, with pale wood and lots of linen."

Be honest about how you live. A pale velvet sofa does not suit a home with a muddy dog, and a room full of small ornaments does not suit someone who hates dusting.

Sort what you own into three groups: keep, change and let go. Many pieces you would replace can be rescued with paint or new handles. Before any purchase, ask whether it fits your style words, your palette and your measurements. If not, walk away.

Use the 60-30-10 color rule

Color is the cheapest way to change how a room feels. The 60-30-10 rule keeps it simple: one main color for about 60 percent of the room, a secondary color for about 30 percent and an accent for about 10 percent.

60 percentWalls, large rugWarm off-white
30 percentSofa, curtains, beddingSage green
10 percentCushions, art, a vaseTerracotta

If you are stuck, pull your palette from a patterned item you love. The designer has already balanced those colors for you.

Test paint the right way

Tiny paint chips under shop lights are almost useless. Buy sample pots of your top two or three colors, paint two coats onto large white boards at least 2 feet (60 cm) square, and tape them around the room. Look at them in the morning, midday and at night under your own lamps for two or three days. Keep the one you still like at night.

To work out how much paint to buy, multiply each wall's width by its height, add them up and subtract doors and windows. One US gallon (about 3.8 liters) often covers 350 to 400 square feet (about 32 to 37 square meters) per coat, but check the label, and plan on two coats.

Pick a palette of four to six colors for the whole home and use them in different amounts in each room. Your home will feel connected, and you can reuse leftover paint.

Layer warm lighting in every room

A single ceiling light makes even a lovely room feel flat and harsh. The fix is three layers of light:

  • Ambient: general light that fills the room, from a ceiling fitting or floor lamps.
  • Task: focused light for reading, cooking or working, such as a desk lamp or a reading lamp.
  • Accent: soft light for mood, such as a small lamp on a shelf or a light behind a plant.

Aim for at least three light sources in a living room, placed in a rough triangle and at different heights. A dark corner makes a room feel smaller, and a floor lamp tucked into it can fix that in minutes.

The bulb matters as much as the lamp. For living rooms, bedrooms and dining areas, choose warm white bulbs of about 2700 to 3000K, printed on the box. Cooler bulbs of 3500 to 4000K suit kitchens, bathrooms and workspaces. Keep the color temperature the same within a room, because mixing warm and cool light is a common reason a room feels off.

Use bulbs within the wattage limit printed on each lamp, replace any lamp with a frayed cord, and never leave candles burning unattended.

Arrange furniture, rugs and art to scale

Layout costs nothing and makes the biggest difference. Pick one focal point, such as a window, fireplace, bed or large piece of art, and arrange seating to face or frame it. Pull the sofa a few inches off the wall and keep the main seats within about 8 to 10 feet (2.5 to 3 meters) of each other so conversation feels easy.

A rug that is too small is one of the most common mistakes. In a living room, at least the front legs of every seat should sit on the rug, which often means about 8 by 10 feet (around 240 by 300 cm). If that size is out of budget, layer an inexpensive large flat-woven rug under a smaller patterned one you love.

Hang curtains (drapes) high and wide. Mount the rod about 4 to 6 inches (10 to 15 cm) above the window frame, or halfway to the ceiling, and extend it 8 to 12 inches (20 to 30 cm) past each side. Windows look bigger and ceilings look taller.

Anchor bookcases, dressers, wardrobes and TV units to the wall with anti-tip straps or brackets suited to your wall type. Tip-overs injure children every year. If you rent, ask your landlord first and explain that it is for safety.

Shop secondhand and thrift smart

Secondhand furniture is often better made than cheap new pieces and costs far less. Good places to look include thrift shops (charity shops in the UK, op shops in Australia and New Zealand), yard sales (car boot sales), estate sales or house clearances, online marketplaces, free and swap groups, and friends who are moving.

Check anything upholstered or full of crevices for bed bugs before it comes home. Use a flashlight (torch) on seams, buttons, cushion edges and joints, and walk away if you see live bugs, tiny pale eggs, shed skins or small dark spots. Push on tables and chairs to test for wobbles, and open every drawer.

Painted furniture from before the late 1970s may contain lead paint. Do not sand, scrape or heat old paint until you have checked it with a lead test kit.

Keep a short hunting list on your phone with measurements and your palette. Be friendly and fair when you negotiate, and for marketplace pickups, meet in public or bring a friend.

Easy DIY and upcycling for beginners

Start small. A side table, a lamp base or a picture frame is a far better first project than a whole dresser. For paint that lasts on furniture, follow five steps:

  1. Clean off grease and grime, and fill any dents.
  2. Scuff sand with 120 to 220 grit sandpaper and wipe off the dust.
  3. Apply a primer suited to the surface.
  4. Paint in thin coats, letting each dry fully.
  5. Protect with a topcoat if needed and let it cure, often a week or more, before heavy use.

Work with windows open, wear a dust mask when sanding, and lay oily rags flat to dry outdoors or put them in a sealed metal container with water, because they can catch fire.

Some of the best upgrades need no paint at all. Swap tired handles and knobs, give a thrifted lamp a new shade, frame fabric or wallpaper offcuts as art, or buy thrifted frames and fill them with your own prints or photos. These small changes cost little and make a room look finished.

Decorate a rental without losing your deposit

Renters can make a big difference without permanent changes. Start by reading your lease and asking your landlord about anything you are unsure of. Get permission in writing, including for painting. Photograph and video every room when you move in, and again before you leave.

  • Bring in color with rugs, curtains, bedding, cushions, throws and art instead of paint.
  • Use removable hooks and strips exactly as the packet says, and test peel-and-stick products in a hidden spot first.
  • Swap fixtures like cabinet handles or a shower head only if you keep the originals to put back.
  • Use plug-in lamps and wall lights rather than changing hard-wired fittings, and leave electrical work to professionals.

Let furniture do the building. A tall bookcase can divide a studio, and a large rug can mark a living zone.

Finish one room in 30 days

Trying to do the whole home at once spreads your money and energy thin. Pick one room and give it a month, working 30 to 60 minutes a day with a longer session on weekends.

WeekFocus
1Declutter, choose style words and palette, measure, set a budget
2Test layouts with painter's tape, fix the lighting, hang curtains
3Thrift for your list, finish one DIY project, add rug and textiles
4Hang art, add plants, style surfaces, then edit

When you style shelves and tables, group objects in odd numbers with different heights and textures, and leave about a third of each surface clear. Take a photo at the end and look at it on your phone. Anything that looks out of place in the photo usually is, so remove it.

Frequently asked questions

How can I make my house look nice with no money?

Start with free changes. Declutter thoroughly, rearrange furniture around one focal point, pull the sofa off the wall, and move lamps so light comes from at least three places. Bring in plants from cuttings, frame things you already own and swap items between rooms. A tidy, well-lit room with a clear layout looks better than a crowded one full of new things.

What is the 60-30-10 rule in decorating?

It is a simple color guide. Use one main color for about 60 percent of a room, usually the walls or a large rug, a secondary color for about 30 percent, such as the sofa or curtains, and an accent for about 10 percent, such as cushions and art. The proportions keep a room balanced so it never looks flat or chaotic.

How do you make a cheap room look expensive?

Focus on the details designers get right. Use warm white bulbs of 2700 to 3000K in several lamps, hang curtains high and wide, choose a rug big enough for the front legs of all the seating, and hang art centered about 57 to 60 inches (145 to 152 cm) from the floor. Swapping cheap handles for heavier ones also helps a lot.

How high should I hang pictures on the wall?

Hang art so its center sits about 57 to 60 inches (145 to 152 cm) from the floor, roughly average eye level. Above a sofa or headboard, leave about 6 to 10 inches (15 to 25 cm) between the furniture and the bottom of the frame. For a group of frames, treat the whole arrangement as one piece and center it at the same height.

Is it safe to buy secondhand furniture?

Usually yes, with a few checks. Inspect upholstered and crevice-filled pieces for bed bugs with a flashlight before taking them home, test for wobbles, and open every drawer. Skip mattresses, pillows and children's car seats. Treat old painted furniture as a possible lead risk and test it before sanding. Clean everything before it goes into your home.

How can I decorate my rental without painting?

Bring color in through textiles and art instead of walls. Rugs, curtains, bedding, cushions and a large print or fabric hanging can cover the 30 and 10 parts of the 60-30-10 rule. Use plug-in lamps, removable hooks used as directed, and peel-and-stick products tested in a hidden spot. Always check your lease and get permission in writing.

Your first step today costs nothing: walk through your home, take before photos of every room, and choose the one room that bothers you most. Then pick one item you love, pull three colors from it and assign them to the 60, 30 and 10 roles. That small decision will guide every choice you make in that room from here on.
