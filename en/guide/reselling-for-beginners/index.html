Reselling for Beginners: How to Find, List and Sell Secondhand Items

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you love a bargain at the thrift store (charity shop or op shop) or your spare room is full of things you never use, reselling can turn that habit into a tidy side income. Reselling for beginners comes down to one repeatable cycle: research what has actually sold, buy low, clean the item, photograph it well, write an honest listing, price it with fees and postage included, and ship it safely.

Nobody can promise you a profit. Many items will not sell, fees and postage eat into margins and the time adds up. This guide shows you how to start small with things you already own, avoid the costly mistakes and build a simple system you can keep up for months.

How reselling works, step by step

Every item moves through the same five stages: buy, clean, list, sell, ship. Profit is mostly decided at the first stage, which is why research comes before buying.

  1. Choose one selling platform that suits your first items, and read its current fees and rules.
  2. Practice with 10 items from your own home.
  3. Learn to research sold prices on the spot with your phone.
  4. Make a first sourcing trip with a firm spending limit written down.
  5. Clean, photograph and list items in short, regular sessions.
  6. Pack and post within a day or two, and record every sale.

A small steady routine beats occasional marathons. Some weeks nothing sells, and that is normal, not a failure.

Your starter kit

  • A smartphone with a clean camera lens.
  • A plain backdrop, such as white poster board or a blank wall.
  • A tape measure and a kitchen or postal scale.
  • Mild soap, soft cloths and a soft brush.
  • A free online spreadsheet or notebook for records.
  • Reused boxes and padding.

Start reselling with what you already own

Your own unwanted things are a no-risk way to practice the whole cycle. Pick 10 test items from wardrobes, kitchen cupboards, bookshelves, the garage or loft, and children's rooms (with their agreement).

For each one, look up sold prices, not asking prices. Anyone can ask a high price; sold listings show what buyers really paid. Check that each match is the same item in the same condition, note whether postage was included, and look at how many have sold recently compared with how many are listed now. Aim for the middle of the range.

Condition grades buyers understand

  • New with tags: unused, with original tags or packaging.
  • Excellent or like new: used, no visible wear.
  • Good: light, normal wear, described clearly.
  • Fair: obvious wear, every flaw described and photographed.
  • For parts or not working: sold honestly as such.

Then read the results. Lots of views but no sale often points to a price that is too high or photos that raise doubts. Few views usually means the title or category needs work.

Plan a route with opening times checked, carry a charged phone, a tape measure, a small torch (flashlight), sturdy bags and your spending limit on paper.

Be a good shopper. Follow shop and sale rules, haggle politely and accept a no, never hide items to come back for later, and be honest if a seller asks whether something is valuable. Take curb or free items only where it is legal and safe.

What sells and what to leave behind

Categories that often sell include clothing and shoes, vintage kitchenware, hand and power tools, books and media, toys and games, collectibles, home decor, and sports and outdoor gear. That does not mean every item in them sells, so check sold prices first.

Signs of quality help: natural fibers and neat stitching in clothes, solid wood and dovetail joints in furniture, maker's marks on the base of ceramics and kitchenware, and maker's names stamped into tools. Before paying, check every side, run a finger around rims for chips, count pieces in games and sets, test zips and hinges, and smell textiles and books for smoke or damp.

Items to avoid

  • Fake, replica or doubtful branded goods.
  • Used child car seats, used bike or motorcycle helmets, and cribs (cots) or baby sleep products that do not meet current standards.
  • Older painted children's items that may contain lead paint.
  • Damaged electrical items and anything with swollen or leaking batteries.
  • Restricted items such as weapons, medicines, alcohol, opened cosmetics and food.
  • Bulky, low-value items that cost more to ship than they earn.
Never knowingly buy or sell counterfeit goods. It is illegal in many countries and against marketplace rules, and accounts can be closed. If you cannot be sure an item is genuine, do not sell it as genuine. Before selling children's products, electrical goods, cosmetics or safety equipment, check your government's product recall website.

Photos and listings that sell

Use soft daylight from the side, flash off and a plain backdrop. Take a bright main photo that fills the frame, then the back, sides, base, inside, labels and maker's marks, and a clear close-up of every flaw. Show scale with a ruler or a common object, and edit only lightly so colors stay true.

Write titles with the words buyers type into the search bar: what it is, maker if known, material, color, size and key feature.

A simple description template: what it is, maker and age if verified, measurements in both units, material, condition with every flaw, what you tested, what is included and not included, and how it will be packed and posted. Say "smoke-free home" only if it is true.

How to price items to resell

Start from the middle of recent sold prices for the same item in the same condition, then adjust for demand and supply. Auctions suit unusual, in-demand items but can end low. Fixed price suits everyday items and clothing. Fixed price with offers works for most things, as long as you decide your lowest price in advance.

Fees come in several forms: listing fees, final value or commission fees, payment processing fees and optional promotion fees. They differ by platform and change over time, so read the current fee page before you price.

The is it worth it rule

  1. Likely selling price (middle of recent sales).
  2. Minus fees, estimated from the current fee page.
  3. Minus postage and packing materials.
  4. Minus what you paid for the item.
  5. What is left is your profit. Compare it with the time the item will take.

If the answer is not worth it, leave the item on the shelf. Bundle low-value items that belong together, such as paperbacks by one author, and refresh stale listings with better photos, a sharper title or a modest price drop.

Packing, shipping and avoiding scams

Wrap fragile items individually with plenty of cushioning so nothing moves when you shake the box. Fold clothing neatly in tissue inside a mailer bag, protect book corners, and use strong boxes for heavy items. Weigh and measure every parcel before buying postage, keep proof of postage and use tracking. For valuable items, buy insurance and photograph the item being packed.

Lithium batteries, aerosols, perfume, flammable products and liquids have shipping restrictions that vary by carrier and country, so check before you list. International parcels need honest customs forms, and buyers may pay import duties.

Common scams

  • Fake payment emails that look like they come from the platform.
  • Overpayment followed by a request to refund the difference.
  • Requests to ship to a different address or pay off the platform.
  • Empty box or switch claims on returns.

Keep all messages and payments on the platform. For local pickups, meet in a busy public place in daylight or a police station safe exchange zone, bring someone with you and never invite strangers into your home alone.

Keep records and understand taxes

Record every item from day one: an item number, what you paid and where, the sold price, fees, postage and packing, and your profit. Keep receipts for supplies and equipment, and log mileage for sourcing trips.

In many countries, selling your own used things at a loss is treated differently from buying items to resell for profit, which is usually a business and taxable. Online platforms and payment services in many countries now report seller income to tax authorities above thresholds that vary by country.

This is general information, not tax or financial advice. Registration and tax rules vary by country, state and province. Check your tax authority's guidance or ask an accountant, especially if your sales are growing or you are unsure whether you count as a business.

Keep business money in a separate account and reinvest carefully: buy more of what sells, not more of everything. The book Reselling Secondhand Items Made Simple includes an inventory and sales log, a condition grading guide and a 30-day reseller plan.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do you need to start reselling?

Very little. You can start with items you already own, so your first listings cost nothing but time. After that, set a small, firm budget for your first sourcing trip and only spend more once those items sell. Useful extras such as a scale, a backdrop and packing supplies can be bought slowly or reused from parcels you receive.

What are the best things to resell for beginners?

Start with categories you already know and enjoy. Clothing and shoes, vintage kitchenware, tools, books and media, toys and games, home decor and outdoor gear often sell. Always check recent sold prices before buying, and avoid fakes, recalled or unsafe children's items, damaged electricals and bulky low-value items that are expensive to ship.

Is reselling worth it?

It can be, but profits vary and are never guaranteed. Many items do not sell, fees and postage reduce margins, and sourcing, cleaning, photos and packing take time. Use the is it worth it check on every item: likely sold price minus fees, postage and your cost, compared with your time. Many people enjoy it as a flexible side income or hobby.

How do I know what price to sell something for?

Search for the exact same item in the same condition and filter to sold or completed listings, not current asking prices. Note whether postage was included, how many sold recently and how many are listed now. Price near the middle of recent sales, then lower it gradually if it sits for weeks without interest.

Do I have to pay tax on reselling?

It depends on where you live and what you are doing. Selling your own used belongings at a loss is often treated differently from buying items to resell for profit, which is usually a taxable business. Platforms in many countries report seller income above certain thresholds. Keep full records and check with your tax authority or an accountant.

How do I avoid buying fakes?

Look closely at overall quality, stitching, materials, labels, logos and serial or date codes, and ask about provenance. Red flags include a price far too low for the condition, slightly wrong fonts and cheap-feeling materials. If you are unsure, walk away or pay for professional authentication. Never list an item as genuine unless you are confident it is.

Your first step today: pick 10 things you no longer use, look up their sold prices and write the middle price next to each one. List the three that look most worth it, and you will learn more from those three sales than from a month of reading.
