Cooking for One: Easy Meals, Smart Shopping and No Wasted Food

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you live alone, you probably know the feeling: a fridge holding two eggs and a sweating bag of salad, and the delivery menu already open. The short answer to cooking for one is to stop cooking as if for a family: buy small amounts, cook single portions or small batches of 2 to 4, freeze the extras flat in labeled bags, and plan every ingredient you buy across about three meals.

This guide walks you through that system step by step. You will find the small kit worth owning, a chart for scaling recipes down, a shopping method that wastes less, the storage and freezer rules that keep food safe, and plenty of quick meal ideas you can make tonight with an ordinary kitchen.

Why cooking for one feels so hard

Most people who live alone do not stop cooking because they lack skill. They stop because recipes, pack sizes and pans are all built for four people, and that system simply does not fit one.

Three traps tend to creep in. The first is takeout and ready meals, which are fine as a treat but expensive and often salty as a habit. The second is cereal or toast for dinner, which asks nothing of you and slowly leaves your energy and mood a little flat. The third is waste: a whole head of celery bought for two stalks, or a recipe for four that you eat twice before the rest grows fur at the back of the fridge.

All three traps have one cure. Start from a single portion instead of four, and give every ingredient a plan before it goes in the fridge. The rest of this guide shows you how.

The small kitchen kit that helps

Big family pans work against you. One chicken thigh in a large roasting pan dries out, and a little sauce in a wide pot boils away before it thickens. Smaller pans cook better and are quicker to wash.

  • A small skillet (frying pan), about 8 inches (20 cm), ideally nonstick, for eggs, a single chop, quesadillas and fried rice.
  • A small saucepan of about 1 1/2 to 2 quarts (1.5 to 2 liters) for rice, grains and one portion of soup.
  • A medium pot of 3 to 4 quarts (3 to 4 liters) for pasta and small batch soups.
  • A quarter sheet pan, about 9 x 13 inches (23 x 33 cm), half a standard baking tray, so a tray dinner for one does not burn.
  • A loaf pan for a small lasagna or meatloaf of 3 or 4 portions.
  • Small freezer bags, a permanent marker, a few one-portion lidded containers and an instant-read thermometer.

A countertop air fryer or toaster oven heats up fast and uses less energy than a full oven for one tray. Most oven recipes work in them at the same temperature: start checking about a third sooner and turn the food halfway. A microwave handles reheating and safe thawing, and a small slow cooker turns out two or three portions of stew while you are out.

How to scale a recipe down for one person

Most savory recipes for four shrink well. Divide every ingredient by four, then round to something sensible. One egg can stand in for half of two, and a quarter of an onion is fine, with the rest bagged for tomorrow.

Season a little under the strict quarter and taste at the end, since small amounts of salt and spice are easy to overdo. Keep the oven temperature the same and expect the cooking time to be a bit shorter. Use a smaller pan, and add a splash of water or broth (stock) if a sauce thickens before the food is done.

How to shop for one without waste

The best shopping trip starts in front of your own fridge. Look at what is already open or going soft, plan your next meals around it, and only then write the list.

  1. Plan three or four dinners at a time, not seven. Life alone is unpredictable, and two small shops a week usually waste less than one big one.
  2. Group the list by aisle: produce, meat and fish counter, dairy, cupboard, frozen.
  3. Buy loose produce. One pepper, two carrots and a single sweet potato may cost a few cents more per pound, but you pay nothing for the half you would have binned.
  4. Ask the meat or fish counter for one pork chop, two chicken thighs or 4 oz (115 g) of ground beef (minced beef). They are used to it.
  5. When only big packs exist, buy them and freeze the meat in single pieces the day you get home.
  6. If you shop online, check pack sizes, because the default is often the family size.

Bulk buying only pays off for long-keeping staples you use often, such as rice, pasta, oats, red lentils, canned tomatoes, beans and oil, plus meat and bread you freeze the same day. Skip bulk salad leaves, soft herbs, mushrooms and giant jars of sauce. Splitting a big bag of potatoes or a family pack of chicken with a friend who also lives alone is a friendly way around it.

Stock a solo pantry and freezer

A small, well chosen cupboard (store cupboard) means you can still make dinner when the fridge looks bare. These ten ingredients keep well, come in small amounts or freeze, and turn up in meal after meal:

  • Eggs
  • Frozen peas and frozen spinach
  • Canned chickpeas and black beans
  • Red lentils, which cook in about 20 minutes with no soaking
  • Boneless chicken thighs, frozen singly
  • Small cans of tuna
  • Onions and garlic
  • Lemons
  • A hard cheese such as Parmesan
  • Rice and a small bag of pasta

Frozen vegetables are not second best. They are already washed and chopped, and you can take one handful and put the bag back. Individually wrapped frozen fish fillets work the same way, and frozen shrimp (prawns) thaw in a few minutes in cold water.

Plan one ingredient across three meals

This is the habit that ends most solo food waste. Instead of planning meals, plan ingredients. Whenever you buy something bigger than one meal needs, decide on the spot what its next two meals will be.

A bunch of cilantro (coriander) for a burrito bowl can also go into a quick chickpea curry midweek and a green herb sauce at the weekend. A small cabbage becomes a stir-fry on Monday, a side for a pork chop on Wednesday and an odds and ends soup on Saturday. A can of coconut milk splits between a curry and a squash soup, with any extra frozen in an ice cube tray.

The same idea works for cooked food. Roast four chicken thighs on Sunday, eat one or two hot with roasted vegetables, then pull the rest off the bone. That chicken becomes a wrap with avocado and crisp lettuce within 2 days and a pesto pasta salad within 3 to 4 days. Leftovers feel like new meals when each one has a different job.

Keep a short note on the fridge door headed "Use next." Every time you open something, write it down with the day, and start your next meal plan from that list.

Freezing single portions the easy way

The freezer is what makes cooking for one flexible. One cooking session can fill it with ready dinners for the nights you are too tired to boil an egg.

  • Spoon soup, chili, curry or stew into small freezer bags, press out the air and freeze them flat on a tray. Flat bags stack like files and thaw far faster than a solid block.
  • Wrap raw chicken thighs, chops and fillets one by one, or freeze them spread on a tray and then bag them so they do not stick.
  • Label every portion with the name and date. "Beef stew, 12 March" beats "brown stuff" every time.
  • Keep a list on the freezer door and cross things off as you eat them.

Several things freeze better than people expect. Grate fresh ginger straight from frozen without peeling it, freeze tomato paste in teaspoon blobs, freeze chopped herbs in oil or water in ice cube trays, and keep grated cheese and sliced bread in the freezer for up to 3 months.

Food kept at 0°F (-18°C) stays safe indefinitely, but it tastes best within about 2 to 3 months.

Leftover safety rules for one

Living alone means leftovers sit around longer, so these rules matter.

  • Refrigerate cooked food within 2 hours, or 1 hour in heat above 90°F (32°C). Spread it in shallow containers to cool fast.
  • Keep the fridge at or below 40°F (4°C); UK guidance is 41°F (5°C) or below.
  • Eat fridge leftovers within 3 to 4 days. Cooked rice is best eaten within 24 hours and should be cooled within about an hour.
  • Never store food in an opened can. Move it to a covered container: canned tomatoes keep 5 to 7 days, while beans, fish, meat and coconut milk keep 3 to 4 days.
  • Thaw in the fridge, the microwave or a sealed bag in cold water, never on the counter.
  • Reheat only once, until steaming hot all the way through, 165°F (74°C).

Quick meal ideas for one person

You do not need dozens of recipes. A handful of fast, flexible dishes covers most weeknights. A simple guide for the plate: roughly half vegetables, a quarter whole grains or potatoes and a quarter protein, balanced across the week rather than at every meal.

Ten to twenty minutes

  • Fried rice with frozen peas and an egg, using rice cooked and chilled the day before (within 24 hours).
  • Spaghetti with a small can of tuna, chili flakes, garlic and lemon.
  • A chickpea and spinach curry from half a can of chickpeas and a few coconut milk cubes.
  • A two-egg vegetable scramble or spinach and feta omelet.

Hands-off dinners

  • One lemon chicken thigh roasted on a quarter sheet pan with sliced potatoes.
  • A salmon fillet with green beans and cherry tomatoes on one small tray.
  • A small pot of red lentil dal that gives you tonight's dinner and a portion to freeze.

Friday is a good night to clear the odds and ends: a wedge of cabbage becomes a noodle stir-fry with soy and ginger, and soft carrots, celery and the last potato make a pot of soup. Anything slimy, moldy or smelling off goes, but wrinkly and a little soft is fine to cook. Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest way to cook for one person?

Cook single portions of quick dishes such as fried rice, omelets or pasta on busy nights, and make small batches of soup, chili or curry once or twice a week. Eat one portion fresh and freeze the rest flat in labeled single-portion bags. That way you always have a homemade dinner ready without eating the same thing for five nights running.

How do I stop wasting food when I live alone?

Shop for three or four dinners at a time, buy loose vegetables and single pieces of meat or fish, and plan each ingredient across about three meals before you buy it. Keep a "Use next" list on the fridge for anything opened, freeze bread, herbs, ginger and meat early, and use one night a week to cook up the odds and ends.

Is it cheaper to cook for one or buy ready meals?

Cooking for one is usually cheaper once you stop throwing food away. Cupboard staples such as rice, lentils, canned beans and tomatoes cost little per portion, and loose produce means you only pay for what you use. Ready meals and takeout are convenient but cost more per meal and are often high in salt, so they work best as an occasional treat.

How long do leftovers last in the fridge?

Most cooked leftovers keep 3 to 4 days in a fridge at or below 40°F (4°C), or 41°F (5°C) in UK guidance. Cooked rice is best eaten within 24 hours. Refrigerate food within 2 hours of cooking, and if you will not finish it in time, freeze it in single portions and reheat it once until steaming hot.

Can you freeze single portions of soup and curry?

Yes. Cool the batch quickly in shallow containers, then spoon single portions into small freezer bags, press out the air and freeze them flat. Label each with the name and date. They keep safely at 0°F (-18°C), taste best within 2 to 3 months, and thaw quickly in the fridge overnight or in the microwave.

How do you cut a recipe for four down to one?

Divide each ingredient by four and round sensibly, for example one egg instead of half of two, or a quarter of an onion. Use slightly less salt and spice than the strict quarter and taste at the end. Keep the same oven temperature, use a smaller pan, and check doneness with a thermometer because smaller amounts cook faster.

Start tonight with one small step: stick a "Use next" note on your fridge, write down everything that is already open, and cook one quick meal, such as fried rice with peas and an egg, that uses something on that list. Once that feels easy, add a small batch for the freezer each week, and the toast-for-dinner nights will become the exception instead of the rule.
