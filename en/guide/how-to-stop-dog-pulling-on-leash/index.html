How to Stop a Dog Pulling on the Leash: A Kind, Step-by-Step Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If every walk feels like water skiing behind your dog, you are in good company: pulling on the leash (lead) is the most common problem owners ask trainers about. To stop a dog pulling, make one rule and keep it every time: a tight leash means the walk stops, and a loose leash means it goes on, while you reward your dog often for being by your side.

Add a well-fitted front-clip harness, plenty of sniff breaks and short daily practice, and most adult and rescue dogs walk far more nicely within a few weeks. This guide gives you the exact steps, a stage-by-stage table and fixes for the usual sticking points.

Why does my dog pull on the leash

Dogs simply walk faster than people. A relaxed trot for your dog is close to a jog for you, so strolling at your pace feels slow and strange until it is taught. Walking calmly beside a human is a learned skill, not something dogs arrive knowing.

The bigger reason is that pulling works. Each time your dog leans into the leash and you keep moving, they get closer to the smell, the park or the other dog. That forward movement rewards the pulling, so it grows stronger. A dog who has pulled for years has had thousands of practice runs.

Some dogs pull because they are anxious or overexcited. A rescue dog who is still settling in may rush ahead because the world feels like a lot. For these dogs, quieter routes, shorter walks and extra sniffing help as much as any technique. Dogs who seem stiff or reluctant on walks may be in pain, so a vet check comes first.

Choose the right harness and leash

You do not need fancy gear, but the right basics make training easier on your dog's neck and your shoulders.

  • A well-fitted front-clip harness. The leash attaches at the chest, so when your dog pulls they are gently turned toward you, which takes some power out of it. It should allow full shoulder movement and not rub under the front legs.
  • A flat collar with an ID tag, as required where you live.
  • A 6 ft (2 m) leash for everyday walks.
  • A treat pouch, so rewards are quick.
  • A long line of 15 to 30 ft (5 to 10 m) for sniffing time in open, safe spaces.

Skip retractable leashes while you train. They teach your dog that pulling makes the leash longer, which is the exact opposite of your lesson.

Check harness fit by sliding two fingers under each strap. A whole hand means it is too loose and your dog may back out; less than two fingers means it is too tight.

Why prong, choke and shock collars are not the answer

These collars stop pulling by causing discomfort or pain. They can injure the neck and throat, make dogs more fearful, and in some dogs create reactivity: a dog who feels a jolt every time they see another dog can start to link other dogs with pain.

Modern trainers have moved away from punishment and old ideas about being the pack leader or alpha. Dogs are not trying to dominate you on a walk. They are doing what has worked for them. Reward-based training changes what works, and it keeps your dog's trust in you intact, which matters for every other skill you teach.

Step by step loose leash training

Pick one side for your dog to walk on, at least while you teach, and keep treats on that side so rewards land by your leg. A loose leash hangs in a soft J or U shape. Your dog does not need a perfect heel; a little ahead or behind is fine, as long as there is no pulling. Keep sessions to 5 to 10 minutes.

  1. Reward the sweet spot. Indoors, with your dog on leash, stand still. Each time your dog comes close to your chosen leg, say a marker word such as "Yes!" and drop a treat by your foot. Repeat 10 to 15 times.
  2. Take a few steps. Walk one step and reward if your dog is by your side. Then two steps, then three. At first, reward every 1 to 3 steps.
  3. Stop when the leash goes tight. The instant it tightens, stand still like a tree. Do not jerk or pull back. Wait for your dog to look at you or step back so the leash slackens, then mark, reward by your leg and walk on.
  4. Turn around. If your dog keeps forging ahead, say a cheerful cue like "This way," turn and walk the other direction. Reward when they catch up beside you.

Your first session outdoors may only get you about ten yards (meters) down the street. That is completely normal. Progress usually speeds up after a few days, once your dog understands that slack is what moves you forward.

Use sniff breaks as a reward

A walk is your dog's chance to read the neighborhood. Sniffing tires dogs mentally and helps them relax, and a dog who never gets to sniff will pull harder to reach every interesting spot.

Turn sniffing into a reward you control. After a stretch of nice walking, say "Go sniff" and let your dog explore a patch of grass for as long as they like. When you are ready, say "Let's go" brightly and walk on. Your dog soon learns that walking nicely leads to sniff time, which removes a big reason to pull.

Some walks can be mostly sniffing. A slow wander on a long line in a quiet field often leaves a dog more content than a fast march around the block.

How to build up to busy streets

Once your dog walks nicely in a quiet place, raise the challenge slowly. Change only one thing at a time: a longer walk, a slightly busier street or more distractions.

StageWhereHow often to reward
1Indoors and hallwayEvery 1 to 2 steps
2Yard or gardenEvery 2 to 4 steps
3Quiet streetEvery 5 to 10 steps
4Busier street or parkEvery 10 to 20 steps, more near distractions
5Familiar routesNow and then, plus sniff breaks

If your dog starts pulling more at a new stage, go back one stage for a few days. That is not failure, just good training.

When a squirrel or dog appears

Act before your dog reacts. Cross the street, turn around or step onto a driveway to add distance, and feed treats as you go. Another handy move is the treat scatter: toss a few treats on the ground so your dog sniffs them up while the distraction passes. If your dog barks, lunges or growls at other dogs or people, that is reactivity, which needs a slower plan and sometimes professional help.

Common leash pulling problems and fixes

  • Your dog zigzags across the path. Reward more often at your side and walk with a bit more purpose. Dogs weave when the pace is slow and nothing tells them where to be.
  • Your dog walks nicely, then pulls harder later. They may be tired or overexcited. Shorten the walk, add more sniff breaks and finish while it is going well.
  • Your dog pulls toward home. Many dogs speed up on the return leg. Add extra stops and turns on the way back.
  • Your dog stops and will not move. This can mean worry, tiredness or discomfort. Watch their body language, give them time and try a quieter route.
  • Two dogs pull together. Train each dog alone first, then walk together with a helper, one dog each.

The biggest mistake is staying consistent on training walks but letting your dog pull "just this once" when you are late. Anything that sometimes works becomes very hard to stop. On rushed days, use a quiet route or play a sniffing game in the yard instead of a walk.

Keep your dog on leash near roads, livestock and wildlife, and follow local leash laws. In hot weather, walk early or late, carry water and test the pavement (sidewalk) with the back of your hand: if you cannot hold it there for 5 to 7 seconds, it is too hot for paws. If your dog seems sore, stiff or suddenly reluctant to walk, see your vet, because pain often shows up first on walks.

A simple 2-week loose leash plan

Ten minutes a day is enough. Here is a realistic plan for a typical adult or rescue dog.

  • Days 1 to 3: indoors only. Reward the sweet spot by your leg, then 1 to 5 steps at a time. Practice the "This way" turn in the hallway.
  • Days 4 to 6: yard or garden. Stop and wait every time the leash goes tight. Reward every 2 to 4 steps.
  • Days 7 to 10: a quiet street at a quiet time. Short walks of 10 to 15 minutes with a sniff break every couple of minutes.
  • Days 11 to 14: your usual route. Add turns before distractions and reward less often, but keep the sniff breaks.

Picture Molly and her rescue dog, Bruno. On day one they managed half a block. By day ten Bruno could walk ten steps on a loose leash, turn with Molly on cue and wait calmly at the curb, and the walk had become the best part of both their days. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to stop a dog pulling on the leash?

Many dogs show clear progress within one to three weeks of short daily practice, though dogs who have pulled for years may take longer. Consistency matters more than session length. Ten minutes a day where pulling never pays off will beat an hour once a week. Expect quiet streets to improve first, with busy places and exciting distractions taking a few more weeks.

What is the best harness for a dog that pulls?

A well-fitted front-clip harness, where the leash attaches at the chest, is a good choice for most dogs who pull. It turns your dog gently toward you when they lean forward, which reduces pulling power without pain. Choose one that leaves the shoulders free to move and does not rub behind the front legs. A harness helps, but training is what changes the habit.

Should I yank the leash when my dog pulls?

No. Jerking the leash can hurt your dog's neck, make them worried on walks and damage their trust in you. It also does not show them what to do instead. Simply stop, stay still and wait for the leash to loosen, then reward and walk on. Your dog learns that slack, not pulling, makes the walk continue.

Can you train an older or rescue dog to walk on a loose leash?

Yes. Adult and rescue dogs learn loose leash walking well with reward-based training, even after years of pulling. Give a newly adopted dog time to settle, often a few weeks, and start with calm, quiet routes. Keep sessions short, use treats your dog really loves, and build up to busier places slowly. Older dogs may need gentler, shorter walks, so ask your vet what suits them.

Why does my dog pull more when they see other dogs?

Other dogs are exciting or worrying, so your dog tries to get closer or makes a fuss. Add distance early, before your dog stares or pulls, and feed treats as the other dog passes. A treat scatter on the ground also helps. If your dog barks, lunges or growls, that is reactivity, and a qualified reward-based trainer or behavior professional can help.

Is it OK to let my dog walk in front of me?

Yes, as long as the leash stays loose. Walking ahead is not a sign that your dog is trying to be the boss. For everyday walks, a dog who strolls a little ahead, sniffs and checks in with you is doing well. A strict heel at your side is a separate skill you only need for crowded places or crossing roads.

Start today with five minutes indoors: stand still, reward your dog every time they come beside your leg, then take ten steps around the room and stop the moment the leash goes tight. That one rule, repeated calmly and kindly, is the foundation of every relaxed walk you will share from here on.
