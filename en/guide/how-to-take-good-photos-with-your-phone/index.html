How to Take Good Photos With Your Phone: 10 Simple Fixes

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your phone is full of pictures that looked lovely in real life but came out blurry, dark or flat, you do not need a new phone or a proper camera. To take good photos with your phone, wipe the lens, turn on gridlines, find soft light from the side, tap the screen on your subject to set focus and brightness, use the lens buttons instead of pinch zoom, and give each keeper a quick two-minute edit.

Those habits fix most disappointing photos, and none of them need technical jargon. This guide explains each one, plus tips for people, kids, pets, food and low light, a light cheat sheet and a simple edit order.

Start with a clean lens and gridlines

The single most common cause of hazy, glowing, slightly soft photos is a smudged lens. Phones live in pockets and hands, so the little glass circle on the back collects fingerprints all day. Wipe it with a soft, clean cloth (a glasses cloth or a soft cotton shirt) before you shoot anything that matters.

Next, turn on gridlines in your camera settings. They divide the screen into nine boxes with two vertical and two horizontal lines. They help you keep horizons level and place your subject in a pleasing spot, and they never appear in the finished photo.

Your first 15 minutes

  • Wipe the lens.
  • Turn on gridlines (look in the camera settings, since menu names differ by phone model and software version).
  • Find the lens buttons, such as 0.5x, 1x and 2x or 3x, if your phone has them.
  • Take one photo with the on-screen shutter and one with a volume button.
  • Tap on something close, then something far, and watch the focus change.
  • Check that your photos are backing up somewhere.

Why are my phone photos blurry

Blur usually comes from one of four things, and each has a simple fix.

CauseThe fix
Smudged lensWipe it with a soft cloth
Jabbing the shutterTap lightly, or use a volume button
Focus on the wrong thingTap your subject on the screen
Pinch zooming too farStep closer or use the next lens button

Hold the phone with both hands, elbows tucked against your body, and breathe out gently as you take the picture. In dim light, lean on a wall or rest the phone on a table, and use the 3 second timer so your finger is off the phone when the shot is taken.

The zoom point deserves its own line. When you tap a lens button like 2x or 3x, most phones switch to a real separate lens and the picture stays sharp. Pinching beyond that just crops and enlarges the image, which is called digital zoom, and it throws away detail. Use the highest lens button, then stop. You can crop a little later.

Tap to focus and fix the brightness

Your phone makes a guess about what matters in every scene, and it often guesses wrong. Tap the screen on your subject, such as a face or a flower, and the phone focuses there and sets brightness for it. A small sun or slider usually appears next to the box. Slide it up to brighten and down to darken before you shoot.

If the phone keeps resetting when you move slightly, press and hold on the subject instead of tapping. After a second or two, a lock message or padlock appears, and focus and brightness stay put while you reframe. Tap elsewhere to release it.

What you seeUsual causeThe fix
Face too darkBright window or sky behindTap the face, slide brighter
Sky pure whitePhone exposed for the groundTap the sky, slide darker
Snow or sand looks grayPhone dims bright scenesSlide brighter
Black pet is a blobPhone brightens dark furTap the fur, slide darker a touch

Leave HDR on automatic. It blends several exposures so both a bright sky and a shaded face keep detail, and most phones decide well when to use it.

Find good light for phone photos

Light matters more than the phone. The same scene can look flat at noon and glowing an hour before sunset. Start noticing where the light comes from and whether it is hard (sharp shadows) or soft (gentle shadows).

SituationWhat to do
Golden hour (about an hour after sunrise or before sunset)Shoot people and scenery freely
Overcast dayIdeal soft light for portraits and flowers
Harsh midday sunMove into open shade or turn your subject
Open shadeFace your subject toward the open sky
Indoors by a windowTurn room lights off, subject near the window
BacklightTap the face and slide brighter
SilhouetteTap the sky and slide darker
Dim roomMove toward light and skip the flash

Open shade means a shaded spot next to bright open sky, such as under a porch roof or on the shady side of a building. It gives soft, flattering light with no squinting.

Indoors, a window is the best light you own. Put your subject a step or two from it, with the light coming from the side, and switch off ceiling lights so you are not mixing orange and blue. Usually skip the flash. It flattens faces, causes red eyes and makes backgrounds black.

Simple composition tips that look professional

  • Pick one subject. Before you tap the shutter, decide what the photo is about. If you cannot say it in a few words, the viewer will not know either.
  • Use the rule of thirds. Place your subject on one of the gridline crossings rather than dead center, and put the horizon on the top or bottom line.
  • Look for leading lines, such as paths, fences, rivers and roads, that pull the eye toward your subject.
  • Frame your subject with a doorway, an arch or overhanging branches.
  • Keep backgrounds simple. Before you shoot, check behind your subject for trash cans (bins), poles growing out of heads and clutter. Moving a step left or right often fixes it.
  • Fill the frame. Most beginner photos are taken from too far away.
  • Use negative space on purpose, such as a small figure against a big sky.
  • Center your subject for symmetry, such as a building front or a reflection in still water.
  • Change your angle. Crouch low, hold the phone high or shoot from the side.

A good habit to build: after your first shot, take three steps (closer, to the side or lower) and shoot again. The second or third picture is usually the better one.

Better photos of people, kids and pets

For portraits, use the 2x or 3x lens if you have one, or portrait mode, which blurs the background. Portrait mode works best with your subject a few feet (about 1 to 2.5 meters) away and some distance between them and the background. The ultra-wide 0.5x lens stretches things near the edges, so keep faces away from it.

Hold the phone at eye level or slightly above, and avoid cropping at joints like knees, elbows and ankles. For natural expressions, talk, joke and shoot between the posed moments. For groups, stand people close together with heads at different heights, and use the 10 second timer so you are in the picture too.

Kids and pets

  • Get down to their eye level. Kneel or sit on the floor.
  • Use burst mode for action, then keep the best one or two frames.
  • Play a game or ask a silly question for real smiles.
  • For pets, focus on the eyes and use a treat or a squeaky sound held just above the phone.
  • Shoot outdoors or near a window, since good light lets the phone freeze motion.

Think before posting children's photos publicly, ask other parents before sharing pictures of their kids, and know that you can turn off location data for the camera in your settings.

How to take good photos indoors and at night

Low light is harder because the phone needs more time to gather light, so any movement shows as blur and the picture turns grainy. Start with the 1x lens, which is usually the best on the phone in dim light.

  • Let night mode work. Many phones switch it on automatically and show a countdown. Hold perfectly still until it ends.
  • Brace yourself. Lean on a wall, rest your elbows on a table or set the phone on a surface.
  • Use the timer so your finger is off the phone when the shot is taken. A small tripod helps a lot.
  • For candles and birthday cakes, turn off ceiling lights, tap a face and slide darker a little so the flames keep their glow.
  • Avoid pinch zoom in low light, which gives the worst results.

For food at home, take the plate to a window, shoot from straight above for flat dishes or at about 45 degrees for tall ones, and clear away clutter.

Stay safe while you shoot. Never take photos while driving, do not walk and shoot near traffic, cliffs or water edges, and keep a secure grip or strap on the phone near water. Check your surroundings before you step back for a wider shot.

How to edit photos on your phone in two minutes

The editor already built into your phone is enough for nearly every photo, and it keeps the original so you can always undo. The order matters, because each step affects the next.

  1. Crop and straighten. Fix the frame and the horizon first.
  2. Adjust brightness or exposure so the subject looks properly lit.
  3. Use highlights and shadows, then contrast. Bring back the sky, open up faces, then add a little snap.
  4. Set warmth so whites look white and skin looks natural.
  5. Add saturation or vibrance last, and only a little.

Then compare with the original, often by pressing and holding on the image. If faces look orange or plastic, or skies turn flat gray, pull every slider back by about a quarter. Go easy on smoothing or beauty sliders, since people look like themselves with a few lines.

You do not need to edit everything. From eighty photos of a day out, choose your best five or six and edit only those. The book Stunning Photos With Your Phone includes a 30-day challenge of short photo missions to practice each of these skills.

Back up your phone photos

Good photos are only worth taking if you keep them. Aim for at least two copies of every picture in different places, such as cloud backup plus a copy on a computer or external drive. Check now and then that the backup shows as up to date.

Delete obvious duplicates and blurry shots after each outing, mark favorites and make albums for trips and family events. It makes finding, sharing and printing your best photos much easier later.

Frequently asked questions

What settings should I use on my phone camera?

Turn on gridlines, leave HDR on automatic and set the flash to off or automatic rather than always on. Choose the highest photo quality if your phone offers a choice, and check that cloud backup is on. Learn tap to focus, the brightness slider and press and hold to lock. Menu names differ by phone, so look in your camera settings.

Why do my phone photos look grainy?

Graininess almost always means there was not enough light. The phone boosts the image to make it brighter, and that adds speckled noise. Move closer to a window or lamp, use night mode and hold very still, and switch to the 1x lens, which handles dim light best. Avoid pinch zoom in low light, as it makes grain much worse.

Should I use the 0.5x lens on my phone?

Use the 0.5x ultra-wide lens for small rooms, tall buildings and big views where you cannot step back. It is less flattering for people, because it stretches anything near the edges, so faces at the side of the frame look wider. For portraits, switch to 1x or 2x and keep any people near the middle if you do use the wide lens.

Is portrait mode better for photos of people?

Often, yes. Portrait mode blurs the background so your subject stands out, which flatters most faces. It works best when the person is a few feet away, about 1 to 2.5 meters, with some distance behind them. Check edges like hair and glasses, which can look smudged. In dim light or with groups, normal photo mode is sometimes the better choice.

When is the best time of day to take photos?

Golden hour, roughly the hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset, gives warm, soft light and long shadows that flatter people and scenery. Overcast days are also excellent for portraits and flowers. Midday sun is the hardest, with harsh shadows and squinting, so move your subject into open shade or turn them so the sun is behind or beside them.

How can I take photos of strangers politely when traveling?

Ask before photographing anyone up close, with a smile and a gesture toward your phone if you do not share a language, and accept a no gracefully. Wider street scenes where people are small are generally fine. Respect signs in museums and places of worship, which may ban photography or flash, and always be aware of your surroundings and your belongings.

Start today with the quickest wins: wipe your lens, switch on gridlines, then take one photo of someone you love beside a window with the room lights off, tapping their face before you shoot. Compare it with your usual snapshots, and you will see how much better your phone can do with just light and focus.
