Hiking for Beginners: How to Plan and Enjoy Your First Day Hike

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You want to get outside and walk a real trail, but you are not sure how far is too far, what to wear, or what happens if the weather turns. The short answer: start with a well-marked loop of 2 to 4 miles (3 to 6 km) with less than about 500 feet (150 m) of climbing, check the forecast, carry the ten essentials and about half a liter to 1 liter of water per hour, and leave your route and return time with someone you trust.

This guide walks you through choosing a first trail, estimating how long it will take, gear that is worth buying, packing a day pack, walking uphill and down, staying safe and a 6-week plan to build your fitness.

How to choose your first hiking trail

A good first trail is short, popular and clearly marked. Look for most of these:

  • 2 to 4 miles (3 to 6 km) in total
  • Less than about 500 feet (150 m) of climbing
  • A clear, well-used trail with markers
  • A loop or out-and-back route, so you cannot miss the way home
  • A trailhead with parking and, ideally, restrooms
  • No river crossings without bridges and no exposed edges
  • Close to home, so you are not tired before you start

Rangers, visitor center staff and outdoor store workers are often happy to recommend a beginner trail. Tell them how far you walk comfortably now.

LevelDistanceClimbingTerrain
EasyUp to 4 miles (6 km)Under 500 ft (150 m)Wide, even, well marked
Moderate4 to 8 miles (6 to 13 km)500 to 1,500 ft (150 to 450 m)Some rough or steep parts
Challenging8 to 12 miles (13 to 19 km)1,500 to 3,000 ft (450 to 900 m)Rocky, steep, less marked

Rating systems differ between countries and parks, so always read the full trail description. After two or three comfortable easy hikes, increase either distance or climbing, but not both at once.

How long will a hike take?

A simple rule works for most beginners on reasonable paths:

  1. Allow about 1 hour for every 2 miles (3 km).
  2. Add about 30 minutes for every 1,000 feet (300 m) of climbing.
  3. Add about 10 minutes per hour for breaks, photos and lunch.

For example, a 4-mile trail with 600 feet of climbing works out at 2 hours walking, plus about 18 minutes for the climb, plus 20 minutes of breaks: roughly 2 hours 40 minutes. Rough or muddy ground, heat, small children and steep, rocky descents all slow you down. Compare your real times with your estimates and adjust.

Start early, so you have spare daylight. Then set a turn-around time: the time you will turn back wherever you are, so you finish well before dark. Sticking to it, even short of the viewpoint, is one of the best safety habits a hiker can have.

What to wear hiking

Dress in layers you can add or remove as you warm up, stop or climb into wind.

  • Base layer. A wool or synthetic top that moves sweat away from your skin.
  • Mid layer. A fleece or light insulated jacket for warmth at breaks and summits.
  • Outer shell. A waterproof, windproof jacket, even if the forecast looks fine.
  • Hiking pants (trousers) or shorts that dry quickly, plus a hat and gloves in cooler months.

Avoid cotton in cool or wet conditions. Jeans and cotton T-shirts soak up water and stay wet, which chills you quickly. In the cold that can become dangerous.

For footwear, comfortable sneakers (trainers) with a decent grip are fine on gentle trails. Trail runners are light and dry fast; hiking boots give more ankle support and protection on rocky ground. Leave about a thumb's width in front of your longest toe so your toes do not hit the front on descents, and break new boots in on short walks first. Wear wool or synthetic hiking socks, and stop to tape any warm, sore hot spot the moment you feel it, before it becomes a blister.

The ten essentials and what to pack

Even on a short hike, carry these ten essentials in a small day pack (rucksack). Most of them weigh very little and live in the pack permanently.

Add a fully charged phone and power bank, a whistle, a small trash bag and toilet paper with a sealable bag to pack it out. Heavy items go close to your back, and things you need often go in outside pockets.

Gear that can wait includes expensive boots for easy trails, technical jackets and gadgets. Renting or buying used is a good way to try things first.

How much water and food to bring hiking

As a general guide, plan on about half a liter to 1 liter (roughly 1 pint to 1 quart) of water for every hour of hiking in moderate conditions, and more in heat, on steep climbs or if you sweat a lot. For a three-hour hike on a mild day, that means about 1.5 to 3 liters. Carry a little extra in case the hike runs long.

Drink little and often rather than gulping at the end. A dry mouth, headache, dark urine or unusual tiredness are signs you need more. On hot days or long hikes, electrolytes added to one bottle can help replace salt lost in sweat.

Water from streams and lakes can carry germs even when it looks clean. Treat it with a filter, purifying tablets or by boiling before drinking.

Eat a proper meal before you set off and take one snack for every hour of walking, plus lunch on longer hikes. Simple ideas: a cheese and pickle sandwich (or wrap) and a bag of trail mix with nuts and dried fruit. Pack out all your trash, including fruit peel.

Walking uphill and downhill on the trail

Find a pace you could keep all day, one where you can still talk. If you are gasping, slow down. Groups should walk at the pace of the slowest person, with them near the front.

Uphill, take shorter steps and keep a steady rhythm rather than charging and stopping. On steep sections, try the rest step: as you step up, briefly straighten and rest your back leg so your bones take the weight for a moment, then step again.

Downhill, slow down, keep your knees slightly bent and take small steps. Place your feet flat where you can and watch for loose stones. Trekking poles take some load off your knees on descents and help balance on rough ground. Set them so your elbow is at about a right angle on the flat, a little shorter for climbs and longer for descents.

Take short breaks of 5 to 10 minutes each hour to drink and eat. Long sit-downs let your muscles stiffen and you cool off.

On the trail, uphill hikers usually have the right of way, dogs stay on a leash where required, and you leave gates as you found them. Follow leave no trace habits: stay on the trail, take everything home and leave plants and wildlife alone.

Hiking safety and what to do if you get lost

Before every hike, check the weather and trail conditions, and leave a trip plan with someone you trust: your route, start time, expected finish time and when they should call for help. Tell them when you are back.

  • Weather changes faster in the hills. If storms build, descend from summits and ridges and avoid open high ground.
  • Do not cross fast-flowing water or water above your knees. Turn back instead.
  • Check for ticks after hikes in grass or woodland, and remove any with fine tweezers.
  • In the UK, walk calmly around cattle, especially with calves, and let go of your dog's lead if cattle chase you.

If you get lost, stop, think, observe and plan. Sit down, have a drink and think back to where you last knew your position. Look for markers and landmarks, and check your phone's map. If you can confidently retrace your steps, do it carefully. If you are truly lost, stay put, keep warm, wear something bright and blow three blasts on a whistle. Call 911 in the US and Canada, 999 or 112 in the UK, 000 in Australia or 111 in New Zealand.

Talk to your doctor first if you have a heart or lung condition, high blood pressure, diabetes, joint problems or have been inactive for a while. Call for urgent help for chest pain, severe breathlessness, confusion, or signs of heat stroke (hot skin, confusion, collapse) or hypothermia (uncontrollable shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness).

A 6-week plan to get fit for hiking

Three weekday walks and one longer weekend walk build hiking fitness without a gym. Walk briskly on weekdays and easily on the weekend, and take at least one full rest day a week.

WeekWeekday walksWeekend walk
13 x 20 min, flat40 min, about 2 miles (3 km)
23 x 25 min, one gentle hill1 hour, gentle hills
33 x 30 min, 5 min hills or stairs1.5 hours with a day pack
43 x 25 min, easy week1 hour, easy
53 x 35 min, 10 min hills or stairs2 hours, some climbing
63 x 40 min, 10 to 15 min hills or stairs2.5 to 3 hours, a trail hike

Add a 15-minute strength and balance routine once or twice a week, such as sit to stand from a chair, step-ups on a stair, calf raises and standing on one leg near a counter. Repeat any week that feels too hard.

Frequently asked questions

How far should a beginner hike?

Start with 2 to 4 miles (3 to 6 km) on a well-marked trail with less than about 500 feet (150 m) of climbing. That usually takes 1.5 to 2.5 hours with breaks. If you finish feeling you could have walked further, increase distance or climbing on your next hike, but not both at once. Building up gradually keeps hiking enjoyable and helps avoid sore knees and blisters.

What should I bring on a day hike?

Carry the ten essentials: navigation, a headlamp, sun protection, a first aid kit, a knife or repair kit, a way to make fire, an emergency blanket, and extra food, water and clothes. Add a charged phone with offline maps, a power bank, a whistle and a trash bag. Pack a waterproof jacket even on sunny days, and leave your trip plan with someone at home.

Can I go hiking in sneakers?

Yes, on gentle, dry, well-kept trails, comfortable sneakers (trainers) with a decent grip are fine for beginners. As you move to rocky, muddy or steep ground, trail runners or hiking shoes give better grip and protection, and boots add ankle support for rough terrain and heavier packs. Whatever you choose, it should fit well, be broken in and leave room for your toes on descents.

Is it safe to hike alone as a beginner?

It can be, with extra care. Choose popular, well-marked trails, check the weather, carry the ten essentials and a charged phone, and always leave a trip plan with your route and return time. Set a turn-around time and stick to it. Many beginners feel more confident hiking with a friend or a local hiking group first, then going solo on familiar trails.

What should you not wear hiking?

Avoid cotton in cool or wet conditions, including jeans and cotton T-shirts, because cotton soaks up water and stays wet, which can chill you dangerously. Skip brand-new boots that have not been broken in, cotton socks that cause blisters, and anything too warm to walk uphill in. Wear layers of wool or synthetic fabric and carry a waterproof jacket.

How do I get in shape for hiking?

Walk regularly and build up gradually. A simple plan is three brisk 20 to 40 minute walks during the week, adding hills or stairs, and one longer weekend walk that grows from 40 minutes to 2 or 3 hours over six weeks, carrying a day pack. Add strength moves such as step-ups, sit to stands and calf raises once or twice a week. Check with your doctor first if you have health concerns.

Your first step today: find one well-marked loop trail of 2 to 4 miles near you, work out how long it should take using the 2 miles per hour rule, and write a short trip plan you can give to a friend.
