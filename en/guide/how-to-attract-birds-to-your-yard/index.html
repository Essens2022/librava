How to Attract Birds to Your Yard or Garden: 9 Simple Steps

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have hung a feeder, or you are about to, and you would love to look out of the window and see more than the odd sparrow. The short answer: offer sunflower seed in a simple tube feeder about 10 feet (3 m) from a shrub, add a shallow dish of clean water, and give the birds two to three weeks to find it without moving anything.

Every wild bird is looking for four things: food, water, shelter and somewhere to nest. The more of those your yard (garden, in the UK) offers, the more birds stop by and the longer they stay. The nine steps below work for a big suburban lot, a small town garden or a fourth floor balcony, in North America and the UK alike.

Step 1. Start with one tube feeder

Shops sell dozens of feeder shapes, but nearly all of them fall into six types: tube, hopper, platform or tray, suet cage (fat ball feeder in the UK), nyjer, and hummingbird feeders, which are useful in North America only. If you buy just one, make it a tube feeder. It keeps seed fairly dry, it is easy to clean, and it suits the small birds most people want to see.

Step 2. Fill it with the right bird seed

The single best food for attracting birds on both sides of the Atlantic is sunflower. Black oil sunflower seed has a thin shell that small beaks crack easily and is the usual choice in North America. Sunflower hearts have the shell removed, so nothing is wasted and there is no pile of husks under the feeder, which is why they are so popular in UK gardens.

Be wary of the cheapest mixed seed. Many bargain bags are bulked out with red millet, milo (sorghum), wheat or split peas, which most feeder birds flick onto the ground. That waste goes moldy and draws rats and pigeons. A good mix is mostly sunflower with some white millet or chopped peanuts.

  • Sunflower seed or hearts: tits, chickadees, finches, cardinals, nuthatches, house sparrows.
  • Nyjer: goldfinches and siskins, in a special nyjer feeder. Buy small amounts, since birds ignore it once it goes stale.
  • White proso millet on a tray or the ground: sparrows, juncos, doves, dunnocks.
  • Suet or fat balls: woodpeckers, nuthatches, tits and starlings, especially in cold weather.
  • Mealworms and chopped fruit: robins, bluebirds, blackbirds and thrushes that rarely eat seed.

Step 3. Put the feeder in the right place

A plain feeder in a good spot will outdraw a fancy one in a bad spot. Use these placement rules:

  1. About 10 feet (3 m) from dense cover such as a shrub, hedge or small tree. Birds can dash to safety when a hawk passes, but a cat cannot hide right beside the feeder.
  2. Either very close to a window, under 3 feet (1 m), or well away from it, over 30 feet (10 m). This cuts the risk of birds hitting the glass hard.
  3. Where you can see it from a chair you use every day, with light falling on the birds rather than behind them.
  4. Somewhere you can reach easily in rain and snow, so it actually gets refilled in February.

If birds still strike your windows, add decals, tape strips or a pattern on the outside of the glass spaced about 2 inches (5 cm) apart. A single silhouette sticker in the middle of a big pane rarely helps on its own.

Step 4. Add a shallow birdbath

Water is the most overlooked way to attract birds, and often the cheapest. Birds drink and bathe all year, and a bath brings in species that never touch a feeder, such as American robins in North America or song thrushes in the UK. Many people find a bath adds more variety than a second feeder.

  • Depth: 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) at the deepest point, with a gentle slope. Small birds stand in water; they do not swim.
  • Surface: rough stone, concrete or unglazed clay, or add a few flat stones to a slippery bath.
  • Position: in view of a window, about 10 to 15 feet (3 to 5 m) from shrubs, in partial shade to slow algae.
  • Upkeep: change the water every day or two in warm weather and scrub it weekly. Never add chemicals.

An upturned plant saucer works fine. For extra pull, hang a jug with a pinhole above the bath so it drips; birds notice the sound and sparkle of moving water from a long way off. In freezing weather, pour in warm (never boiling) water each morning or use a heater made for outdoor birdbaths. Never add salt, antifreeze or glycerin.

Step 5. Give birds shelter and native plants

Small birds will not linger in a wide open space with nowhere to hide. Picture the edge of a wood and copy it in miniature: trees for lookouts, shrubs and hedges in the middle, flowers and grasses lower down, and leaf litter on the ground. Even one dense shrub planted near your feeders this year changes how birds use the space. Evergreens such as holly, juniper and yew give winter shelter and safe roosts at night.

Native plants are the best long term bird feeders you can own, because local insects live on them, and insects are what most songbirds feed their chicks. A single pair of chickadees or blue tits may need several thousand caterpillars to raise one brood.

You also have permission to be a little less tidy. A brush pile of crisscrossed branches 3 to 5 feet (1 to 1.5 m) high, at least 10 feet (3 m) from the feeders, gives wrens, sparrows and dunnocks a hiding place. Leave seed heads standing and sweep fallen leaves under shrubs in fall (autumn), then cut back in late spring. Skip pesticides and slug pellets, which remove the insects parent birds rely on.

Step 6. Put up a nest box before spring

Chickadees, titmice, bluebirds and wrens in North America, and blue tits and great tits in the UK, nest in holes. A good box stands in for a hollow in an old tree and can bring the same pair back year after year. Put it up in late winter, before the breeding season begins, though a box hung in fall gives birds time to find it and may be used for winter roosting.

Choose plain, untreated wood about 3/4 inch (15 to 20 mm) thick, with no perch, small drainage and ventilation holes, and a side or roof that opens for cleaning. Mount small-hole boxes 5 to 10 feet (1.5 to 3 m) up, facing away from the prevailing wind and strong afternoon sun.

Wild birds and their active nests are protected by law, under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act in the US and the Wildlife and Countryside Act in the UK. Watch from about 30 feet (10 m) away or through binoculars, and clean the box out only in fall (autumn) once it is empty.

Step 7. Avoid foods that harm birds

Some kind gestures do more harm than good. Keep these off your bird table:

  • Bread as a main food. It fills birds up with very little nutrition.
  • Anything salted, such as salted peanuts, chips (crisps in the UK) or bacon.
  • Cooking fat and roasting pan drippings, which stay soft, go rancid and smear feathers.
  • Milk, which birds cannot digest properly.
  • Desiccated coconut, which can swell after it is eaten.
  • Moldy or stale seed, bread or fruit.

Two safety habits matter most. Always remove the plastic mesh bag from fat balls before hanging them, because small birds can trap their feet or tongues in it. And offer peanuts only in a mesh peanut feeder or chopped up, since whole peanuts can choke nestlings in spring and summer. Use peanuts sold for birds or people, never salted or roasted ones.

Step 8. Keep feeders and baths clean

Busy feeders can spread disease, so cleaning is part of attracting birds, not a chore you can skip. Clean seed feeders about every two weeks, and more often in warm, wet weather or when lots of birds are visiting. Empty the feeder, scrub it in hot soapy water or a weak bleach solution (about 1 part household bleach to 9 parts water), rinse well and let it dry completely before refilling.

Rake up old seed and droppings under the feeder, and put out only what your birds finish in a day or two. Store seed in a metal bin with a tight lid to keep out damp and mice.

In some years wildlife officials ask people in certain areas to take feeders down for a while to slow a disease outbreak, so follow your local or national wildlife agency. In bear country, check local guidance too, as many areas recommend bringing feeders in from spring to fall.

Step 9. Be patient while birds find your feeder

Most songbirds find food by sight and by watching other birds, not by smell. A new feeder is an unfamiliar object in their daily route, and wary birds stay alive. It can take a few days, and sometimes two or three weeks, before the first regular visitors arrive. Chickadees and titmice in North America, and blue tits and great tits in the UK, are often the first to discover it, and their mixed winter flocks then follow.

To speed things up, scatter a small handful of seed on the ground or a tray under the feeder so passing birds notice it. Then leave the feeder where it is. Moving it every few days resets the clock.

Watch in the first few hours after sunrise and again in late afternoon, when birds refuel.

Quick checklist when no birds come

  • Is the seed fresh, dry and mostly sunflower?
  • Is there cover about 10 feet (3 m) away?
  • Has a cat or a hawk been hanging around?
  • Has it been at least three weeks in the same spot?
  • Is natural food plentiful right now, such as in late summer and fall?

Frequently asked questions

What is the best bird seed to attract the most birds?

Sunflower is the best single food. Black oil sunflower seed is the standard in North America, and sunflower hearts, which have no shells, are popular in the UK because nothing is wasted. More feeder birds eat sunflower than any other seed, including tits, chickadees, finches, cardinals and nuthatches. Avoid cheap mixes padded with red millet, wheat or split peas, which most birds throw on the ground.

How long does it take birds to find a new feeder?

Usually anywhere from a few days to two or three weeks. Birds are naturally cautious about new objects, so the first visitors may take time to trust it. Scatter a little seed on the ground below to catch their eye, place the feeder near cover where birds already pass, and do not move it while you wait, because each move starts the process over again.

Why are there no birds at my bird feeder?

Common reasons are stale or damp seed, a feeder in a wide open spot with no cover nearby, a cat or hawk hunting close by, or plenty of natural food elsewhere, which is normal in late summer and fall. Check the seed, move cover closer or the feeder nearer a shrub, keep it clean, and give the birds a few weeks.

Should I feed birds all year round?

You can. Feeding is most helpful in winter, when natural food is scarce and nights are long and cold, but many people feed all year. In summer, keep food fresh, put out smaller amounts, avoid soft fat that can go rancid, and offer peanuts only in mesh feeders or chopped. Follow any local advice to pause feeding during disease outbreaks or in bear country.

Do birds need water in winter?

Yes. Birds drink and bathe all winter, and when puddles freeze they may fly a long way to find water or eat snow, which costs them body heat. A bath with liquid water can make your yard the busiest on the street. Add warm, never boiling, water each morning or use a heater made for outdoor birdbaths, and never add salt or antifreeze.

Is it okay to feed bread to wild birds?

A few crumbs now and then will not harm birds, but bread should never be a main food. It fills them up without giving them the fat and protein they need, and moldy bread can make them seriously ill. Offer sunflower seed, suet or fat balls, mealworms or fresh fruit instead, which give far more energy for the same space in their stomachs.

Your first step today is simple: hang one tube feeder of sunflower seed about 10 feet (3 m) from a shrub, set a shallow dish of water nearby, and write the date in a notebook so you can see how long the first visitor takes.
