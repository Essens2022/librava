How to Stop Hot Flashes: Fast Relief Now and Treatments That Help

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If heat is suddenly rushing up your chest and face in the middle of a meeting, or you are waking up soaked at 3 a.m., you want it to stop now. The short answer: cool your face, neck and wrists and start slow belly breathing at the first warning sign, cut your personal triggers, and talk to your doctor, because hormone therapy is the most effective treatment and non-hormonal options exist too.

Hot flashes (called hot flushes in the UK, Australia and New Zealand) are not dangerous in themselves, but they can wreck your sleep, your concentration and your confidence. This guide covers quick relief, nighttime fixes, triggers and the treatments worth asking about.

Why hot flashes happen

Your brain has a built-in thermostat that keeps your body temperature in a comfortable range. As estrogen falls and swings around in perimenopause and menopause, that thermostat seems to become oversensitive, so the comfortable range narrows.

A tiny rise in temperature you would never have noticed before now sets off a full cooling response: blood rushes to your skin, you sweat and your heart speeds up. Your body is not dangerously overheating; it is overreacting to a small change. That is also why small things like a hot drink or a stuffy room can tip you over.

A flash usually lasts a few minutes and is often followed by chills as the sweat cools. Many people get a warning first, such as pressure in the head, a sense of unease or a tingle in the skin. Night sweats are simply hot flashes that happen while you sleep.

They can start while your periods are still regular and often carry on for several years after your last period, sometimes longer. You do not have to wait them out.

What to do the moment a hot flash starts

Acting at the first warning sign tends to work better than gritting your teeth. Keep a simple routine you can follow without thinking:

  1. Start slow belly breathing (see below) as soon as you feel the prickle.
  2. Loosen or peel off a layer.
  3. Cool your face, neck and wrists: a fan, a cold damp cloth, or cold water run over your wrists.
  4. Sip cold water.
  5. Once it passes, dry off and add a thin layer back if you feel chilly.

Cooling tips at home, at work and out

The aim is to give your touchy thermostat more room before it reacts.

  • Dress in thin layers, with cotton or a moisture-wicking fabric next to your skin. Several thin layers beat one thick sweater (jumper), because you can adjust both ways.
  • Keep a small fan in the rooms you use most and a folding or handheld fan in your bag.
  • Keep rooms cool and open a window when you can.
  • Carry a water bottle and keep a spray bottle of plain water in the fridge.
  • At work, sit near a window or air vent if possible, and ask whether a lighter uniform fabric is available.
  • For travel, choose a seat where you can reach an air vent or get up, and pack a spare thin top.

A small bag kit helps a lot: a folding fan, a bottle of water, face wipes, a spare top and deodorant. It turns a flash on a crowded train from a crisis into a nuisance.

Find and cut your hot flash triggers

Not everyone has triggers, and they vary from person to person. The ones people mention most often are:

  • hot drinks and hot rooms
  • spicy food
  • alcohol, especially red wine for some people
  • caffeine
  • stress, rushing and feeling flustered
  • smoking
  • tight or synthetic clothing

Keep a simple symptom diary for two weeks: note the time of each flash, how strong it felt, and what you ate, drank or were doing beforehand. Then look for patterns. One woman, Jen, found her worst flashes came about 20 minutes after her afternoon coffee; another, Priya, noticed hers clustered on mornings she was running late.

Change one thing at a time for a week and see whether it helps. If dropping your second coffee makes no difference, have it back. Stopping smoking is worth it regardless, since smoking is linked with more hot flashes and harms your bones and heart.

How to stop night sweats ruining your sleep

Night sweats disturb sleep, and poor sleep makes almost every other symptom feel worse. These steps help most:

  • Keep the bedroom cool, with a window open or a fan on if practical.
  • Wear light cotton or moisture-wicking nightclothes, or less.
  • Use layered bedding, such as a sheet plus a light blanket, instead of one heavy comforter (duvet), so you can throw a layer off without fully waking.
  • If you share a bed, try separate covers. It solves a lot of nighttime tug of war.
  • Keep cold water, a spare set of nightclothes and a small towel or spare pillowcase by the bed.
  • Cut back on alcohol and caffeine later in the day, and keep regular sleep and wake times.

When a sweat wakes you, deal with it in low light, flip to the dry pillowcase, and use slow breathing to settle back down rather than checking the clock.

Slow paced breathing and CBT for hot flashes

Slow paced breathing costs nothing and you can do it anywhere. Breathing slowly from your belly seems to calm the body's stress response, and some people find flashes feel less intense or pass more quickly.

Try it for one week

  1. Sit comfortably with one hand on your belly.
  2. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 5, feeling your belly rise.
  3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of 5.
  4. Keep this rhythm, about 6 breaths a minute, for 10 minutes, twice a day.
  5. When a flash starts, switch straight into the same rhythm.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has good evidence for hot flashes, night sweats, sleep and mood. It may not cut the number of flashes, but it changes how distressing and disruptive they feel. A typical course runs 4 to 6 sessions, one to one or in a group, and covers breathing, sleep techniques and testing worried thoughts such as "everyone is staring at me". Ask your doctor (GP) how to find it locally, or look for guided self-help versions.

Hormone therapy for hot flashes

Hormone therapy (HRT or MHT) is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats. For many people they improve a great deal within a few weeks to a few months. It can also help sleep, mood swings, joint aches and vaginal dryness, and it helps prevent bone loss.

  • Estrogen comes as tablets, patches, gels or sprays. Patches and gels carry a lower risk of blood clots than tablets.
  • If you still have a womb (uterus), you need a progestogen with the estrogen to protect the womb lining.
  • For most healthy people under 60 or within about 10 years of their last period, current guidance suggests the benefits generally outweigh the risks, but your personal and family history matters.
  • Combined therapy is linked to a small increase in breast cancer risk, depending on the type and how long you take it.
  • Extra care or other options may be needed if you have had breast cancer, blood clots, stroke, liver disease or unexplained bleeding.

There is no fixed time limit for most people; review it with your doctor each year. Do not buy hormones online or use unregulated compounded "bioidentical" mixtures, which are not tested in the same way and may not protect your womb lining. Hormone therapy is not a contraceptive.

Non-hormonal treatments and natural remedies

If you cannot take hormones, or would rather not, there are prescription medicines that are not hormones and can reduce how often hot flashes happen and how strong they feel. Some come from the antidepressant family, some were first used for nerve pain or blood pressure, and newer ones target the brain's temperature control directly. They usually help a little less than hormone therapy, and each has its own side effects and interactions.

You do not need to know the names. You can simply say: "I cannot take hormones, or would prefer not to. What non-hormonal options could help my hot flashes?"

Herbal remedies and supplements fill whole shelves, but the evidence for most is limited, mixed or weak, and none is recommended as a reliable treatment. Quality varies between bottles, and some herbs interact with medicines, including contraceptives, blood thinners and cancer treatments. Plant estrogens are not automatically safe if you have been told to avoid estrogen.

Be wary of anything that promises to "balance your hormones naturally", relies on glowing personal stories, or uses a home saliva test to diagnose an imbalance. The book Menopause Made Simple has a side by side table of hormone and non-hormonal options with questions to take to your appointment.

When to see a doctor about hot flashes

You do not need to be in crisis to ask for help. If hot flashes or night sweats affect your sleep, work, mood or enjoyment of life, that is reason enough to book an appointment. Bring two weeks of your symptom diary and a short list of questions.

Check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement, herbal remedy or new medicine, or changing your diet or exercise a lot. Sweats can sometimes have other causes, such as thyroid problems, infections or some medicines, so see a doctor if they come with fever, unexplained weight loss or feeling generally unwell. Any bleeding after menopause, even spotting, needs prompt checking. Call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand) if a flash comes with chest pain, breathlessness or fainting, or for stroke signs.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to stop a hot flash?

Act at the first warning sign. Start slow belly breathing, loosen a layer, and cool your face, neck and wrists with a fan, cold water or a cool damp cloth. Sip cold water while it passes. You may not be able to switch a flash off instantly, but many people find it feels milder and ends sooner when they respond straight away rather than waiting it out.

How long do hot flashes last?

Each hot flash usually lasts a few minutes, though it can feel longer, and may be followed by chills. Over the years, hot flashes often start in perimenopause and continue for several years after the last period. For a smaller number of people they last much longer. Treatment can help at any stage, so there is no need to simply wait for them to stop.

What foods trigger hot flashes?

Common food and drink triggers include spicy food, hot drinks, caffeine and alcohol, with red wine bothering some people more than others. Triggers vary a lot from person to person, and some people have none at all. Keep a symptom diary for two weeks, then change one thing at a time for a week to see whether it actually makes a difference before giving anything up for good.

Are there natural remedies for hot flashes that work?

The non-drug approaches with the best support are slow paced breathing, cognitive behavioral therapy, cooling strategies, avoiding personal triggers, stopping smoking and staying active. Herbal remedies and supplements have limited or mixed evidence, their quality varies, and some interact with medicines. If you want to try one, talk to your doctor or pharmacist first and give it a set trial period.

Why are my hot flashes worse at night?

Night sweats are hot flashes that happen while you sleep, and a warm bedroom, heavy bedding, alcohol or caffeine later in the day can make them more likely. Because they wake you, they can feel worse than daytime flashes. A cool room, layered bedding, light nightclothes, separate covers and a glass of cold water by the bed help many people.

Can hot flashes happen before periods stop?

Yes. Hot flashes and night sweats often begin in perimenopause, sometimes while your periods are still fairly regular, because hormone levels rise and fall unpredictably. If you are in your 40s and getting flashes, poor sleep or changing periods, talk to your doctor. Pregnancy is still possible in perimenopause, so keep using contraception until your doctor says it is safe to stop.

Today, write your own three-step hot flash plan on a card, put a small fan and a bottle of water in your bag, and start a two-week symptom diary you can take to your doctor.
