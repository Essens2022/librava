Calligraphy for Beginners: How to Start With a Brush Pen Today

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have seen those flowing letters on cards and wedding signs, and you would love to make them yourself, but your handwriting feels more like a shopping list than a work of art. Good news: modern calligraphy for beginners comes down to one rule (thin lines going up, thick lines going down), one small brush pen, smooth paper and about 15 minutes of slow practice a day.

This guide walks you through the kit, the grip, the eight basic strokes every letter is built from, how to join letters into words and a simple 30-day plan. You can start tonight with almost nothing.

What you need to start calligraphy

You need far less than the craft aisle suggests. Beginners who buy a big set of pens and thick textured paper usually fray their brush tips within an hour, because rough paper acts like sandpaper on the fibers.

A starter kit that usually costs around $25 to $40 (roughly £20 to £30):

  • One small felt-tip brush pen in black or another dark color. Felt tips spring back into shape, so they are much easier than bristle tips.
  • One large brush pen, added after a week or two once the basic strokes feel familiar.
  • One black fineliner, about 0.3 to 0.5 mm, for faux calligraphy and small details.
  • An HB pencil (a No. 2 pencil in the US), a soft eraser (a rubber in the UK) and a sharpener.
  • A clear 12 in (30 cm) ruler.
  • A pad of smooth paper: marker paper (often sold as layout paper in the UK) or smooth laser paper around 32 lb (120 gsm).

Not sure about a paper you already own? Run your fingertip across it, then across the back of a glossy magazine. The closer it feels to the magazine, the kinder it is to your pen. Then make a few test strokes in a corner and check that the edges stay crisp.

Skip dip pens, metallic markers, color sets and watercolors for now. Buy one pen and get to know it well before you add another.

How to hold a brush pen for calligraphy

Most early frustration comes from setup, not talent. If your downstrokes come out thin and scratchy however hard you press, the pen is probably standing straight up.

  1. Hold the pen like an ordinary pen, between thumb and index finger, resting on your middle finger.
  2. Tilt it to about 45 degrees, leaning back toward your hand, so the side of the tip can lie on the paper when you press.
  3. Grip about 1 in (2 to 3 cm) above the tip, a little higher than for everyday writing.
  4. Keep the grip loose. If you cannot wiggle the pen slightly mid-stroke, you are squeezing too hard. Pressure comes from pressing down through the pen, not from tightening your fingers.
  5. Rest your whole forearm on the table and turn the paper 10 to 30 degrees to suit your hand. Slide the paper as you work so the spot you are lettering stays in front of you.

Left-handers can do all of this too. Many find that turning the paper clockwise and keeping the hand below the line of writing stops smudging, along with quick-drying ink.

Lettering is slow, careful work. Take a short break every 15 to 20 minutes to shake out your hand and roll your shoulders. If you feel pain, tingling or numbness, stop for the day, and see a doctor if it keeps coming back.

Draw guidelines so letters stay even

Guidelines are what make lettering look polished. They keep every letter the same height, sitting on the same line and leaning at the same angle.

LineWhat it doesLetters that touch it
Ascender lineTop of tall loopsb, d, h, k, l
X-height lineTop of small lettersa, c, e, n, o, u
BaselineWhere letters sitAll letters
Descender lineBottom of tailsg, j, p, q, y

Start with three equal spaces. With a small brush pen, an x-height of about 3/8 in (10 mm) is a comfortable size. Starting large helps, because big letters show you exactly where your pressure changes. Small letters hide wobbles, and that also hides what you need to fix.

Slant lines are optional. Modern calligraphy often leans at about 55 degrees from the baseline, but upright letters are fine too. What matters is that every downstroke leans the same way.

A time saver: draw one dark master sheet of guidelines in fineliner, then slide it under see-through marker paper and practice on top. One master sheet lasts for months.

The 8 basic calligraphy strokes

Every lowercase letter is built from a small set of shapes. Learn these eight and you already know most of the alphabet. The rule behind all of them is light pressure on the way up, heavy pressure on the way down, with the pressure changing gradually on curves.

StrokePressureFound in
Entrance stroke (upstroke)LightThe start of most letters
DownstrokeHeavyp, q and many stems
OverturnLight, then heavyh, m, n
UnderturnHeavy, then lighta, i, t, u
Compound curveLight, heavy, lighth, m, n
OvalLight, heavy, lighta, d, g, o
Ascending loopLight, then heavyb, d, h, k, l
Descending loopHeavy, then lightg, j, y

Two habits make these strokes work. First, go slowly: one downstroke should take about as long as saying "one, two" calmly. Second, lift the pen between strokes. A letter like n is not one movement. You draw one stroke, lift, and restart lightly right on top of where the last stroke ended, overlapping by a hair.

Watch the turns at the top and bottom of curves. They should be rounded like the top of a hill, not pointed like a mountain peak. A sharp point usually means you rushed the change of direction.

Calligraphy practice drills that build control

Drills feel dull, yet they are where the real progress happens. Do these on your guideline sheet, lifting between every stroke.

  1. Thin and thick lines. A row of upstrokes, a row of downstrokes, then alternate them in a zigzag.
  2. Waves. One continuous wavy line, pressing on every part that moves down and easing off on every part that moves up. This is the best drill for smooth transitions.
  3. Turns and ovals. A row each of overturns, underturns, compound curves and ovals, keeping spacing and slant even.
  4. Loops. A row of ascending loops and a row of descending loops, with the crossing points lined up.
  5. Pressure ladder. Five or six downstrokes from your lightest hairline to full pressure, each one a little heavier. This teaches you that pressure is a dial, not an on and off switch.

After each sheet, look at it from arm's length and ask three questions. Are the downstrokes all leaning the same way? Are the thick parts about the same width? Are the hairlines truly thin? Then fix one thing at a time. Trying to fix everything at once usually fixes nothing.

If your hairlines wobble, try moving a touch faster on the upstrokes only. Thin lines often look steadier at a slightly quicker pace.

How to connect letters into words

Single letters can look lovely and still turn into a messy word, because the spacing and joins are uneven. The key idea is simple: the thin exit stroke of one letter becomes the entrance stroke of the next. You do not add an extra stroke.

Joins fall into three groups:

  • Baseline to stem. The exit rises from the baseline into the next downstroke, as in "in" or "um". The word "minimum" is perfect practice.
  • Baseline to oval. The exit rises to just below the x-height line, where the oval of a, c, d, g, o or q begins. The connector should touch the oval at its start, not cut through its middle.
  • Top connections. After b, o, v and w, the exit leaves from the x-height line and travels sideways. Do not drag it down to the baseline and back up.

For spacing, leave about the same gap between letters as the space inside an n, nudging round letters slightly closer. Between words, leave about the width of a lowercase o. While you learn, you can pencil a tiny o lightly between words, letter around it and erase it once the ink is fully dry.

Good first words: in, mini, gold, idea, wave, hello, thanks. Write each one at least three times, slowly.

Faux calligraphy with any pen

No brush pen yet, or a project on rough card, wood or a chalkboard? Faux calligraphy gives you the same look with any pen that draws a steady line.

  1. Write the word in a relaxed joined-up style with a fineliner or gel pen, leaving a little extra space between letters.
  2. Find the downstrokes by tracing the word in the air. Every place the pen moved downward would be thick with a brush pen.
  3. Draw a second line beside each downstroke and fill the narrow channel with ink.

Add the extra line on the side that keeps the letter open: usually the right side of straight stems and the outside of curves, so the small holes inside letters stay clear. Aim for thick strokes about two to three times your line width, tapering gently at each end like a long leaf.

Faux work also trains your eye. Once you know exactly where thick strokes go, your brush pen pressure tends to get more confident. One more tip: wait at least five minutes before erasing pencil sketch lines, because ink that looks dry can still smear.

A 30-day calligraphy practice plan

Lettering is a physical skill, closer to learning an instrument than learning facts. Fifteen to twenty minutes a day beats one long weekend session, which tends to end with a tight grip and frustration.

Shape each session like this: 3 to 5 minutes of warm-up (ovals, waves and basic strokes), 10 to 12 minutes on the day's focus, then 2 to 3 minutes circling your best attempt and noting one thing to improve.

DaysFocus
1 to 7Basic strokes, lowercase letters, first connections
8 to 14Even spacing and slant, capitals, faux calligraphy
15 to 21Style choices, bouncy letters, simple flourishes
22 to 30A quote layout, a card and envelope, your own alphabet

On day 1, write "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" (it holds every letter) plus your name, date it and put it away. On day 30, write it again and compare. Keep every dated sheet in a folder too.

Around week two or three, most people hit a plateau where letters seem to get worse. It is normal and often comes just before a jump in skill. Go back to basic strokes, letter larger, switch to faux for a day or take a day off. Frequently asked questions

Can I teach myself calligraphy?

Yes. Modern brush calligraphy is very learnable at home with a brush pen, smooth paper and guidelines. Start with the eight basic strokes, practice 15 to 20 minutes a day, and compare dated sheets every week so you can see progress. Most beginners write steady, readable words within a few weeks of regular practice, and a full month gives you a solid foundation.

What is the best pen for calligraphy beginners?

A small brush pen with a felt tip is the easiest place to start. Its short, firm tip is forgiving and makes it simpler to control the change from thin to thick. Choose black or another dark color so you can see your strokes clearly. Add a large, softer brush pen after a week or two, and keep bristle tips for later.

Why does my brush pen not make thick lines?

Usually the pen is held too upright, so only the point touches the paper. Tilt it to about 45 degrees so the side of the tip can lie down when you press, and grip a little higher up the barrel. Also check that you are pressing down through the pen on downstrokes rather than squeezing harder with your fingers.

How long does it take to learn calligraphy?

With 15 to 20 minutes a day, most people see a clear difference in about 30 days: steadier downstrokes, thinner upstrokes and more even spacing. Developing a polished personal style takes longer, often several months. Short daily sessions work better than occasional long ones, because your hand learns pressure and speed through frequent repetition.

What paper is best for practicing calligraphy?

Smooth paper protects brush pen tips and keeps edges crisp. Marker paper (layout paper in the UK) is thin and slightly see-through, so you can slide a guideline sheet underneath. Smooth laser paper around 32 lb (120 gsm) is a good budget choice. Avoid textured, watercolor and cheap copy paper with brush pens, because they fray the tip and make ink bleed.

Is calligraphy hard for left-handed people?

No, left-handers can learn the same letters. Try turning the paper clockwise and keeping your hand below the line you are writing, which helps avoid smudging fresh ink. Use quick-drying ink, lift your hand slightly between strokes and keep adjusting the paper angle until thin upstrokes and thick downstrokes both feel natural.

Your first step today is small: find one smooth sheet of paper, draw a set of guidelines with a 3/8 in (10 mm) x-height, and fill one row each of light upstrokes and heavy downstrokes with your pen at 45 degrees. Date the sheet and keep it, because in a month you will want to see how far you have come.
