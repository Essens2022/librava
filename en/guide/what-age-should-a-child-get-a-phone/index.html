What Age Should a Child Get a Phone? A Calm Readiness Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your child has started asking for a phone, maybe every day, you are probably weighing safety and independence against sleep, schoolwork and everything you have read about screens. There is no single right age: readiness depends far more on your child's habits than their birthday, but many families start with a basic phone or kids' smartwatch around 10 to 12 and wait for a full smartphone until around 13 to 14, with social media later still.

This guide gives you a practical way to judge readiness, a checklist, the common ages families choose, the in-between options, and the first rules that matter most once you say yes.

Is there a right age for a first phone

Children grow at different speeds. One 10-year-old keeps track of their things, stops a game when the timer goes and tells you when a friend is unkind. Their twin might lose a water bottle a week and melt down whenever a screen goes off. Same birthday, very different readiness.

Age still matters in a few ways. Most major social platforms require users to be at least 13, and some countries are moving that limit higher, so check the current rules where you live. Your child's school may also have its own phone policy.

Within those outside limits, a better question than "How old?" is "What has my child shown me they can handle?" The best predictor of how a child will manage a phone is how they already manage similar responsibilities.

Signs your child is ready for a phone

You do not need a test. You already have years of evidence from ordinary family life. Look for these patterns, and look for "mostly" rather than "always".

  • They tell you things. A child who comes to you when something upsetting or embarrassing happens is far better protected online. This matters more than anything else on the list.
  • They look after their belongings. If coats and lunch boxes vanish weekly, a phone will probably join them.
  • They follow limits without a big fight. A bit of grumbling is normal. Repeated meltdowns, sneaking extra time or hiding devices suggest the pull of a phone may be too strong right now.
  • They can put things down for dinner, homework and bed.
  • They are usually kind and can say sorry. Online, a cruel joke travels fast and lasts.
  • They understand privacy: no sharing your address, their school name or photos with people they do not know.

Readiness is not pass or fail. Many children are ready for some parts of phone life, such as texting family and close friends, but not for open browsing or social media.

A first phone readiness checklist

Go through this with another adult who knows your child well, such as a co-parent, grandparent or trusted teacher. You are not after a perfect score, just an honest picture.

  • Keeps track of their belongings most of the time.
  • Usually stops a screen activity when asked, without a major struggle.
  • Tells you about upsetting or confusing things, including online.
  • Gets to bed and up for school without a long battle.
  • Manages homework mostly without constant reminders.
  • Understands that people online may not be who they say they are.
  • Knows not to share personal details or photos with strangers.
  • Treats others kindly and can apologize when wrong.
  • Can talk about rules without shouting or storming off, most of the time.
  • Has a real need, such as walking to school alone or keeping in touch with a parent who lives elsewhere.

If your child ticks most of these, they may be ready for a first step. If they tick only a few, you now know what to work on, and you have a calm, honest reason to wait.

Turn gaps into goals

Pick one or two gaps and make them goals with a date. For example: "If you can stop the tablet when the timer goes, without arguing, for the next month, we will talk about it again." Children notice fairness, and a yes they earned makes the rules easier to keep.

Common ages for a first phone

There is no correct age, but some patterns are common among families in the US and UK. Treat this as a rough map, not a rule.

Age rangeCommon first stepWhy families choose it
8 to 10Shared family phone, home tabletContact with adults, low risk
10 to 12Basic phone or kids' smartwatchWalking or traveling alone
12 to 14Smartphone with tight controlsMore independence, school travel
14 and upSmartphone with fewer controlsTrust built over time
13 to 16 or laterFirst social media accountPlatform age limits, maturity

A steady 11-year-old crossing a city alone might need more than this suggests. A 13-year-old who finds self-control hard might do better with a basic phone for another year.

In recent years many parents have chosen to delay smartphones, often until around 14, and to agree with other parents in the class so no child is the only one without one. Their reasons are practical: protecting sleep, attention for school and hobbies, and time to build friendships face to face first.

Basic phone, smartwatch or smartphone

You do not have to jump from no phone to a full smartphone. Start with the need. If the honest answer is "call me after school" or "let me know you got there", your child does not need an app store, a browser and a camera roll.

OptionGood forWatch out for
Shared family phoneAges 8 to 10, occasional outingsAwkward for older kids

A basic phone is the most popular first step among families who are waiting. Before buying, check what it can do with group texts and whether you can block unknown numbers. Present any in-between option as a first step, not a consolation prize, and say what the next step could be and what would help you say yes.

First phone rules that work

Whatever device you choose, write a short family agreement together before handing it over. One or two pages is enough, and specific beats vague: "no phone at the dinner table" works, "use it sensibly" does not.

TopicExample rule
OvernightPhone charges in the kitchen from 8:30 pm
MealsNo phones at the table, for anyone
New appsAsk first, decide together
MoneyNo purchases without a yes
StrangersOnly message people known in real life
ProblemsTell a parent, and you will not be in trouble for telling
ReviewEvery three months

That last problems rule sits above all the others. Children hide trouble when they fear losing the phone, so promise that telling you will never be punished, even when a rule was broken on the way.

Why the phone should sleep outside the bedroom

If you adopt only one rule, make it this: phones charge outside bedrooms overnight. At an agreed time each evening, the phone goes to a shared charging spot, such as a kitchen counter or hall shelf, and stays there until morning.

This works better than trying to limit use in the bedroom. A phone under the pillow asks a tired child to resist a buzzing group chat alone, in the dark. Taking it out of the room removes the decision, which many children quietly find a relief.

  • Start the rule on the very first night. It is far easier than introducing it months later.
  • Put adult phones on the same charging spot, so it feels like a household habit, not a punishment.
  • Buy a cheap alarm clock if "I need it as an alarm" comes up.

Setting up a child's first phone safely

Plan a quiet setup evening a day or two before handover, and allow about two hours the first time. Rushing is how settings get missed.

  1. Create a child account linked to yours, rather than letting your child use an adult account.
  2. Turn on the built-in parental controls: screen time limits, downtime at night, content filters and approval for app downloads and purchases.
  3. Check privacy settings, and turn off location sharing for apps that do not need it.
  4. Decide on family location sharing openly, and tell your child plainly that you can see where they are and why.
  5. Set a passcode that you know too.
  6. Invite your child for the last half hour and show them what you set up and why.

Menu names differ between phone systems and software versions, so look in the settings for screen time or family controls. The First Phone Playbook walks through each of these steps, plus scripts for the harder conversations.

Tell your child, before anything goes wrong, that if anyone online asks for photos, threatens them or makes them feel scared, they can come to you and you will help, not punish. If a child is being threatened or exploited, contact the police and report it to the platform. If your child seems very low, withdrawn or talks about harming themselves, speak to your doctor straight away, or call emergency services if they are in immediate danger.

Frequently asked questions

Is 10 too young for a phone?

Not for every phone. Many 10-year-olds manage a basic phone or kids' smartwatch well when the need is real, such as walking home from school. A full smartphone with apps, browsing and social media is a bigger step that many families delay until around 13 or 14. Judge by your child's habits, especially whether they tell you when something goes wrong.

What is the best first phone for a child?

The simplest one that meets the real need. If your child mainly needs to call and text family, a basic phone does that cheaply with few online risks. Younger children who lose things may do better with a smartwatch that calls approved contacts. If you choose a smartphone, set up a child account and parental controls before handing it over.

What age do most kids get a smartphone?

Many children in the US and UK get a smartphone somewhere between 11 and 13, often around the move to middle school or secondary school. A growing number of families are choosing to wait until around 14, usually with a basic phone in the meantime. What other families do is useful information, but your own child's readiness is a better guide than the class average.

How do I say no to a phone without a fight?

Say "not yet" instead of "no", and explain what ready looks like. Pick one or two goals from a readiness checklist, such as stopping screens when asked, and set a date to talk again. Listening first helps. When they say everyone has one, it often helps to talk to other parents, because many families are quietly waiting too.

Should I read my child's text messages?

Many families agree on occasional, open check-ins for a first phone, especially with younger children, and write that into the family agreement so it is not a surprise. Secret monitoring tends to damage trust once discovered. As your child shows good judgment, check less often. Your strongest safety tool is a child who knows they can tell you anything without losing the phone.

When should a child get social media?

Most major platforms set a minimum age of 13, and some countries have set higher limits, so check the rules where you live. Many parents wait longer, often until 15 or 16, because comparison and endless feeds are hard for younger teens. When you start, use private accounts, agree on followers together and use the account alongside your child at first.

Your first step this week: sit down with another adult who knows your child, go through the readiness checklist honestly and pick one gap to turn into a goal with a date. Whatever the answer, you will be saying yes or not yet for clear reasons your child can understand, and that makes every rule that follows easier to keep.
