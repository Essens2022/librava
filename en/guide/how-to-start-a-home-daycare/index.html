How to Start a Home Daycare: A Safe, Licensed 8-Step Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you are home with your own little ones, or you have worked with young children for years, caring for other families' children in your home can be a lovely way to earn. To start a home daycare (called childminding in the UK), you contact your local licensing agency, complete the required training and background checks, make your home safe with a written risk assessment, pass a home inspection, then set clear fees and contracts before the first child arrives.

The order matters, because in most places it is illegal to be paid for childcare in your home without the right approval once you pass certain limits. This guide takes you through each step with checklists you can use today. It is general information, not legal advice, so always follow your own licensing agency's rules.

Decide if home childcare truly suits you

Home childcare is rewarding, but it is long, physical work inside the place where you live. Full-time providers often work ten or eleven hour days, with cleaning, shopping and paperwork after the last child leaves. You rarely get a moment alone, you will catch every cold going in your first year, and if you are ill you may need to close.

Your home also becomes a workplace. Inspectors may visit, play areas take wear and tear, and your family shares their space, toys and parent every day. Before you apply, talk it through at home and answer these questions:

  • Can I handle noise, mess and constant responsibility for a full day?
  • How will my own children feel about sharing their home?
  • Do I want part-time or full-time hours?
  • Which ages do I want to care for: babies, toddlers, preschoolers or school-age children?
  • Can I keep one room completely child-free for my family?
Try a practice day on paper. Write out a full day hour by hour with the ages you plan to take, including meals, naps, diaper or nappy changes and school runs. Gaps and pinch points show up quickly.

Home daycare license requirements by country

Every country, and often every state, province or territory, has a body that regulates childcare. Find yours first through your local government website, your local childcare information service, a childcare association or other registered providers. Then ask for the information pack for new home-based providers.

  • United States. Most states require a license or registration for a family child care home once you care for more than a small number of unrelated children. Some states have two levels, such as a small family home and a larger group home with an assistant.
  • England. People paid to look after children under 8 in their own home for more than 2 hours a day usually must register with the national inspectorate or a childminder agency. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own regulators.
  • Canada sets rules by province or territory, often allowing a small number of children without a license.
  • In Australia, family day care educators usually work through an approved family day care service, and in New Zealand through a licensed home-based early childhood service provider.

"Just helping out a friend" for money can still count as childcare that needs approval. If you are unsure, ask your licensing agency in writing and keep their answer.

The steps from inquiry to approval

Most registrations follow the same pattern and take anywhere from a few weeks to several months, so start early.

  1. Inquiry. Request the information pack and attend any introductory session.
  2. Training: pediatric (paediatric) first aid, safeguarding or child protection, and often an introduction to home childcare or your early years framework. Food hygiene and safe sleep training are common too.
  3. Background checks for yourself and, in many places, every adult who lives in your home. These can take weeks.
  4. Application, with identity documents, references, a health declaration and proof of training.
  5. Home preparation, including your risk assessment and written policies.
  6. Inspection or visit, where an officer checks your home and asks how you would handle situations such as an allergic reaction.
  7. Approval. Your license or certificate usually comes with conditions, such as your maximum number of children.

Keep copies of everything in one folder for renewals and inspections. Set reminders for certificate renewal dates, because an expired first aid certificate can stop you operating.

Ratios and group sizes

Your approval will limit how many children you may care for at once, with lower limits for babies. Your own young children usually count. Plan your numbers for the busiest moment of the busiest day, such as a school holiday when your preschooler is home, and never go over, even for an hour.

Make your home safe with a risk assessment

A risk assessment is a written walk-through of your home: what could cause harm, who could be hurt and what you do to prevent it. Get down on the floor in every room and look at what a crawling baby could pull or a toddler could climb.

Home daycare safety checklist

  • Anchor tall furniture, bookcases and TVs to the wall.
  • Remove or tie up looped blind and curtain cords.
  • Fit stair gates top and bottom, and window restrictors.
  • Lock away medicines, cleaning products, dishwasher tablets and laundry pods.
  • Keep hot drinks out of reach and never hold one while holding a child.
  • Keep small objects away from under-3s; if it fits through a toilet roll tube, it is too small.
  • Cut grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthways into quarters, and sausages into strips.
  • Check fences, gates and outdoor play areas every morning.
  • Empty paddling pools and water trays straight after use, and never leave a child near water.
  • Fit and test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, and record each test.

Write a fire evacuation plan with two ways out, a plan for getting babies out, a meeting point and a grab-ready register and phone. Practice it with the children and record each drill.

Button batteries and magnets are emergencies. If you think a child has swallowed one, call emergency services immediately, even if the child seems fine, and do not try to make them vomit.

Health, safe sleep and safeguarding rules

Inspectors will ask about your health and safety routines, so put them in writing before you open.

  • An accident and incident record, signed by parents the same day
  • A sickness and exclusion policy that follows local public health guidance
  • A medicines policy with written permission and a record of every dose
  • Allergy and anaphylaxis plans for any child who needs one
  • Handwashing, diaper or nappy changing and cleaning routines

Safe sleep for babies

Always place babies on their back for every sleep, on a firm, flat mattress that fits the crib or cot snugly, with no pillows, bumpers, loose blankets or soft toys. Never let a baby sleep on a sofa, adult bed or in a car seat indoors. Check sleeping children regularly and record each check. A parent's request cannot override safe sleep guidance unless there are written medical instructions from the baby's doctor, and even then check with your licensing agency.

Safeguarding

You must know the signs of concern, how to record what you see and hear, and how to report it to your local child protection service. Have a collection password policy, a phones and photos policy and a clear plan for a child who is not collected.

Write contracts and policies parents trust

Most money arguments in home childcare come from things that were never written down. Every family should sign their own contract before the child starts. Include:

  • Days, hours, drop-off and pickup times
  • Your fees, what they cover and when they are paid
  • Any registration fee or deposit and whether it is refundable
  • Notice periods to end the contract or change hours
  • Fees during your holidays, their holidays and sickness on either side
  • Late pickup rules and fees
  • What you provide and what parents supply
  • A settling-in or trial period
  • Agreement to follow your policies, with signatures and dates

Be specific. "Fees may be charged during holidays" leads to arguments. "Fees are charged in full during the family's holidays and not during my two weeks of holiday each year" does not.

Alongside the contract, keep a registration form for each child with emergency contacts, authorized collectors, health details, allergies and routines, plus written permissions for things like outings, photos and emergency medical treatment. Ask a lawyer or your childcare association to check your contract before you use it.

How much to charge for home daycare

Fees vary enormously by area, so start by researching what five local providers charge and exactly what each fee includes, such as meals, diapers or nappies and outings. Then list your own costs: registration, insurance, training, food, toys, cleaning supplies, extra heating and laundry, outings and admin.

A worked example with made-up round numbers: at 10 per hour, a family booking 8 hours a day, 3 days a week pays 240 a week, or 960 on a 4-week monthly basis. Add a one-off registration fee of 50 and the first invoice is 1,010.

Many providers charge a retainer, meaning families pay to keep their place whether or not the child attends, because you cannot fill odd days with another child. Whatever you choose, write it down and apply it to every family. Invoice on the same date each month, ask for fees in advance and check which government funding or subsidy schemes you can join.

Find families and fill your places

Once you are approved, let people know. Word of mouth is often the strongest source, so tell friends, neighbors and local parent groups. Register with your local childcare information service, set up a simple website or social media page, add a local map listing and put flyers on community noticeboards.

Write a two-line summary of your setting: where you are, the ages and hours you offer and what makes your days special, such as outdoor play every day. Answer inquiries quickly and invite families for a visit when children are present, so they can see your routine in action.

Keep a waiting list once you are full, ask happy families for references and stay inspection ready all year. The book Home Daycare Business Made Simple includes a full startup checklist, contract and policy lists and a 90-day plan if you want a step by step companion.

Insurance and the boring essentials

  • Public liability insurance for home childcare, which many agencies require
  • Business use cover on your car if you drive children
  • Telling your home insurer, mortgage lender or landlord
  • A separate bank account and simple records for tax

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a license to watch kids in my home?

In most places, yes, once you are paid to care for more than a small number of unrelated children or for more than a few hours a day. In England, paid care for children under 8 for over 2 hours a day usually needs registration. Some US states and Canadian provinces exempt very small arrangements. Ask your licensing agency in writing before you take any child.

How many children can I look after at home?

Your license or registration sets the limit, and it varies by place. Rules usually cap the total number of children, how many can be under school age and how many can be babies, which is often a very small number. Your own young children usually count. Some places allow more children if you have an approved assistant.

How long does it take to get a home daycare license?

Expect anywhere from a few weeks to several months. Background checks for you and other adults in your household can take weeks on their own, and you may need to complete first aid and safeguarding training, prepare your home and wait for an inspection visit. Start the checks and training as early as your licensing agency allows.

Can I start a home daycare if I rent?

Often yes, but you need your landlord's permission, and many rental agreements limit business use. Ask in writing before you apply. Check any building, condo or strata rules too, and tell your contents insurer. Your licensing agency may also ask whether your landlord has agreed, so keep the written reply in your registration folder.

What qualifications do you need to be a childminder?

Formal degrees are rarely required, but you will almost always need pediatric first aid, safeguarding or child protection training and a background check. Many places also require an introduction to home childcare or the local early years framework, plus food hygiene and safe sleep training. Your licensing agency will give you the exact list for your area.

Do parents pay when their child is sick or on holiday?

That depends on your contract. Many home providers charge full fees when the family is on holiday or the child is ill, because the place is held and costs continue, and charge nothing during the provider's own holidays or sickness. Others offer some free weeks. Choose a clear rule, write it into every contract and apply it to every family.

Your first step today is to contact your licensing agency or local childcare information service and ask for the information pack for new home-based providers. Once you know your exact rules, write a one-page dated plan listing every check, course and document, and start with the background checks, because they take the longest.
