Foods That Lower Cholesterol: What to Eat, Swap and Limit

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If a blood test has just told you your cholesterol is high, you may be staring at the fridge wondering what is still allowed. The short answer: the foods that help most are those rich in soluble fiber (oats, barley, beans, lentils, apples, citrus), plus unsaturated fats such as olive or canola oil used in place of butter, a small handful of nuts, soy foods and oily fish.

You do not have to give up eggs or eat sad salads. Below you will find how cholesterol works in plain words, the four food changes that matter most, a swaps table, how to read a label in ten seconds, a sample day of meals and the warning signs that need urgent care.

What high cholesterol means in plain words

Cholesterol is a waxy fat your body needs to build cells and make some hormones and vitamin D. Your liver makes most of it, and a smaller amount comes from food. It travels in your blood inside little packages called lipoproteins, and those are what your test results describe.

  • LDL is often called bad cholesterol. Too much of it can build up in artery walls over many years.
  • HDL is often called good cholesterol. It helps carry extra cholesterol back to the liver.
  • Triglycerides are another blood fat. They tend to rise with lots of sugar, refined starch and alcohol.

High cholesterol usually causes no symptoms, so a blood test is the only way to know. Targets depend on your overall heart risk (age, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, family history), so ask your doctor what your own numbers mean rather than hunting online for a perfect figure.

Food can help many people, but how much varies a lot. Some people, including those with high cholesterol that runs in families (familial hypercholesterolemia, or hypercholesterolaemia in the UK), need medicine whatever they eat.

Soluble fiber foods that help lower LDL

Soluble fiber forms a soft gel in your gut that helps carry cholesterol away before your body can reuse it. Studies suggest it can help lower LDL. Good sources include:

  • Oats and oat bran
  • Barley
  • Beans, lentils and chickpeas
  • Okra and eggplant (aubergine)
  • Apples, citrus fruit and berries

In a real day that might be oatmeal (porridge) at breakfast, a bean and barley soup at lunch and lentils stirred into dinner. If you are not used to much fiber, add it over a few weeks and drink plenty of fluids. Going from almost none to a lot overnight is a fast route to wind and bloating.

Swap saturated fat for healthier fats

This is the change that gets most people the furthest. Saturated fat, found mostly in fatty and processed meats, butter, ghee, lard, cream, hard cheese, pastries and coconut and palm oil, pushes LDL up. The aim is to swap it for unsaturated fat, not just cut fat and fill up on white bread and sugary snacks.

Instead ofTry
Butter for cookingOlive or canola (rapeseed) oil
Butter on toastA soft vegetable oil spread or mashed avocado
Fatty ground (minced) beefLean ground beef, ground turkey or half lentils
Sausages and baconSkinless chicken, beans or smoked fish
Cream in saucesLower-fat plain yogurt, stirred in off the heat
Lots of hard cheeseA little strong cheese, finely grated
Pastry pie toppingMashed potato or sweet potato with olive oil
Chips and crackersUnsalted nuts, roasted chickpeas or oatcakes
Coconut milk curriesTomato-based curries with chickpeas or lentils

Start with the foods you eat most often. If you butter four slices of toast a day, that is your first swap. If sausages appear twice a week, start there.

Nuts, soy and oily fish for heart health

A small handful of unsalted nuts, about 1 oz (30 g), most days can be part of a heart-healthy pattern. Nuts are calorie dense, so a handful is plenty. Skip them if anyone you cook for has a nut or peanut allergy.

Soy foods such as tofu, edamame and unsweetened soy milk, along with beans and lentils, can stand in for some of the meat in your week. You do not have to give up meat. Swapping a few meals makes room for more fiber and less saturated fat at once.

Aim for at least two portions of fish a week, one of them oily: salmon, mackerel, sardines, trout, herring or pilchards. Oily fish is good for heart health in general and can help lower triglycerides, though it does less for LDL. Canned (tinned) and frozen fish count. If you are pregnant or trying to conceive, follow your country's limits on oily fish and avoid shark, swordfish and marlin.

What about eggs and shellfish?

For most people, saturated fat matters far more than the cholesterol in eggs or shrimp (prawns). Eggs can be part of a balanced diet. If your doctor has given you different advice, follow that.

Foods to limit with high cholesterol

Nothing here is banned forever. These are foods to keep small or occasional:

  • Fatty cuts of meat and processed meats such as sausages, bacon, salami, ham and pies.
  • Butter, ghee, lard, suet and cream used every day.
  • Large amounts of hard cheese.
  • Pastries, cakes and biscuits (cookies) made with butter or palm oil.
  • Coconut oil, which is very high in saturated fat despite its healthy reputation.
  • Anything listing partially hydrogenated oil (industrial trans fat), now banned or limited in many countries but still worth checking on imported or fried snacks.

Salt does not raise cholesterol, but it affects blood pressure, which matters just as much for your heart. Season with herbs, spices, lemon and garlic, and taste before adding salt.

How to read food labels for saturated fat

You only need to check a few lines: saturated fat first, then salt and fiber.

  • UK: front-of-pack traffic lights. Aim for mostly green and amber, and treat reds as occasional.
  • US: percent Daily Value. 5% or less of saturated fat per serving is low and 20% or more is high.
  • Canada, Australia and New Zealand: compare saturated fat per 100 g between similar products and pick the lower one.

Always check the serving size. A low figure on a tiny serving can turn high when you eat a normal portion. Also be wary of low fat claims on the front of the pack, since some of these foods are loaded with sugar and salt, while nuts and oily fish are high in fat and still good choices.

You may see spreads, yogurts or milk drinks fortified with plant sterols or stanols. They can help lower LDL when eaten daily at the amount on the pack, but not everyone needs them, they are not suitable for children or during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and you should talk to your doctor first if you take any medicine.

A sample day of cholesterol-friendly meals

Here is what one ordinary day can look like. None of it feels like diet food.

MealIdea
BreakfastSavory oatmeal (porridge) cooked with spinach and mushrooms, topped with a poached egg
LunchMushroom and barley soup with a slice of whole grain bread
SnackAn apple and a small handful of unsalted nuts
DinnerSheet pan salmon with lemon, asparagus and new potatoes, roasted in olive oil

A busy-night example: soften an onion in olive oil, add lean ground turkey, a can of rinsed black beans, canned tomatoes, cumin and chili, and simmer for 20 minutes. Serve with brown rice. Cook ground poultry to 165°F (74°C) and fish to 145°F (63°C) or until it flakes.

Planning helps. Cook a pot of barley or dried beans at the weekend and use it in soups, salads and bowls all week. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and eat them within 3 to 4 days. The Low Cholesterol Cookbook Made Simple has a 28-day meal plan built on exactly this kind of week.

Food, medicine and the rest of the picture

If your doctor has prescribed a statin or another cholesterol medicine, food does not replace it. Think of medicine and meals as two people pushing the same car. Never stop, skip or change a medicine because your eating has improved without talking to your doctor or pharmacist first. A better result may be partly thanks to the tablets.

Grapefruit and grapefruit juice can interact with some statins and other medicines. Check your leaflet or ask a pharmacist. Some herbal products and supplements can interact too, so ask before buying any so-called natural cholesterol remedy.

Beyond food, not smoking is one of the biggest things you can do. Aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking, built up gradually. Keep alcohol within your country's low-risk guidance and keep blood pressure and diabetes well managed.

Talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before big diet or exercise changes, especially with diabetes, kidney disease, a heart condition, very high triglycerides or pregnancy. Call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand) for chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back, sudden breathlessness, or a drooping face, arm weakness or trouble speaking.

Frequently asked questions

How can I lower my cholesterol quickly?

Food changes take weeks to show in a blood test, and how much they help varies from person to person. The most useful steps are adding soluble fiber every day, swapping butter and fatty meat for olive oil, lean protein and beans, and moving more. If your doctor thinks you need faster or bigger changes, medicine may be recommended. Ask when you should be retested.

What is the best breakfast for high cholesterol?

Oats are a great start because they contain soluble fiber. Try oatmeal (porridge) made savory with spinach and mushrooms, or with berries and a few chopped nuts. Whole grain toast with mashed avocado and white beans works well too. Keep butter, bacon and sausages occasional, and choose skim or low-fat milk or unsweetened soy milk.

Are eggs bad for cholesterol?

For most people, eggs can be part of a balanced diet. Saturated fat in foods like fatty meat, butter and pastries affects blood cholesterol more than the cholesterol in eggs. The bigger issue is often what comes with them, such as bacon, sausages and buttered toast. If your doctor has advised you to limit eggs for your own situation, follow that advice.

Can diet alone lower cholesterol?

For many people, eating more soluble fiber and swapping saturated fat for unsaturated fat helps, but results differ widely. Genes play a big part, and people with inherited high cholesterol usually need medicine whatever they eat. Diet and medicine work well together. Never stop a prescribed medicine without speaking to your doctor or pharmacist first.

Is cheese bad for high cholesterol?

Hard cheese is high in saturated fat, so it is best eaten in small amounts rather than avoided completely. Use a little of a strong cheese, such as aged cheddar, Parmesan-style cheese or feta, finely grated so it goes further. Lower-fat cottage cheese and plain yogurt are useful everyday swaps in sauces and on baked potatoes.

Do I need plant sterol spreads or supplements?

No one has to use them. Foods fortified with plant sterols or stanols can help lower LDL when eaten daily at the stated amount, but they are not suitable for children or during pregnancy or breastfeeding. Supplements, including fiber pills and fish oil capsules, can interact with medicines. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before trying any of them.

Pick one swap today, based on the food you eat most often, and add one soluble fiber food to tomorrow's breakfast, such as oats or an apple. Write down your latest results, take a few questions to your next appointment and build from there, one meal at a time.
