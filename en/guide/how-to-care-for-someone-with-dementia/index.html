How to Care for Someone With Dementia: A Calm, Practical Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If someone you love has dementia, you may be learning a whole new job with no training and very little sleep. To care for someone with dementia, keep your words short and calm, build a steady daily routine, look for a hidden cause such as pain or infection whenever behavior changes, make the home safe, put legal plans in place early and get regular breaks for yourself.

Dementia is an illness, so the changes you see are not anyone's fault, yours or theirs. This guide covers the everyday skills that make the biggest difference, from what to say when they ask the same question for the tenth time to knowing when you need help.

What dementia does and why behavior changes

Dementia is an umbrella term for conditions that cause a gradual decline in memory, thinking, communication and everyday abilities. Common types include Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia and mixed dementia. It is not a normal part of aging.

Doctors often talk about early, middle and late stages, but treat these as a rough guide, not a timetable. Everyone changes at their own pace. There is currently no cure, though some medicines may help some symptoms for some people for a time. That is a conversation for the specialist.

Most behavior changes happen because the brain can no longer make sense of the world, and the person is trying to meet a need they cannot put into words. Behavior is a message. The person you love is still there, even when they cannot show it the way they used to.

How to talk to someone with dementia

Simplify everything. Short sentences with one idea each, one question at a time, and plenty of time to answer.

  • Get their attention first: say their name, make eye contact and sit at their level.
  • Speak a little slower, not louder, unless they have hearing loss.
  • Use names, not pronouns: "Jenny is coming" rather than "She's coming."
  • Offer simple choices: "Tea or coffee?" instead of "What would you like?" Later, hold up two sweaters.
  • Give one step at a time: "Put your arm in here," then wait.
  • Count to ten in your head before repeating yourself, and repeat with the same words.

Your face and tone matter as much as your words. Approach from the front, keep your voice warm and your movements slow. People with dementia pick up tension quickly, even when your words sound calm.

Respond to the feeling, not the facts

Correcting rarely works and often causes embarrassment or anger, so ask yourself whether it really needs correcting. "I want to go home" often means "I feel lost or unsafe." Try naming the feeling ("You sound worried") and offering comfort: a hand, a cup of tea, a familiar photo or a short walk. Avoid "I already told you" and "Don't you remember?"

Build a calm daily routine

Routine reduces confusion because the person does not have to work out what comes next. Build the day around their old habits and keep the order of things the same.

Schedule bathing and appointments for when your loved one is usually at their best, often late morning. Give them real jobs that suit their stage, such as folding towels, sorting photos, watering plants or listening to favorite songs. A small whiteboard showing three or four things happening today can help.

Handling difficult behavior in dementia

Before trying anything else, check for a cause. Many changes ease once the hidden problem is fixed.

  • Pain (teeth, joints, constipation, sore skin, a tight shoe)
  • Infection, especially urine or chest
  • Hunger, thirst or needing the toilet
  • Tiredness, feeling too hot or too cold
  • Too much noise or too many people, or boredom
  • Missing glasses or a hearing aid that is switched off
  • A new medicine or a change in routine

Medicines for behavior should be a last resort, prescribed and reviewed by a doctor, because some carry serious risks for people with dementia. People with Lewy body dementia can react badly to some of them. If aggression puts anyone in danger, step away and get help.

Sudden worsening over hours or days, with new confusion, drowsiness or agitation, can be delirium caused by infection, dehydration, constipation, pain or medicines. Contact the doctor the same day. Call 911 (US and Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand) for a fall with injury, chest pain, breathing problems, stroke signs, a seizure or if the person cannot be woken.

Personal care, meals and sleep

Washing and dressing can feel frightening or embarrassing. Warm the bathroom first, keep the person covered with a towel, explain each step and let them do as much as they can. If they refuse, stop, try later and change the words: "Let's freshen up before your visitor" can work where "You need a bath" does not.

At meals, use plain plates that contrast with the food, serve one or two items at a time and offer finger foods if cutlery becomes hard. Offer drinks regularly through the day, because dehydration is common and can cause confusion. Coughing or choking when eating needs a doctor or speech therapist assessment.

For better nights, get daylight and activity in the day, limit long naps and caffeine, and keep a calm, unhurried evening routine with a night light on the way to the toilet.

Make the home safe and plan for getting lost

  • Remove loose rugs and clutter, add night lights and fit grab rails.
  • Use automatic shut-off devices for the stove if needed.
  • Lock away medicines, cleaning products and sharp tools.
  • Remove or secure firearms, with ammunition stored separately.
  • Set the water heater no hotter than about 120°F (49°C) to prevent scalds.
  • Test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.
  • Use an ID bracelet or card with your contact details.

Keep a recent photo and a written description ready in case your loved one goes missing, and tell trusted neighbors. If they do go missing, search nearby briefly, then call the police or emergency services quickly. Do not wait.

Driving ability will decline over time, and rules differ by country and state. Watch for new dents, getting lost or near misses, and talk to the doctor about a driving assessment.

Legal and money plans to make early

The most important paperwork needs to be done while your loved one can still make and understand decisions. If capacity is lost first, a court or tribunal process may be needed, which is slower and harder.

Look after yourself as a caregiver

Caregiver burnout is common and is not a sign of failure. Watch for exhaustion that sleep does not fix, getting ill more often, snapping at your loved one, feeling numb or trapped, drinking more and pulling away from friends.

  • Take a daily five-minute break that is only for you.
  • Accept specific offers of help and give family clear jobs.
  • Ask about respite: day centers, in-home help and short stays.
  • Ask for a carer's assessment through your local council (UK) or local health and social services elsewhere.
  • Join a carers' support group or call a national dementia helpline.
  • Keep your own doctor and dental appointments.

Grief can start long before a death, as you lose parts of the person you knew. Guilt, resentment and love can all sit together. That is normal.

Talk to the person's doctor, nurse or pharmacist before changing their care, diet or medicines, and see your own doctor if you are struggling. If you feel you might hurt the person or yourself, step away to a safe place and call for help now: 988 in the US and Canada, Samaritans 116 123 in the UK, Lifeline 13 11 14 in Australia, or call or text 1737 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

What should you not say to someone with dementia?

Avoid phrases that point out memory loss, such as "Don't you remember?" or "I already told you." Avoid arguing about facts, quizzing them, talking about them as if they are not there, or repeatedly telling them that someone has died. Instead, answer again as if for the first time, respond to the feeling behind their words and gently move on to something else.

What is sundowning and how can I help?

Sundowning is restlessness, confusion or agitation that tends to get worse in the late afternoon and evening. Helpful steps include daylight and activity earlier in the day, a quiet afternoon, putting the lights on before it gets dark, a light snack and calm music. Keeping a short diary of when it happens often reveals triggers such as tiredness or hunger.

How do I get someone with dementia to bathe?

Make it calm and comfortable. Warm the room, have everything ready, keep them covered with a towel and explain one step at a time. Offer choices, such as bath or shower, now or after breakfast. If they refuse, do not force it. Try again later, try different words, or ask whether a paid carer of the same sex would be more acceptable to them.

Can someone with dementia live alone?

In the early stage, some people can, with support, routines and safety measures such as stove shut-off devices and regular check-ins. As dementia progresses, living alone usually becomes unsafe. Warning signs include missed medicines, not eating, getting lost, scams, falls or leaving appliances on. A needs assessment through local health or social services can help you decide what support is required.

When is it time for a care home?

It may be time when the person's needs are greater than what can safely be provided at home, even with help. Common signs are frequent falls, wandering at night, needing two people for personal care, or a caregiver whose own health is breaking down. Moving someone into a care home (nursing home) is not giving up. It can mean better care and a better relationship.

How do you calm someone with dementia who is agitated?

Stay calm yourself, lower your voice and give them space. Check quickly for pain, thirst, a toilet need, noise or tiredness. Acknowledge the feeling ("You seem upset, I'm here") rather than arguing, then gently redirect to a drink, a walk, a familiar song or a simple task. If anyone is at risk of harm, step away and get help.

Start with one change today: before you answer the next confusing question or difficult moment, pause, check for a simple cause like pain, hunger or the toilet, and respond to the feeling first.
