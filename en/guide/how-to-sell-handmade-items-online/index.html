How to Sell Handmade Items Online: Pricing, Photos and First Sales

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your shelves are filling up with candles, earrings or knitted hats and friends keep saying you should sell them, you are probably ready to try. To sell handmade items online, check that buyers want your product, price it with a formula that covers materials, your time, overheads and profit, choose one place to sell, take clear phone photos in daylight, write listings using the words buyers search for, and follow the safety and tax rules for your product.

None of this needs a big budget. It does need honest numbers and some patience, because new shops are usually quiet at first. This guide walks you through each step with worked pricing examples that add up, so you can open a shop that pays you rather than one that keeps you busy.

Check that people will buy your product

Friends and family are kind, which makes them a poor guide to the market. Before you spend money on stock or branding, spend an hour looking at what real buyers already do.

  1. Search for your product on handmade marketplaces, social media shops and general search engines, exactly as a buyer would.
  2. For the first 20 similar listings, note the price, the style and one thing buyers praise or complain about in reviews.
  3. Look for gaps. Repeated complaints, such as candles that tunnel or hats that run small, are openings you can fill.
  4. Describe your ideal customer in a few lines: who they are, what they care about and who they buy for.

Then test cheaply with a small batch before you scale up. One more check matters: can you make the item again, to the same standard, in a reasonable time? A one-off piece that took a week is lovely, but it is hard to build a shop around.

How to price handmade items for profit

Underpricing is the most common reason handmade shops struggle. Use this formula for every product:

  • Materials + labor + overheads + profit = wholesale price
  • Retail price = wholesale price x 2, as a common rule of thumb

Materials include small things like jump rings, labels and tissue paper. Labor is the time to make one item, from setup to cleanup, at an hourly rate you would accept from an employer. Overheads are monthly running costs (fees, tools, packaging bought in bulk) divided by the items you expect to sell, so $90 a month across 60 items is tabindex="-1".50 each. Profit, often 10% to 30% of costs, belongs to the business and keeps it going through slow months.

Quietly cutting your hourly rate to almost nothing is not one of them.

Include fees and shipping in your numbers

Online sales carry extra costs: marketplace fees, payment processing fees and postage. Fees vary by platform and change over time, so check the current fee page wherever you sell. Keep your retail price the same everywhere you sell. If a boutique later wants to stock your candles at tabindex="-1"5 wholesale, it cannot compete with your own site selling them for $20.

Where to sell handmade items online

There are four main places makers sell, and each trades money for time or control for convenience.

Start with one channel and learn it properly. Most beginners do best on a handmade marketplace, where buyers are already searching. Add a second channel once the first runs smoothly and you have spare time, not because a new option looks exciting.

Take product photos with just your phone

Your phone is enough. Light does most of the work. Set up beside a window that does not get direct sun, turn off overhead lights to avoid odd color casts, and tape baking paper (parchment paper in the US) over the glass if the sun shines straight in. Prop a piece of white card on the shadow side as a reflector.

For the main photo, use a plain white, light gray or soft neutral background. A sheet of poster board curved from the table up the wall hides the corner line. Then shoot a set for every listing:

  • Hero shot of the whole item, filling most of the frame. It becomes your thumbnail, so check it reads clearly when tiny.
  • Detail shot of texture, stitching or finish.
  • Back, side, clasp or base, the parts buyers worry about.
  • Scale shot beside a hand or an everyday object, which prevents disappointed buyers.
  • Lifestyle shot of the item in use or in a simple home setting.
  • Color or size variations side by side, in the same light.

Keep backgrounds, light and angles consistent across your shop, so your thumbnails look like a collection rather than a jumble.

Write listings that buyers can find

Buyers search using plain, practical words: what the item is, what it is made of, the style and who it is for. Use the marketplace search bar's suggestions and the wording in similar listings to find your keywords, then use them naturally.

A title pattern that works for most crafts is main keyword, then material or key feature, then style or use, then gift angle.

Avoid stuffing titles with repeated keywords, which looks spammy and may break marketplace rules. Fill every tag slot with different phrases buyers might type. In the description, lead with the benefit in one or two sentences, then give the facts: size, materials, care, scent or color notes, processing time and shipping. Answer every question a buyer would ask if they held the item. No wording can guarantee search rankings, but clear, accurate listings give you the best chance.

Legal and safety basics for selling crafts

In the US, many makers start as sole proprietors, with a local business license if their area requires one, and a doing business as name if trading under a different name. Marketplaces in many states collect sales tax for you under marketplace facilitator laws, but you may be responsible on your own website or at fairs. In the UK, most makers start as sole traders and register as self-employed with HMRC once trading income goes over the trading allowance. VAT applies only above a threshold that changes from time to time. Keep records from day one and set money aside for tax.

Some product types have specific rules:

  • Cosmetics and soap: labeling rules in the US, and in the UK a Cosmetic Product Safety Report and a UK Responsible Person before sale. Claims such as soothing for eczema can change how a product is regulated.
  • Candles: fire-safety warning labels, and possible chemical hazard labels for scented products.
  • Children's products and toys: among the strictest rules, including testing and certificates in the US and UKCA or CE marking in the UK.
  • Jewelry: limits on nickel, lead and cadmium, and accurate metal descriptions.

Never copy other makers' designs, characters or brand logos, and consider product liability insurance.

If your product goes on skin, near a flame or into a child's hands, check the current rules for your product and country before you sell. This is general information, not legal or tax advice, and rules change, so pull a listing rather than guess.

Shipping, customer service and honest reviews

Weigh each product in its packaging and check real postage costs before you set shipping prices. Protect fragile items with snug inner padding so nothing moves when you shake the box, and send a test parcel to a friend before your first sale. For international orders, fill in customs forms accurately and tell buyers they may pay import charges.

Set a message response time you can actually keep, such as within one working day. When something goes wrong, reply calmly, apologize for the trouble, and offer a clear fix such as a replacement or refund. Write a returns policy that suits your products and follows the law. In the UK, buyers of online goods generally have a 14-day cancellation period after delivery, with exceptions such as personalized items. In the US, marketplaces may set their own rules. Check the current rules for your country.

Ask happy buyers for an honest review with a friendly note in the package or a short follow-up message. Never write fake reviews, swap reviews or pay for them. Respond to a negative review politely and briefly, because future buyers read your reply as much as the complaint.

A 90-day plan from hobby to open shop

  • Weeks 1 to 3: choose a small starting range, describe your customer, price every product with the formula, and check registration, tax and safety rules.
  • Weeks 4 to 7: photograph your products, set up the shop name, about page and policies, write your first ten listings and set up a packing station.
  • Weeks 8 to 10: open with a soft launch to friends and ask for feedback, start posting on one social platform, begin an email sign-up list and prepare customer message templates.
  • Weeks 11 and 12: open a separate bank account, track income and costs in a simple spreadsheet, review what sold and plan the next season.

Do not judge the shop by its first few weeks. Buyers, search results and followers take time to catch up with you. The book Sell Your Handmade Products Online includes a pricing worksheet, listing template and this plan in weekly detail.

Frequently asked questions

How do I price my handmade items?

Add materials, labor at a fair hourly rate and a share of overheads to get your cost, then add a profit margin, often 10% to 30%, to reach a wholesale price. A common rule of thumb sets retail at about twice wholesale. Finally, check that the price still leaves profit after marketplace fees, payment processing and shipping.

What handmade items sell best online?

Items that photograph well, ship easily and suit gifting tend to do well, such as jewelry, candles, prints, knitwear and small homeware. What matters more than the category is a clear niche, a product buyers are already searching for, consistent quality and fair pricing. A one-hour market check of similar listings will show you more than any general list.

Do I need a business license to sell crafts online?

It depends on where you live. In the US, rules vary by state, county and city, and some areas require a local license or seller's permit even for small home businesses. In the UK, most makers register as self-employed once income passes the trading allowance. Check your local government and tax office websites for the current rules.

Is it better to sell on a marketplace or my own website?

A handmade marketplace is usually easier for beginners because buyers are already searching there, though you pay fees and compete with many sellers. Your own website gives more control and lower per-sale fees, but you must bring every visitor yourself. Many makers start on a marketplace, then add their own site once sales and an email list are growing.

How do I get my first sale on a new shop?

Start with ten or so complete listings with clear photos, accurate keyword-rich titles and full descriptions. Tell friends, family and past craft fair customers that the shop is open, share your making process on one social platform, and invite people to an email list. Keep listing new items steadily. Early sales often come from people who already know you.

Your first step today is to take your single most important product, time yourself making a small batch, list every material cost and run it through the pricing formula, including fees and shipping. Compare the result with what you charge now. That one calculation tells you whether your shop is ready to pay you, and it makes every other step easier.
