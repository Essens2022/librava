How to Write a Novel: A Step-by-Step Guide From Idea to First Draft

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have carried a story around in your head for years, and every time you sit down to write it, a small voice asks who you think you are. To write a novel, you turn your idea into a one-sentence summary, get to know what your main character wants, sketch a simple three-act plan, then write scenes in short regular sessions and let the first draft be messy until it is finished.

This guide walks through each step in plain words, with small invented examples, word count numbers and a 90-day plan. No special talent or spare afternoons required.

How much time it takes to write a novel

Novel length varies by genre and publisher, but many adult novels fall roughly between 70,000 and 100,000 words. First drafts are often shorter or longer, and that is fine, because revision adds and cuts.

Those numbers look huge until you break them down. Most people write 250 to 1,000 words in a focused hour.

RoutineWords per weekWords in a year
300 words, 3 days a week900about 47,000
500 words, 4 days a week2,000about 104,000
750 words, weekends only1,500about 78,000

You do not have to write every day. What helps most is a regular rhythm, so your story stays warm in your mind. Look at an ordinary week and circle every gap of 15 to 60 minutes: before the house wakes up, a lunch break, after the children are in bed. Then write three of those gaps into your calendar as fixed appointments.

Fifteen minutes with a timer is often enough for 200 or 300 words. Short sessions add up faster than most people expect.

How to find a story idea worth writing

Do not worry that your idea has been done before. Nearly every story shape has: two people falling in love, a detective solving a murder, a family keeping a secret. What has not been done is your version, with your characters and your way of seeing things.

Good ideas often start with a "what if" question. What if a woman inherits a failing bakery and the only buyer wants to knock it down? What if the anonymous letters in a seaside town start mentioning your own secret?

Choose a genre you love to read, because you will spend months inside this book. Then test the idea for conflict: is there something your character badly wants and something standing in the way?

Finish this step with a one-sentence summary naming who the story is about, what they want and what stands in their way. It does not need to be pretty. It is a compass for the hard weeks ahead.

Create characters readers care about

Readers follow people, not events. Before you plot, get clear on your main character:

  • What they want. A concrete goal the reader can track, like keeping the bakery or clearing their son's name.
  • What they need. Often something deeper they do not see yet, like learning to trust other people.
  • A flaw. A real weakness that causes trouble, so the ending has to be earned.
  • Backstory in small doses. Let the past slip out in a line here and there rather than in long early chapters.

Give supporting characters their own wants, too, so they do more than stand around helping. An antagonist does not have to be a villain; anyone whose goals clash with your hero's will do, and the best ones believe they are right.

A simple worksheet helps: name, age, what they want, what they fear, a flaw, one secret, how they talk. Fill it in for your main character and antagonist, and a shorter version for two or three others.

How to plot a novel in three acts

A plot is not a pile of interesting events. It is a chain where each event pushes the next. Test it by joining your events with "but" or "so" instead of "and then". "She inherits a bakery, and then meets the neighbor, and then there is a storm" sits still. "She inherits a failing bakery, so she must sell it, but the only buyer wants to knock it down" pulls the reader forward.

Most stories fall naturally into three parts:

  • Act one, the beginning (often about a quarter): normal life, then something disrupts it.
  • Act two, the middle (often about half): the character tries, fails and makes things worse while the stakes rise.
  • Act three, the end: everything comes to a head, a final choice, then a short settling.
Turning pointRough positionWhat it does
Inciting incidentEarly in act oneDisrupts normal life
First big choiceEnd of act oneCommits the hero to the story
MidpointMiddle of act twoChanges the game or the goal
Lowest momentEnd of act twoAll seems lost
ClimaxLate in act threeThe final test

You do not need all five before starting. Your inciting incident and a rough ending are enough. Some writers outline in detail; others discover the story as they write. For a first novel, a middle path works well: know your beginning, ending and a few turning points, and discover the scenes between them. Then make a scene list, one line per scene with whose point of view it is in, what happens and what changes.

How to write scenes that move the story

Every working scene needs three things: a goal (what the character wants right now), conflict (what gets in the way) and change (what is different by the end). When a scene feels flat, one of the three is usually missing.

Enter late and leave early. Skip the car ride, the parking and the hellos, and start when the conversation turns tense. End on a decision, a discovery or a fresh question instead of the character going to bed.

Show the moments that matter emotionally. Here is an invented telling version:

Mark was very angry with his son for lying again.

And a showing version:

Mark smoothed the report card flat on the table. "Your mother signed this?" The boy said nothing. Mark folded the card in half, then in half again, and put it in his pocket.

Nobody says "angry", yet the reader feels it. Telling still has a place: use it to move quickly through time ("The winter passed without a word from him") or share plain information. The more a moment matters to your character, the more you should slow down and show it.

For dialogue, start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes. US style usually uses double quotation marks and UK style often single; pick one and stay consistent.

Why your first draft is allowed to be messy

A first draft has one job: to exist. You are not competing with the polished books on your shelf, which went through many rewrites and several editors.

Three habits keep you moving:

  1. Use bracket notes. When you do not know a name or fact, write [name?] or [check] and carry on. Stopping to research is one of the fastest ways to lose an evening.
  2. End each session with a one-line note for next time, such as "Next: Tom finds the letter and decides not to tell Grace." You will never face a blank page.
  3. When you miss a session, do not double the next one. Just come back at your next planned time.

Hitting the middle and feeling lost is close to universal. It is the longest part of most novels, often half the book. Try a "ten next things" list: write the last thing that happened, then list ten possible next events without judging them. The useful idea often shows up around number seven or eight. Raise the stakes one step at a time, by making the problem personal, adding a deadline or putting someone your hero loves at risk. You can also write a scene you can see clearly out of order and fill the gaps later.

When a shiny new idea tempts you, give it a page in a "later books" file and return to your draft.

A 90-day plan to finish your first draft

A plan you can see every day is what turns "someday" into a draft. Here is a 13-week shape:

WeeksFocusGoal
1Idea and writing timeCalendar set, one-sentence summary
2Characters and settingWorksheets for main characters, key places
3PlotThree acts, turning points, scene list
4 to 5Act oneAbout 5,000 then 6,000 words
6 to 10The middle and midpointAbout 6,000 words a week
11 to 12Toward and through the climaxAbout 6,000 words a week
13Ending and gapsFinal scenes, back up, type The End

That pace gives a first draft of roughly 55,000 to 60,000 words, a strong start that revision often lengthens. The weekly goals are totals, so three sessions of 2,000 words count the same as six of 1,000. If 6,000 a week is too much, halve it and double the drafting weeks. If you fall behind, move the dates rather than doubling your effort. The book Write Your First Novel walks through this plan week by week, with worksheets for characters, plot and scenes.

How to revise and what comes after

When you finish, back the draft up in two places and let it rest for two to four weeks. Then read it all in a few days without fixing anything, noting what drags and what you love.

Revise from large to small. First the structure: is the central question clear, does the middle keep raising stakes, does the ending pay off what was set up? Then scenes, checking goal, conflict and change. Sentences come last, read aloud to catch clumsy rhythm and filler words like "just", "really" and "suddenly". Two to five beta readers who enjoy your genre can tell you where they were bored or confused; look for patterns rather than single opinions.

Your options afterward, in general terms: traditional publishing usually goes through a literary agent, approached with a query letter and sample pages, while self-publishing means uploading to online retailers and using print on demand, handling editing and covers yourself. Your writing is protected by copyright as soon as you create it in the US and UK.

Reputable literary agents and traditional publishers do not charge reading fees. Be cautious of any company that praises your book quickly and asks for a large up-front fee to publish it. Paying for services you choose, like an independent editor, is normal in self-publishing; paying just to be accepted is a warning sign.

Frequently asked questions

How many words should a first novel be?

Many adult novels land roughly between 70,000 and 100,000 words, though it varies by genre and publisher. A first draft of 55,000 to 60,000 words is a strong start, since revision often adds scenes and depth. Check typical lengths for your genre, and do not pad: deepen characters and subplots instead.

Should I outline my novel or just start writing?

Both approaches produce finished books. Outlining helps you avoid getting stuck and suits people with little time, while discovering the story keeps it fresh but risks wandering. For a first novel, a light middle path works well: know your beginning, a rough ending and a few turning points, then discover the scenes between them.

Do I need to write every day to finish a novel?

No. Plenty of writers work three or four days a week, or only on weekends. What matters is a regular rhythm you can keep. Even 500 words four days a week adds up to around 104,000 words in a year. When you miss a session, simply return at your next planned time rather than trying to catch up.

What do I do when I get stuck in the middle?

Find the cause. You may not know what your character wants in the scene, things may be too comfortable for them, an earlier scene may have taken a wrong turn, or you may simply be tired. Try listing ten things that could happen next, raise the stakes a notch, or write a later scene you can clearly see.

Can I write a novel with no experience?

Yes. There is no exam or license; a writer is someone who writes. Skill grows from writing a lot of words and then improving them in revision. Start with a one-sentence summary, a character who wants something and a short scene list, and give yourself permission to write a messy first draft.

Do I have to pay to get my novel published?

Not with traditional publishing. Reputable agents and publishers do not charge reading fees; they earn money when your book sells. In self-publishing you may choose to pay for an editor or cover designer, which is normal. Be wary of companies asking for large up-front fees simply to publish you, especially if they pressure you to decide fast.

Your first step today: look at your week, choose three writing sessions of 15 minutes or more, put them in your calendar, and use the first one to write your story in one sentence. Once that sentence exists, everything else in this guide has something to build on.
