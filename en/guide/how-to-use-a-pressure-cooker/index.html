How to Use a Pressure Cooker: A Calm Beginner's Guide With Times

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your new multi-cooker is still in its box because the hissing valve makes you nervous, you are in good company. To use an electric pressure cooker, you add at least 1 cup (240 ml) of thin liquid, lock the lid, turn the valve to sealing, choose pressure cook on high for the recipe's time, then let the pressure out by natural or quick release before opening the lid.

Electric models control their own heat and will not let the lid open while there is pressure inside, so most of the old stovetop horror stories do not apply. This guide walks you through the parts, a first test run, the liquid and fill rules, release methods, the burn warning and a time chart for everyday foods.

The parts of an electric pressure cooker

Only six parts matter day to day. Find each one on your own cooker with the manual open, since lids and valves differ a little between models.

  • The lid twists and locks onto the base.
  • The sealing ring is a silicone gasket inside the lid. If it is missing, damaged or seated crookedly, the pot will never come to pressure.
  • The steam release valve on top has two positions: sealing, which traps steam so pressure builds, and venting, which lets it out.
  • The float valve is a small metal pin that pops up under pressure and drops when the pressure is gone. While it is up, the lid stays locked.
  • The inner pot is the removable pot you cook in. Never put food or liquid straight into the base.
  • The trivet is the little metal rack that lifts food out of the liquid for steaming or holds a dish above the water.

How to use a pressure cooker for the first time

Before you cook real food, do a water test. It checks that everything works and lets you hear what a normal cook sounds like, which takes most of the fear away.

  1. Press the sealing ring firmly into its groove all the way round.
  2. Pour 3 cups (720 ml) of cold water into the inner pot and set it in the base.
  3. Lock the lid and turn the steam release valve to sealing.
  4. Select pressure cook (sometimes called manual) on high for 5 minutes.
  5. Wait. After some steam and hissing, the float valve pops up and the countdown starts.
  6. When the timer ends, turn the valve to venting with a long spoon, keeping your hand and face away from the opening.
  7. When the float valve drops, open the lid by tilting it away from you.

Notice how long it took to come to pressure, usually 10 to 15 minutes. That warm-up is not included in recipe pressure times, so always add it when planning dinner.

If nothing has happened after 20 minutes, check the valve first. The most common beginner slip is leaving it on venting, so the pot steams forever and never seals. If the valve is right, check that the sealing ring is seated properly.

How much liquid to put in a pressure cooker

A pressure cooker works by boiling liquid in a sealed pot. The steam builds pressure, which raises the boiling point and cooks food faster. No steam means no pressure.

Most 6 quart (about 6 liter) cookers need at least 1 cup (240 ml) of thin liquid, such as water, broth (stock), wine, juice or the juices from canned (tinned) tomatoes. Some models ask for more, so check your manual.

Thick ingredients do not count toward that cup. Tomato paste (tomato purée), crushed tomatoes, cream, cheese, barbecue sauce, honey and flour or cornstarch (cornflour) slurries are too thick to make steam reliably, and they tend to sink and scorch.

Do not add extra liquid just in case. Very little evaporates under pressure, and meat and vegetables release their own juices, so a stew that looks dry going in usually comes out with plenty of sauce. Too much liquid gives you a watery result.

The fill lines

  • Never fill above the 2/3 line.
  • For beans, grains, oats, pasta and anything that foams, stay at or below the 1/2 line.

Natural release vs quick release

When the timer ends, you have two ways to let the pressure out. Recipes tell you which to use, and choosing the right one matters as much as the cooking time.

MethodWhat you doUse it for
Natural releaseNothing. Pressure drops on its own over 10 to 30 minutesMeat, beans, grains, oats, pasta, soups
Timed natural releaseWait the stated minutes, then vent the restRice, chicken, most stews
Quick releaseTurn the valve to venting right awayVegetables, eggs, seafood

Natural release is gentle. Meat rests and stays juicy, and starchy foods do not spray foam out of the valve. Food keeps cooking while the pressure falls, and recipe times already allow for that.

Quick release stops the cooking fast, which protects delicate food from going mushy. Use a long spoon or the release button, keep your face and hands clear and do not vent under a low kitchen cabinet. If foam spits out, turn the valve back to sealing, wait a few minutes and vent in short bursts.

A beginner mistake to avoid: quick releasing a pot of beans or oats. Starchy foam sprays out of the valve and coats the lid, the counter and sometimes you.

How to avoid the burn warning

Sooner or later you will see a burn message (it might read burn, food burn or a short code, depending on your model). It means the bottom of the pot is too hot, usually because something is stuck there or there is not enough thin liquid. The cooker stops heating to protect your food.

Five habits prevent nearly all of them:

  1. Deglaze after sautéing. Pour in a little of the recipe's liquid and scrape every browned bit off the bottom until it is smooth.
  2. Put the thin liquid in first, on the bottom.
  3. Layer tomato sauce, crushed tomatoes and tomato paste on top, and do not stir them in.
  4. Add cream, milk, cheese and thickeners after pressure cooking, using the sauté setting.
  5. Save sugary glazes, such as barbecue or teriyaki, mostly for the end.

If the warning appears, cancel and quick release. If food is stuck but not burned, scrape it up, add 1/4 to 1/2 cup (60 to 120 ml) of thin liquid and start again. If it has truly scorched, move the good food to another pot without scraping the bottom, wash the inner pot and carry on.

Pressure cooker safety tips

Electric pressure cookers have several built-in safety features, but a few habits keep every cook uneventful.

  • Check that the sealing ring is clean and seated before every cook.
  • Make sure both valves are free of food bits.
  • Stay within the 2/3 fill line, or 1/2 for foamy foods.
  • Use at least the minimum thin liquid your manual asks for.
  • Never force the lid open. Wait until the float valve has dropped.
  • Open the lid tilted away from you, and keep children and pets back during quick release.
  • Check meat with a thermometer: poultry 165°F (74°C), ground meat (mince) 160°F (71°C), and pork, beef steaks and roasts and fish 145°F (63°C). If something is underdone, lock the lid and cook a few more minutes under pressure.
Two firm rules. Do not use an electric pressure cooker for home canning, even if it has a canning button, because most models cannot hold a reliable enough pressure or temperature for low-acid foods. And never slow cook dried red kidney beans from raw: they contain a natural toxin destroyed only by thorough boiling, so soak them and pressure cook them fully.

If you live above 3,000 feet (about 900 meters), you will usually need about 5 percent more time for every 1,000 feet (300 meters) above 2,000 feet, or follow your manual.

How to clean a pressure cooker and sealing ring

Wash the inner pot, lid and sealing ring after every use, and check your manual for which parts can go in the dishwasher. Wipe the base with a damp cloth and never put it in water. Every so often, remove and rinse the small shield under the lid that covers the steam valve.

The silicone ring absorbs smells, so a dessert made after a curry may taste faintly of garlic. Soak the ring in equal parts white vinegar and water for 15 minutes, rinse and let it air dry. Many cooks keep two rings, one for savory dishes and one for sweet.

Replace the ring every 12 to 18 months, or sooner if it cracks, stretches or no longer seals well. A tired ring is the usual reason an older cooker suddenly struggles to come to pressure.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to use an electric pressure cooker?

Yes, when you follow the manual. Electric models regulate their own heat and pressure, and the lid locks while the float valve is up, so it cannot be opened under pressure. Keep the ring and valves clean, stay under the fill lines, use enough thin liquid and never force the lid. Keep your face and hands clear of the valve during quick release.

Do you put water in a pressure cooker with meat?

Yes. Even meat needs at least 1 cup (240 ml) of thin liquid, such as water, broth (stock) or wine, so the pot can make steam and build pressure. You do not need to cover the meat. It releases its own juices, and very little liquid evaporates, so adding much more than the recipe asks usually gives a watery sauce rather than a better result.

How long does it take a pressure cooker to build pressure?

Usually 10 to 15 minutes, sometimes longer with a very full pot or cold ingredients straight from the fridge. The countdown only starts once the float valve pops up. Recipe pressure times do not include this warm-up or the release time, so a recipe with a 10 minute pressure time often takes 30 to 40 minutes in total.

Why is my pressure cooker not sealing?

Check the valve first: if it is set to venting, steam escapes and the pot never seals. Next, check that the sealing ring is pressed evenly into its groove and is not cracked or stretched. Food on the rim or in the float valve can also cause leaks. Make sure there is at least 1 cup (240 ml) of thin liquid in the pot.

Can you open a pressure cooker while it is cooking?

Not while it is under pressure, and you should never try to force it. To check food, cancel the program, release the pressure by quick release (or natural release for foamy foods) and wait for the float valve to drop. Then open the lid tilted away from you. If the food needs more time, lock the lid again and cook a few more minutes.

What is the difference between pressure cooking and slow cooking?

Pressure cooking seals the pot and cooks above the normal boiling point, so a beef stew that needs about 3 hours on the stove is tender in roughly 35 minutes under pressure. The slow cook setting heats gently with the valve on venting, over several hours. It often runs cooler than a countertop slow cooker, so you may need longer times or the high setting.

Your first step today is simple: take the cooker out, find the six parts, and run the 5 minute water test. Once you have heard it seal, hiss and drop its float valve, you know what normal looks like, and a weeknight stew or a pot of rice becomes an easy next step.
