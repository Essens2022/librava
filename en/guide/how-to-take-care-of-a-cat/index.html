How to Take Care of a Cat: A Friendly Guide for New Owners

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Bringing a cat home is exciting, and it is normal to feel a bit unsure about what they actually need. To take care of a cat, give them a complete and balanced cat food in measured meals, fresh water in several places, one litter box per cat plus one that you scoop every day, two short play sessions a day, a scratching post, a safe home free of toxic plants and a vet check-up at least once a year.

Get those basics right and most cats settle in happily. This guide explains each one in plain steps, plus the first week at home, the foods and plants to keep away, and the warning signs that mean you should call your vet straight away. It is general information from experienced cat keepers, not veterinary advice.

What a cat needs before day one

Buy the basics before your cat arrives, so their first days are calm rather than a rush to the store.

  • The same food the shelter, rescue or breeder used, for the first week or two
  • Two litter boxes (litter trays) and unscented litter
  • Wide, shallow food and water bowls
  • A sturdy cat carrier
  • A tall scratching post that does not wobble
  • A bed and a hiding spot, even a cardboard box on its side with a towel inside
  • A wand toy and a few small toys
  • A soft brush and cat nail clippers

Then cat-proof your home. Remove lilies completely, tuck away cords, string, hair ties and rubber bands, store medicines and cleaning products in closed cupboards, and check that window screens and balcony doors are secure. Pick a vet and book a first check-up for the first week or two.

Your first week with a new cat

Start your cat in a safe room, a small quiet room with a door, such as a spare bedroom or office. Put the litter box on one side and food and water on the other, because cats prefer not to eat near their toilet. Add a bed, a hiding box, a scratching post, a couple of toys and a worn T-shirt that smells of you.

Block gaps behind appliances and under heavy furniture where a frightened cat could wedge themselves. A cat who can hide feels safer, and a cat who feels safer comes out sooner.

  • Visit for short, quiet spells several times a day and let your cat come to you.
  • Sit on the floor, talk softly and offer a hand to sniff.
  • Keep notes on eating, drinking and litter box use.
  • Open up the rest of the home gradually once your cat is eating well and exploring confidently.
  • Supervise all meetings with young children and teach them to stroke gently, never pick up or chase.

Some cats stroll out within hours. Others hide for several days. Both are normal, as long as your cat is eating, drinking and using the box.

What to feed a cat and how much

Cats are obligate carnivores. Their bodies need nutrients found in animal tissue, such as taurine for the heart and eyes. That is why cats should never be fed a vegan or vegetarian diet, and why dog food is not suitable as a regular meal.

The simplest choice is a commercial cat food labeled complete and balanced for your cat's life stage (in the UK and Europe, look for the word "complete"). Foods marked "complementary" are treats or toppers, not meals.

TypeBenefitsThings to consider
Wet foodHigh moisture, fewer calories per biteSpoils if left out, costs more
Dry foodConvenient, works in puzzle feedersLow moisture, easy to overfeed
MixedVariety plus extra waterPortion both carefully

Most adult cats do well on two or more measured meals a day. Kittens need three or four. Use the pack guide as a starting point only, because neutered indoor cats often need less. Weigh dry food or use a proper measuring cup, since a casual scoop can be double what you meant.

Change foods gradually over 7 to 10 days, mixing more new and less old each day. Keep treats under about 10 percent of daily calories. Cats do not need milk, and many adult cats are lactose intolerant.

Water and a healthy weight

Many cats do not drink much, so make water easy and appealing. Wash bowls and refill them daily, choose wide shallow bowls that do not brush the whiskers, and place several around the home, away from food and well away from the litter box.

Extra weight is common and can contribute to diabetes and arthritis. A cat in good condition has ribs you can feel easily under a thin layer of fat, a gentle waist when seen from above and a slight tuck behind the ribs from the side.

If you think your cat is overweight, ask your vet before cutting back. Weight loss must be slow. A cat should never go without food, because a cat who stops eating can develop a serious liver problem.

How many litter boxes per cat

The general rule used by vets and shelters is one box per cat, plus one. One cat gets two boxes, two cats get three. Spread them out in quiet spots your cat can reach day and night, with a box on each floor of a multi-level home.

  • Size: about one and a half times your cat's length from nose to base of tail. A large shallow storage box works well.
  • Litter: many cats prefer a fine, soft, unscented litter about 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7 cm) deep.
  • Covered boxes: some cats dislike them because they trap smell. Offer an open box beside it and let your cat choose.
  • Cleaning: scoop at least once a day. Empty and wash clumping litter boxes every 2 to 4 weeks with hot water and mild unscented soap.
  • Changes: switch litter slowly over a week or two, mixing new into old.

Daily scooping doubles as a health check. Notice more or larger clumps, tiny clumps, blood, diarrhea or no urine at all.

If your cat starts going outside the box, see the vet first. Bladder problems, constipation, arthritis and kidney disease can all make the box feel painful. Clean accidents with an enzyme cleaner made for pet urine, avoid ammonia-based cleaners and never punish your cat.

Pregnant? Ask someone else to clean the litter box if you can. If you must do it, wear disposable gloves, scoop daily and wash your hands well, because cat feces can carry the parasite that causes toxoplasmosis. Your doctor can tell you more.

Play, scratching and a happy indoor life

Most adult cats need at least two play sessions of 10 to 15 minutes a day. The best play copies a real hunt: stalk, chase, pounce, catch and eat.

  1. Use a wand toy and move it away from your cat, the way real prey would.
  2. Mix quick dashes with still pauses, and let it hide behind furniture.
  3. Let your cat catch it often so they do not lose interest.
  4. Slow the toy down near the end, like a tired mouse, and finish with a final catch.
  5. Give a meal or a few treats from the daily allowance right afterward.

An active session about an hour before bed, followed by the last meal, helps many cats sleep through instead of racing around at 3 a.m. Put string toys away afterward, since swallowed string is dangerous.

Scratching is normal and keeps claws healthy. Give your cat a tall, stable post near where they already scratch and reward them for using it. Declawing is the surgical amputation of the toe bones. It is illegal in the UK, banned in some US states and cities and opposed by many vet and welfare groups, and posts plus regular nail trims make it unnecessary.

Shelves, cat trees, puzzle feeders and a window perch with a view all keep indoor cats busy. Indoor and outdoor lives both have benefits and risks, and a secure outdoor enclosure (a catio) or supervised walks on a leash (lead) can offer a middle ground.

Grooming, teeth and nails

Start with touch, not tools. Gently handle paws, ears and mouth for a second or two, then give a treat, so handling predicts good things.

  • Brush short coats once or twice a week and long coats daily. Never cut out mats with scissors, because cat skin is thin and easy to cut. Ask your vet or a groomer for help.
  • Trim only the clear tip of each claw, well away from the pink quick. One or two claws a day is fine.
  • Ask your vet about dental care and use only toothpaste made for cats.
  • Bathe rarely, and only with cat shampoo.

Leave the carrier out all the time with a soft blanket and the odd treat inside. A cat who naps in the carrier at home is far easier to take to the vet.

Vet visits, vaccines and microchips

Most adult cats benefit from a check-up at least once a year, and seniors (often from around age 11) and cats with health conditions usually need more. Before each visit, write down any changes in appetite, thirst, weight, litter habits or behavior.

  • Vaccines: your vet will recommend which ones your cat needs and how often, based on age and lifestyle.
  • Spaying and neutering: prevents unwanted litters and usually reduces spraying, roaming and fighting. Your vet will advise on timing.
  • Microchipping: a tiny chip under the skin links to your contact details. In England, it has been a legal requirement for pet cats since June 2024. Keep your details up to date.
  • Fleas, ticks and worms: ask your vet for a prevention plan and use only products made for cats.
Important. Never use a dog flea or tick product on a cat. Many contain permethrin, which can cause seizures and death in cats. Never give your cat human medicines either, since common painkillers such as acetaminophen (paracetamol) and ibuprofen are very dangerous to them. This guide is general information, so always ask your vet about your own cat's health.

Toxic foods, plants and emergency signs

Keep these away from your cat at all times:

  • Lilies, every part including pollen and vase water, which can cause fatal kidney failure
  • Onions, garlic, chives and leeks in any form, including powders and gravy
  • Chocolate, caffeine and alcohol
  • Xylitol, a sweetener in some sugar-free gum, candy (sweets) and peanut butter
  • Raw yeast dough, cooked bones, and grapes and raisins (best avoided)
  • Essential oils and diffusers, and antifreeze

Because cats hide illness, small changes matter. Call your vet if your cat eats less, drinks or pees more, loses weight, hides more, stops grooming, vomits more than occasionally or limps.

Go to a vet the same day, or an emergency vet straight away, for:

  • A cat, especially a male, straining in the litter box with little or no urine
  • Trouble breathing or open-mouth breathing
  • Collapse, seizures or sudden weakness in the back legs
  • Suspected poisoning, even if your cat seems fine
  • Not eating for more than a day
  • Repeated vomiting, bleeding that will not stop, or any fall or road accident

Frequently asked questions

Are cats easy to take care of?

Cats are fairly low maintenance compared with dogs, but they still need daily care. Plan on measured meals, fresh water, scooping the litter box at least once a day, two short play sessions and some brushing. Add yearly vet visits and the cost of food and litter. Most cats live 12 to 18 years, often longer, so it is a long commitment.

How often should I feed my cat?

Most adult cats do well on two or more measured meals a day, and kittens usually need three or four. Cats naturally eat small meals, so splitting the daily amount or using a puzzle feeder for part of it suits them. Use the pack guide as a starting point, then ask your vet for a daily target, since neutered indoor cats often need less.

Can I leave my cat alone for a weekend?

It is safer not to. Even with plenty of food and water left out, a cat alone for two or more days can get sick, stuck or stressed, and no one would notice a blocked bladder or a spilled water bowl. Arrange for a trusted cat sitter or friend to visit at least once a day, or book a reputable boarding cattery.

Do indoor cats need vaccines?

Usually yes, though the plan may differ from an outdoor cat. Indoor cats can still be exposed to some diseases through open doors, escapes, visitors or a stay in a boarding cattery, and many catteries ask for proof of vaccination. Your vet will recommend which vaccines your cat needs and how often, based on age, health and lifestyle.

Should I give my cat milk?

No, cats do not need milk. Many adult cats are lactose intolerant, and cow's milk can cause diarrhea. Fresh, clean water is all your cat needs to drink. If you want to give a treat, a few pieces of their regular food or a tiny bit of plain cooked chicken with no seasoning is a better choice, kept within about 10 percent of daily calories.

Why is my cat peeing outside the litter box?

Start with a vet visit, because bladder problems, constipation, arthritis and kidney disease often cause it. A male cat straining with little or no urine is an emergency. If your vet finds nothing wrong, check the setup: enough boxes, big enough, scooped daily, quiet location, unscented litter, and no recent changes. Clean accidents with an enzyme cleaner and never punish your cat.

Start today by saving your vet's number and the nearest emergency vet in your phone, then count your litter boxes and make sure you have one per cat plus one. Those two small steps cover a surprising amount of what keeps a cat safe and settled, and everything else in this guide builds on them.
