How to Start a Tutoring Business: A Clear 9-Step Guide

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you know a subject well and enjoy helping someone finally get it, tutoring can become a flexible business that fits around family or other work. To start a tutoring business, you pick the subjects and age group you can genuinely help, decide whether to teach online, in person or both, register with your tax authority, get the background check expected where you live, write a safeguarding policy and clear terms, set prices from local research, then find your first students through word of mouth and local connections.

The first term is usually slower than people expect, and that is normal. This guide gives you each step in order, with a lesson structure, pricing methods and parent communication habits you can use straight away. It is general information, so check official rules where you live.

Find out if tutoring fits you

Tutoring covers a wide range: homework help for young children, exam and test preparation for teenagers, adult learners returning to study, music and languages. Most tutors teach after school, in the early evening and at weekends, with quieter weeks in the school holidays and busy spells before exams.

Subject knowledge is only the start. The qualities that keep students coming back are:

  • Patience that lasts through the fifth explanation
  • Curiosity about how each student thinks
  • Reliability every single week
  • Warmth with clear boundaries
  • Simple, steady organization
  • Honesty with parents, even when progress is slow

Expect cancellations, anxious parents and a slow first term while word spreads. If you can live with that, the work is deeply satisfying and grows steadily through recommendations.

Choose subjects and students you can help

Start with what you know well and can explain clearly, then check it against real demand. In most places, math (maths), reading and writing and the sciences are in steady demand because they matter for school progress and exams. Languages and music vary a lot by area, and exam preparation is busy but seasonal.

Check demand in practical ways:

  • Read local community noticeboards and parent groups for tutor requests.
  • See which subjects have few tutors on tutoring marketplaces in your area or online.
  • Ask teachers you know what families most often ask for.
  • Ask parents in your community what they have searched for.

Then write a one-sentence description, such as "I help 11 to 14 year olds build confidence in math, online or at my kitchen table." Curricula differ by country, so learn the syllabus and exam formats your students actually follow, and say no politely to subjects or levels outside your strengths.

Decide whether to tutor online or in person

In-person lessons build rapport quickly and suit younger children, but travel eats time. Online lessons let you teach students anywhere and fit more lessons into an evening. Many tutors do both.

OptionStrengthsWatch out for
At your homeNo travel, your resources to handA tidy, open teaching space and parking
At the student's homeConvenient for familiesTravel time and costs, charge for them
OnlineWider reach, efficient timetableReliable internet and a quiet setup
Small groupsLower price per studentStudents of similar level only

For online work, you need a reliable computer, a headset, a camera at eye level, good lighting, a plain background and a digital whiteboard or shared document. Have a backup plan for when the internet fails, such as switching to a phone call with work sent to the parent.

Set up your tutoring business properly

Even a few lessons a week usually count as self-employment, so tell your tax authority and register where required. Many tutors start as a sole trader or sole proprietor. Open a separate bank account so lesson fees and costs never mix with household money.

  • Ask about professional indemnity and public liability insurance, especially if students come to your home.
  • Keep simple records of lessons, income, expenses and mileage.
  • Find out when sales tax, VAT or GST might apply.
  • Follow data protection rules for students' personal details.
  • Get local advice from an accountant or a small business service.

Write short terms and conditions covering lesson length, prices, payment dates, cancellations, holidays, safeguarding, communication and how either side can end lessons. Send them to every family before the first lesson. Set aside one short weekly slot to send invoices, log payments and move money into a tax savings account.

Keep children safe with a safeguarding policy

Safeguarding protects students and protects you from misunderstandings. Background checks differ by country, and some higher-level checks can only be requested through an organization such as an agency.

CountryCommon check in general terms
UKCriminal record checks, including an enhanced check for work with children
AustraliaA working with children check, run by each state or territory
New ZealandPolice vetting or a police check
CanadaA police check or vulnerable sector check
USBackground checks, often requested by families, schools or agencies

Three rules for under-18s. Never teach a child alone in a closed room, never give a child a lift on your own and never agree to keep secrets. Have a parent or carer at home throughout, teach in a shared space and send online lesson links to parents, not children.

Do not message students on private social media, record lessons or take photos without written consent and a clear purpose. If a child is in immediate danger, call emergency services; otherwise contact your local child protection services and write down what was said, using the child's words.

Start strong with a first consultation

A short consultation call with parents builds trust before you teach. Ask what prompted them to look for a tutor, what the student finds hard and easy, upcoming exams or tests, and any support plans at school. Agree two or three specific goals together.

In the first lesson, use a friendly starter assessment rather than a formal test: a few questions at different levels, a short piece of reading or a problem talked through aloud. You are looking for gaps, such as a student struggling with fractions because times tables are shaky.

Skip the myth of fixed learning styles. Instead, notice what helps this student, such as worked examples, diagrams or talking ideas through. Afterward, send the family a short written plan with goals, lesson frequency and how you will report progress.

Plan tutoring lessons that help students remember

A clear rhythm means the student does most of the thinking. This structure works for most subjects and ages:

StageWhat happensRough share of time
Warm-up recallQuick questions on earlier topicsAbout 10 percent
New ideaExplain and model one new conceptAbout 20 percent
Guided practiceStudent tries with your supportAbout 25 percent
Independent practiceStudent works alone while you watchAbout 25 percent
Quick checkA few questions to test understandingAbout 10 percent
Next stepsRecap, set practice, preview next timeAbout 10 percent

Warm-up questions should reach back several weeks, because recalling information strengthens memory far more than rereading it. Teach one idea well rather than three half understood. During independent practice, stay quiet unless the student is completely stuck. Families pay for their child to become independent, not to watch you solve problems.

Plan each term around agreed goals, building in review lessons every few weeks. For exam students, work backwards from the exam date.

How much to charge for tutoring

Rates vary widely by country, subject, level and experience, so research rather than guess. Check tutoring marketplaces, local tutor profiles and agency websites for your subject and level, noting online and in-person rates separately, and compare like with like.

MethodHow it worksGood for
Per lessonSet price for each lessonSimple budgeting for families
Per hourPrice based on timeVaried lesson lengths
Block bookingSet number of lessons paid in advanceCommitment, steady income
Small groupEach student pays a shareGroups of similar level

Count all the time a lesson really takes: planning, travel, notes, messages and invoicing. Experience, specialist exam preparation and travel can reasonably raise your price.

Ask for payment in advance, set a cancellation notice period, often 24 to 48 hours, and apply it to everyone. Avoid deep introductory discounts; a free consultation call is usually enough. Review prices once a year and raise them for new families first.

Find tutoring students and grow steadily

Recommendations are the strongest source of students. Tell friends, neighbors and former colleagues what you teach, using your one-sentence description, and give them a short information sheet to pass on. Add notices to community boards, libraries and local groups, and create a simple website or profile with your subjects, levels, areas and a contact form.

OptionProsCons
Tutoring marketplaceReaches many families, handles bookingsFees and price competition
Tutoring agencyFinds students, may handle checksTakes a share, less control
IndependentFull control, keep all feesYou find every student yourself

Keep parents informed with a short message after lessons and a termly report, and keep a progress file for each student so growth is visible. Happy, informed parents are your best marketing. Frequently asked questions

Do you need qualifications to be a tutor?

In most places private tutoring is not regulated, so formal teaching qualifications are not legally required. Families still expect strong subject knowledge, and many look for teaching experience or relevant study. Agencies and marketplaces may set their own requirements. A suitable background check, a written safeguarding policy and clear terms reassure parents as much as any certificate.

Do I need a background check to tutor children?

Often yes, or families will expect one. The UK uses criminal record checks, Australia uses working with children checks, New Zealand uses police vetting, Canada uses police or vulnerable sector checks and US families often ask for background checks. Some higher-level checks must be requested through an organization such as an agency, so look up the official route where you live.

How much should I charge for tutoring?

There is no single right figure, because rates vary by country, subject, level and experience. Research tutors offering similar lessons locally and online, compare like with like and place yourself within that range. Count planning, travel and admin time as well as teaching time. Choose a method such as per lesson, per hour or block bookings and review prices yearly.

Is online tutoring as good as in-person tutoring?

It can work very well, especially for older students and adults, as long as you have a reliable connection, a clear camera and a digital whiteboard or shared document. Younger children often focus better in person. Many tutors offer both. For under-18s online, get written parental consent, send links to parents and ask that the student sits in a shared family space.

How do I get my first tutoring students?

Most tutors find their first students through people they already know. Tell friends, neighbors and former colleagues what you teach and who you help, put notices on community boards and set up a simple profile. Marketplaces and agencies can help at the start but take a share. Do excellent work and keep parents informed, because recommendations grow from there.

How long should a tutoring session be?

Common lengths are 30 to 45 minutes for young children and around an hour for older students and exam preparation. Shorter sessions suit limited attention spans, while longer ones allow time for guided and independent practice. Choose standard lengths, price each clearly and keep a steady rhythm of recall, new idea, practice, quick check and next steps.

Your first step today is to write your one-sentence description: what you teach, who you help and where. Then look up the official background check route where you live and start your application, because it often takes the longest of anything on your list.
