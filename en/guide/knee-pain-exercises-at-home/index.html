Knee Pain Exercises at Home: A Simple Plan for Stronger Knees

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your knees creak on the stairs, ache after a walk or stiffen up when you sit for a while, you are far from alone. The short answer is that gentle, regular strengthening usually helps more than resting: start with easy moves in a chair or on the bed, such as quad sets and seated knee straightening, then build up to sit to stands, mini squats and step ups, alongside short daily walks.

This guide shows you how to do each exercise, how many to start with, how much discomfort is okay, and how to turn it all into an 8-week plan. It also covers stretches for stiffness, what to do on a bad day and the warning signs that mean you should get help instead.

Is it safe to exercise with knee pain?

For most everyday knee pain, including knee osteoarthritis and pain around the kneecap, yes. Joints are living tissue that respond to movement, and staying active does not wear them out faster. Studies suggest that strengthening exercise combined with general activity such as walking is one of the main, best supported ways to ease pain and help you move better, at any age.

Strong thigh, hip and calf muscles act like a support team. When your thigh muscles (quads) are strong, they soak up some of the load with every step, so less force goes straight through the joint. Your hip muscles keep the knee lined up over your foot on stairs and when you stand up.

Pain also does not always match what an X-ray shows. Plenty of people with changes on an X-ray have little pain, and the reverse is true too.

Check first. This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor (GP), physical therapist (UK: physiotherapist) or pharmacist before starting new exercises or medicines, especially if your symptoms are severe, new or getting worse. If you are recovering from knee surgery or a recent injury, follow your own team's plan first.

How much knee pain is okay during exercise

Some discomfort while you exercise is normal. Your knee is getting used to doing more, and it may grumble a little. A useful guide is the 24-hour rule: mild to moderate discomfort that settles within about 24 hours, and does not leave your knee more swollen, is fine.

If you score pain from 0 (none) to 10 (worst imaginable), staying around 5 or below during exercise is usually reasonable, as long as it calms down by the next day.

Mild ache that settles by tomorrowCarry on

Before each session, warm up for two or three minutes: march on the spot holding a counter, then sit on the edge of a chair and slide each foot back and forward ten times. Breathe normally throughout. Breathing out as you stand up or lift your leg helps you avoid holding your breath.

Gentle knee exercises in a chair or bed

Start here if your knees are very stiff or sore, or if you have not exercised for a while. These moves wake up the muscles without putting much weight through the joint. Begin with about 5 repetitions on each leg, once or twice a day.

  1. Static quads (quad sets). Sit with your legs out and a rolled towel under one knee. Tighten the front of your thigh to press the back of the knee into the towel and pull your toes toward you. Hold 5 seconds, then relax fully.
  2. Heel slides. Lying on the bed, slowly slide one heel toward your bottom as far as is comfortable, pause, then slide it back. A plastic bag under the heel helps it glide.
  3. Short arc quads. Lie back with a firm roll under your knee. Keeping the knee on the roll, lift your foot until the knee is straight. Hold 3 to 5 seconds and lower slowly.
  4. Seated knee extension. Sit well back in a firm chair. Straighten one knee, pull your toes up, hold 3 to 5 seconds, then lower slowly.
  5. Straight leg raise. Lie down with the other knee bent. Tighten your thigh and lift the straight leg to about the height of the bent knee. Hold 2 or 3 seconds and lower slowly.
  6. Ankle pumps. Point your toes away, then pull them back, 10 to 20 times.

This set takes about 10 minutes and works well as a daily warm-up even after you move on. If your knee bends as you lift in the straight leg raise, stay with quad sets for another week or two.

Standing exercises to strengthen your knees

Once the gentle set feels comfortable, add these. They build strength the way you actually use your knees. Do them two or three days a week with a rest day between, and work both legs even if only one knee hurts.

  • Sit to stand. Sit near the front of a sturdy chair with arms, placed against a wall. Lean forward so your nose goes over your toes, push through your feet and stand, using the arms at first. Sit back down slowly with control. Start with 5.
  • Mini squats. Hold the kitchen counter, feet hip width apart. Push your bottom back as if sitting on a high stool, only about a quarter of the way, keeping your knees in line with your second toe. Start with 5.
  • Step ups. Hold the stair rail and use the bottom step only. Step up with one foot, bring the other up, then step down. Start with 5 each leg.
  • Calf raises. Hold the counter, rise onto the balls of your feet and lower slowly. Start with 5.
  • Bridges. Lying on the bed with knees bent, squeeze your buttocks and lift your bottom, hold 2 or 3 seconds, lower slowly.
  • Clamshells. Lie on your side with knees bent and feet together. Lift the top knee without rolling your hips back.
  • Standing hamstring curls. Holding the counter, bend one knee to bring your heel toward your bottom, then lower.

Build up toward 10 to 15 of each. When that feels easy, make it harder in small steps: move more slowly, use the chair arms less, then later add a light ankle weight or exercise band. Always hold something solid for standing moves.

Stretches for stiff knees

Stretching eases stiffness, especially first thing in the morning or after you have been sitting. Move slowly, stop at a mild stretch and hold for 20 to 30 seconds, twice on each leg. No bouncing.

  • Calf stretch. Hands on a wall, one foot back with the leg straight and the heel down. Lean in until you feel a stretch in the back calf.
  • Hamstring stretch. Sit on the edge of a chair with one leg out straight, heel on the floor. Sit tall and lean forward from your hips.
  • Front of thigh stretch. Hold a counter, bend one knee and hold your ankle or trouser (pant) leg behind you. Use a towel around the ankle if you cannot reach.
  • Bending and straightening. Sit on the edge of a chair and slide one foot back under the seat, then forward again, 10 times each leg.

A quick morning routine might be ankle pumps and heel slides in bed, then bending and straightening on the edge of the bed before you stand.

Is walking good for knee pain?

For most people, yes. Walking keeps the joint moving, strengthens the legs and is good for your heart, mood and sleep. Adults are generally advised to aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus strength work on two days, but you build toward that gradually.

Find your baseline first: the walk you can manage on a fair day without paying for it the next day. That might be 5 minutes. Then add 1 to 2 minutes each week. This avoids the boom and bust pattern of doing loads on a good day, then needing three days on the sofa.

  • On stairs, lead with your good leg going up and your sore leg going down.
  • Wear cushioned, supportive shoes with a low heel and good grip.
  • A walking stick (cane) goes in the hand opposite the painful knee, with the top at your wrist crease when your arms hang by your sides.
  • Cycling, swimming and water exercise are knee friendly options on days walking feels too much.

An 8-week knee strengthening plan

Many people notice a difference after 6 to 8 weeks of steady work, though every knee is different. Use this as a guide and repeat any week that feels too much.

What to do when your knee flares up

Flares are common and usually settle within a few days to a couple of weeks. Keep moving gently rather than stopping altogether: do the chair and bed set every hour or so within comfort, shorten walks and cut back your strength routine for a few days.

  • Wrapped heat for 15 to 20 minutes eases stiffness. A wrapped cold pack for about 10 to 15 minutes can calm a hot, puffy knee.
  • Never put heat or cold straight on skin or on numb skin, never use them while asleep, and take extra care with diabetes, poor circulation or thin skin.
  • Ask a pharmacist (chemist) which pain relief suits you. Anti-inflammatory gels are often suggested first for knee osteoarthritis. Follow the label and never exceed the dose.
  • Build back up over a week or two using the 24-hour rule.

Knee pain warning signs that need help

Get help quickly for any of these:

  • A hot, red, very swollen or very painful knee with fever or feeling unwell: urgent care the same day, or the emergency department (A&E) if very unwell.
  • You cannot put weight on the leg after an injury, felt a pop with fast swelling, or the knee looks deformed: urgent care.
  • Calf pain, swelling, warmth or redness in one leg, especially after surgery, travel or bed rest: urgent same day care.
  • Chest pain or breathlessness: call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand).
  • A knee that locks or keeps giving way, pain at night that does not ease with rest, unexplained weight loss, or numbness or weakness in the leg: see a doctor soon.

Also see a doctor or physical therapist if your pain is not improving after a few weeks of exercise and pacing, keeps getting worse or stops you doing daily things.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best exercise for knee pain?

There is no single best exercise, but sit to stand from a sturdy chair comes close because it trains the exact movement you use to get out of chairs, cars and off the toilet. Pair it with seated knee straightening, mini squats at the counter and calf raises. A mix of thigh, hip and calf work, plus regular walking, tends to help most.

How often should I do knee exercises?

The gentle chair and bed exercises can be done daily, even twice a day if your knee likes them. Standing strength exercises work best two or three days a week with a rest day in between. Stretches can be done daily. Short, regular sessions beat one long session that leaves you sore for days.

Should I rest my knee if it hurts?

Long spells of rest usually leave knees stiffer and weaker. During a flare, ease back rather than stopping: shorter walks, fewer repetitions and gentle movement every hour or so. Pain that settles within about 24 hours is generally fine. Rest and get checked if you have sharp pain, the knee gives way or swelling appears suddenly.

How long does it take for knee exercises to work?

Many people notice a difference after about 6 to 8 weeks of steady exercise, though some feel better sooner and some take longer. Every knee is different. Keep a simple diary of what you do and how your knee feels the next morning, and give the plan a fair try before deciding whether it helps.

Is it okay if my knee clicks when I exercise?

Clicking, cracking or creaking during exercise is very common and usually nothing to worry about. Noisy knees are often perfectly healthy knees. What matters more is how the knee feels and whether any discomfort settles within about 24 hours. Get advice if clicking comes with sharp pain, locking, giving way or swelling.

Do I need a knee brace or special insoles?

Most people do not. Comfortable, cushioned shoes with a low heel, a firm back and good grip are usually enough. Knee supports can help some people feel steadier, but it is best to discuss one with a physical therapist (physiotherapist) first. A podiatrist can advise if you think you need insoles.

Your first step today is simple: sit in a firm chair, do 5 slow seated knee straightenings on each leg and 5 quad sets with a rolled towel, then note how your knee feels tomorrow morning. Small, steady steps like this add up to stronger, more confident knees over the coming weeks.
