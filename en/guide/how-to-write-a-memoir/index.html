How to Write a Memoir: A Gentle Step-by-Step Guide for Beginners

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have stories your family has heard a dozen times and many they have never heard at all, and you would like to get them down before they fade. To write a memoir, you gather memories first, choose a simple shape for the book, then write one specific moment at a time as short scenes, revising only once a full draft exists.

This guide takes you through each step in plain terms, from a blank page to a finished book your family can hold, with prompts, examples and a 12-week plan you can start this week.

Decide who your memoir is for

Before you write a word, picture your reader. A book for your grandchildren can explain things they would never know, like what a school day looked like in 1960 or how much your first wage bought. A memoir for strangers needs a tighter focus and a clear story.

Most life story books fall into one of these groups:

  • Family keepsake. Stories, photos and recipes for relatives, often 30 to 150 pages.
  • Full memoir. One life or one period told as a story, often 50,000 to 80,000 words if published, though many family memoirs are much shorter.
  • Letters. Chapters addressed to a child or grandchild, which makes it natural to share lessons alongside stories.

A helpful exercise: write a short letter to your reader explaining why you are writing this book. Keep it on your desk. On days you feel stuck, it reminds you who is waiting for these pages. Many writers start with a family edition and decide later whether to share more widely.

How to gather memories before you start

Few people can sit down and remember on command. The mind goes quiet the moment you tell it to perform. That is why gathering comes before writing, so you are choosing from a list rather than hunting for something to say.

  • Draw a floor plan of a house, school or workplace that mattered to you. Mark each room and note what happened there, who was present and what you could smell or hear. Stories surface surprisingly fast.
  • Make a memory map. Write a topic in the middle of a page, such as "my first job", and draw lines out to every person, object and event that comes to mind.
  • Study photographs slowly. Ask who took it, what happened just before and after, and who is missing from the frame.
  • Build a life timeline with moves, schools, jobs, marriages, births and world events. Leave question marks where you are unsure.
  • Use your senses. Play music from your teens, cook a family dish or handle an old school report.
  • Talk to siblings, cousins and old friends over a cup of tea, and note both versions when memories differ.

Keep a small notebook with you, because memories pop up at odd moments. Give gathering two or three weeks at most, then start writing while you keep collecting on the side.

Choose a structure for your life story

Structure turns a pile of memories into a book someone can follow. You can pick it before you write or after you have a dozen stories. Either way, choose one main shape.

StructureHow it worksBest for
ChronologicalBirth or family roots to nowWhole-life keepsakes
ThematicGrouped by subject: homes, work, food, faithVaried lives, short stories
One periodA single era or thread, like years nursing or living abroadMemoirs for wider readers
LettersEach chapter written to a loved oneBooks for grandchildren

Not sure which fits? Write the title of each memory on an index card. Lay the cards out in date order, then shuffle and group them by theme. One arrangement usually feels more natural and shows you where the gaps are.

If you go chronological, do not cover every year. Choose the stories that matter and let the quiet years pass in a sentence or two, or the book starts to read like a list.

How to write your first memoir scene

Compare two openings. "My father was strict but fair" is a summary. It tells the reader something. A scene shows one moment: the evening he caught you doing something you should not have, what he said, what you felt. Readers remember scenes.

Good memoir uses both. Summary carries the reader across years; scenes slow down for the moments that count. Whenever you write a general statement about a person or a time, ask whether one moment shows it, and write that moment instead.

To write your first scene:

  1. Choose a moment that happened in one place, over a short time, with at least one other person present. A family meal, your first day at school or a time you got into trouble all work well.
  2. Close your eyes and step back into it. Where are you standing? What time of day is it?
  3. Make a five-senses list: see, hear, smell, taste, touch. Pick the two or three most specific details, the ones nobody else would think of.
  4. Write quickly in one sitting. A simple shape is: set the place, bring in the people, let something happen, show how it ended or how you felt.
  5. Do not explain the scene afterward. If you have shown it well, the reader already knows.

Most memoirs use the past tense and the first person. Stay with your younger self during the scene, then step back to reflect with what you know now.

Writing dialogue from memory honestly

You almost certainly do not remember conversations word for word from decades ago. That is normal, and memoir writers still use dialogue, because one line in someone's own voice can say more than a paragraph of description.

Write what the person would have said, in the way they said it, using their favorite expressions and kinds of words. Keep lines short, as real speech usually is. Avoid inventing conversations you did not witness or putting words in someone's mouth that change what happened.

Many memoirists add a short note at the front of the book saying the conversations are written from memory and may not be word for word, but are true to the people and what was said. That simple note keeps faith with readers.

Writing about the past can bring up painful memories you did not expect. It is fine to stop, set a story aside and come back another day, or not at all. Take breaks, write in short sessions, and talk to someone you trust, or your doctor or a counselor, if difficult feelings stay with you.

How to write about family without hurting them

Your story is tangled up with other people's lives, and they did not ask to be in a book. Write about them as you would hope to be written about: honestly, fairly and with some generosity.

  • Give people their full shape. A stern parent who also sang badly in the car is more real, and more forgivable, than one shown only at their worst.
  • Add context. A short temper reads differently when readers know the person was raising five children on one wage.
  • Label opinions as opinions. "I came to feel I could not trust what she told me" is as honest as "she was a liar" and much fairer.
  • Change names and identifying details for former partners, colleagues or neighbors, and say so in a note at the front.
  • Before including sensitive details about living people (health, money, affairs, adoptions), ask whether the detail is essential to your story. Often you can tell the truth without it.

When a sensitive detail truly matters, talk to the person before the book is shared and offer to show them the passage. Assume any family book will travel further than you plan. This is general information, not legal advice; if you plan to publish serious claims about living people widely, speak to a lawyer where you live.

How to revise and finish your memoir

When the first draft is done, make a backup and put it away for a week or more. Your memory fills the gaps on the page, so you need fresh eyes to see them.

Then revise in layers, from big to small:

  1. Structure. Read the whole draft without fixing anything. Note where you got bored or confused. Move, cut or add chapters. Keep a cuttings file so nothing is lost for good.
  2. Scenes. Turn important summaries into scenes and trim scenes that drag. Check every chapter's first and last paragraph.
  3. Paragraphs and sentences. Break up long blocks, cut words like "very" and "really", and swap vague words for specific ones.
  4. Proofreading. Check spellings of names and that ages and dates line up with your timeline.

Read every chapter aloud with a pencil in hand. Your ear catches awkward sentences and stiff dialogue your eye skips over. Keep your own voice, though. If you always said "folks" or "a good while", leave it in.

Give trusted readers specific questions, such as where their attention drifted. Look for patterns, and set a limit of about three full passes so the book actually gets finished.

Ways to share your finished life story

Match the format to your readers. For a few copies, print at home and have a copy shop bind them, or use an online print service. For a wider audience, self-publishing through a print on demand platform means copies are printed only when ordered. Read the terms, including who owns the rights, and be wary of any company asking for large up-front fees with big promises.

A few finishing touches go a long way: a clear typeface at 12 to 14 point, generous margins, photos with captions that add something, and one proof copy printed before you order more. A PDF is easy to send to relatives far away, and a recording of you reading a chapter aloud becomes a treasure for grandchildren.

If you have not started yet, a 12-week rhythm works well with three to five hours a week: weeks 1 to 3 for memories, timeline and structure, weeks 4 to 9 for drafting by life stage, and weeks 10 to 12 for revising, extras and sharing. Frequently asked questions

How do I start writing my life story?

Start by gathering, not writing. Draw a floor plan of a childhood home and note one memory beside each room, or study a single old photo and jot down who took it and what happened that day. Within a session or two you will have a list of moments. Pick the clearest one and write it as a short scene.

What is the difference between a memoir and an autobiography?

An autobiography usually covers a whole life in order, from birth to the present, with an emphasis on facts and events. A memoir often focuses on one period, theme or thread and leans on scenes, feelings and reflection. A family keepsake can mix both, with stories, photos and recipes written mainly for relatives.

Can I write about family members without their permission?

You can tell your own story, but care matters. Stick to the truth, label opinions as your own, change names where it helps privacy, and leave out sensitive details that are not essential. For sensitive material about living people, talking to them first is kindest. For wide publication with serious claims, get advice from a lawyer where you live.

How long should a memoir be?

There is no fixed length. A family keepsake can run 30 to 150 pages, often with photos. A published memoir is often around 50,000 to 80,000 words, but many family memoirs are shorter. If each story is 500 to 1,000 words, forty stories make a substantial book of 20,000 to 40,000 words.

What if I cannot remember exact conversations?

Hardly anyone can, and that is fine. Reconstruct dialogue so it is true to the person and what was said, using their usual words and expressions, and keep lines short. Add a brief note at the front explaining that conversations are written from memory. Avoid inventing talk for events you did not witness.

Do I need to be a good writer to write a memoir?

No. Your readers want your voice, your details and your memories, not polished literary prose. Clear sentences, specific details and a few vivid scenes matter far more than fancy words. A messy first draft is normal, and reading your work aloud during revision fixes most problems that new writers worry about.

Your first step today: draw a rough floor plan of a home you grew up in, mark five spots and write one memory beside each. One of those notes is very likely to become the first scene of your book.
