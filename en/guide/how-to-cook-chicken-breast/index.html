How to Cook Chicken Breast So It Stays Juicy Every Time

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your chicken breasts keep coming out dry and stringy, the recipe is rarely the problem. To cook chicken breast so it stays juicy, pound it to an even 3/4 inch (2 cm), season it, cook it in a hot oven, pan or air fryer and take it off the heat the moment the thickest part reads 165°F (74°C), then rest it for 5 minutes before slicing.

That one rule, cook to 165°F and stop, fixes most dry chicken. Below you will find exact times for each method, how to season ahead, how to handle raw chicken safely and the small mistakes that quietly ruin a good breast.

Why chicken breast dries out

Breasts are lean, with very little fat to protect them. A modern supermarket breast can weigh 10 oz (280 g) or more and be twice as thick at one end as the other. By the time the thick end is cooked through, the thin end is well past done.

The other common causes are just as easy to fix:

  • Cooking by the clock instead of by temperature
  • Cooking straight from the fridge in a crowded pan, so the chicken steams instead of browning
  • Leaving it on the heat "just a few more minutes to be safe"
  • Slicing it the second it comes off the heat, so the juices run onto the board

Fix the shape, measure the temperature and rest the meat, and even a plain breast with salt and pepper turns out tender.

Pound chicken breast to an even thickness

An uneven breast is the number one cause of dry chicken, and pounding fixes it in under a minute.

  1. Put the breast between two sheets of plastic wrap (cling film) or baking paper, or inside a large freezer bag.
  2. Using a meat mallet, rolling pin or the bottom of a small heavy pan, tap the thickest part gently, working outward.
  3. Stop when the whole piece is about 3/4 inch (2 cm) thick. For quick cutlets or schnitzel, keep going to 1/4 to 1/2 inch (6 mm to 1 cm).

If you do not want to pound, slice the breast in half horizontally to make two thinner cutlets, or butterfly it by cutting through the thick part and opening it like a book. Wash your hands, the board and the mallet in hot soapy water afterward.

Season ahead with a simple dry brine

Dry brining just means salting the chicken ahead of time. The salt draws out a little moisture, dissolves in it and is pulled back into the meat. The breast ends up seasoned all the way through and holds on to more of its juices.

  • Use about 3/4 teaspoon of fine salt per pound (450 g) of chicken. With coarse or flaky salt, use a little more.
  • Sprinkle it evenly over all sides and refrigerate the chicken uncovered on a plate or rack.
  • Even 30 minutes helps. A few hours or overnight is better.
  • Do not add more salt before cooking. Add spices, garlic powder or herbs just before it goes in.

If you prefer a marinade, keep it in the fridge, never on the counter. Yogurt and buttermilk marinades are fine for up to 24 hours, but strongly acidic citrus marinades can turn the outside mushy, so keep those to 2 to 4 hours. Pat the chicken dry before cooking, because wet chicken steams before it can brown.

Chicken breast cooking times and temperatures

These times assume boneless, skinless breasts of 6 to 8 oz (170 to 225 g), pounded to about 3/4 inch (2 cm). Ovens, pans and air fryers vary, so start checking a few minutes early and trust the thermometer.

Oven425°F (220°C, 200°C fan)18 to 22 minutes

Bone-in, skin-on breasts take longer, so rely on the thermometer rather than the clock whenever you swap cuts.

How to bake chicken breast in the oven

This is the easiest hands-off method and a good place to start.

  1. Take the chicken out of the fridge about 15 minutes before cooking and heat the oven to 425°F (220°C, 200°C fan).
  2. Pound the breasts to an even 3/4 inch (2 cm) and pat them dry.
  3. Brush with a little olive oil and season all over. A simple mix is salt, smoked paprika, garlic powder, dried oregano and black pepper.
  4. Arrange them in an oiled dish or lined sheet pan (baking tray) with space between each piece.
  5. Bake for 18 to 22 minutes, checking from 16 minutes, until the thickest part reads 165°F (74°C).
  6. Rest on a board for 5 minutes, then slice across the grain.

A hot oven matters. At lower temperatures the outside dries out during the long wait for the middle to cook.

How to cook chicken breast in a pan

The stovetop is the fastest method, especially with thin cutlets, and it gives you a golden crust and a pan sauce.

  1. Slice each breast in half horizontally and pound the pieces to about 1/2 inch (1 cm).
  2. Pat them dry and season. For extra crust, press them lightly into seasoned flour and shake off the excess.
  3. Heat a little oil, or oil plus butter, in a large skillet (frying pan) over medium-high heat until it shimmers.
  4. Lay the cutlets in without crowding. Cook in batches if needed, because a crowded pan steams.
  5. Leave them alone for 3 to 4 minutes until golden underneath, flip and cook another 3 to 4 minutes, to 165°F (74°C).

For a quick lemon pan sauce, move the chicken to a warm plate, pour about 1/2 cup (120 ml) of chicken broth (stock) and the juice of a lemon into the hot pan, simmer for 2 minutes while scraping up the browned bits, then swirl in a knob of butter and spoon it over.

Air fryer and grilled chicken breast

Air fryer

Pound the breasts to 3/4 inch (2 cm), rub with a little oil and season. Heat the air fryer to 375°F (190°C) if your model needs preheating, lay the breasts in a single layer and cook for 16 to 20 minutes, turning halfway. Start checking at 14 minutes, since air fryers vary a lot. Rest for 5 minutes.

Grill

Marinate pounded breasts in olive oil, lemon zest and some of the juice, grated garlic, oregano, salt and pepper for 30 minutes to 2 hours in the fridge. Heat the grill to medium-high, oil the grates and throw away the used marinade. Grill for 5 to 7 minutes per side without moving the chicken until it is well marked and reads 165°F (74°C). Finish with the rest of the lemon juice and some chopped parsley.

Always take a clean plate out for the cooked chicken. Putting it back on the platter that carried it raw is one of the most common food safety slips at a barbecue.

Use a thermometer and rest the meat

An instant-read digital thermometer is the single most useful tool for juicy chicken. It reads in 2 to 3 seconds and ends the guessing for good.

  • Push the probe sideways into the thickest part of the breast, not touching bone.
  • Take breasts off the heat as soon as they reach 165°F (74°C). There is no benefit in going further.
  • Clear juices and white meat are not reliable signs. Trust the number.
  • Rest boneless breasts for about 5 minutes, uncovered. The temperature rises a few degrees as they rest and the juices settle back in.

If you often cook for a family, thighs are worth knowing about. They are safe at the same 165°F (74°C), but many cooks take them to 175 to 185°F (80 to 85°C) for softer meat, and they stay juicy even if dinner runs late. Handling raw chicken safely

A few habits keep everyone at your table safe.

  • Store raw chicken on the bottom shelf of the fridge at 40°F (4°C) or below and use it within 1 to 2 days, or freeze it.
  • Thaw in the fridge or in a sealed bag in cold water, changing the water every 30 minutes. Never thaw on the counter.
  • Do not rinse raw chicken. It does not remove bacteria and splashes them around your sink. Pat it dry with paper towels instead.
  • Use a separate board and knife for raw chicken, and wash your hands after every touch.
  • Never reuse a marinade that touched raw chicken unless you boil it hard for at least 1 minute.
Leftovers. Refrigerate cooked chicken within 2 hours (within 1 hour on a day above 90°F / 32°C) in shallow containers and eat it within 3 to 4 days. Reheat until steaming hot all the way through, or 165°F (74°C), and reheat it only once.

Frequently asked questions

How long do you bake chicken breast at 400°F?

A boneless breast of 6 to 8 oz (170 to 225 g), pounded to about 3/4 inch (2 cm), usually needs around 20 to 25 minutes at 400°F (200°C, 180°C fan). Thicker, unpounded breasts take longer. Ovens vary, so start checking a few minutes early and take the chicken out as soon as the thickest part reads 165°F (74°C).

Is it better to bake chicken at 350 or 400?

For boneless chicken breast, a hotter oven usually gives juicier results. At 400 to 425°F (200 to 220°C, 180 to 200°C fan) the breast cooks quickly, so the outside has less time to dry out while the middle catches up. Lower temperatures work for slower dishes, such as casseroles with plenty of sauce, but dry the breast out more on its own.

Is chicken breast safe if it is slightly pink?

Color is not a reliable sign either way. Chicken can stay slightly pink even when fully cooked, and it can look white before it is safe. The only reliable test is an instant-read thermometer in the thickest part. If it reads 165°F (74°C), the chicken is safe to eat. If it reads less, keep cooking and check again.

Should you cover chicken breast when baking?

There is usually no need. Baking boneless breasts uncovered in a hot oven gives better color and keeps cooking time short, which keeps them juicy. Even thickness, a light coat of oil and pulling them out at 165°F (74°C) do more than foil. Rest them uncovered for 5 minutes so the outside does not turn soggy.

How do you cook frozen chicken breast?

The safest and juiciest way is to thaw it first, overnight in the fridge or in a sealed bag in cold water, changing the water every 30 minutes. Never start frozen chicken in a slow cooker, because it spends too long at unsafe temperatures. Chicken thawed in the microwave or in cold water should be cooked straight away.

Why is my chicken breast rubbery?

Rubbery chicken is usually overcooked, cooked in a crowded pan where it steamed, or cut from a breast with a tough, woody texture. Pound it even, pat it dry, give each piece space in a hot pan or oven and take it off the heat at 165°F (74°C). A short dry brine with salt beforehand also helps it stay tender.

Next time you cook chicken breast, do just two things differently: pound it to an even 3/4 inch (2 cm), and pull it off the heat the moment a thermometer reads 165°F (74°C). Rest it for 5 minutes before you slice. Those two changes alone will turn dry, stringy chicken into the juicy kind your family actually asks for.
