How to Become a Copywriter: A Beginner's Plan From Skills to Clients

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If people keep asking you to look over their website text, or you are a teacher, journalist or office worker who quietly enjoys rewriting clumsy signs in your head, copywriting may suit you. To become a copywriter you learn the core skills (reader research, benefits, headlines, structure and editing), build a small portfolio of six to ten samples, set up simple business basics and then reach out to clients in a niche you understand.

You do not need a degree or a fancy vocabulary. You do need patience, because building a freelance client base usually takes time. This guide gives you the full plan, with before and after examples you can practice on tonight.

How to become a copywriter in 7 steps

  1. Learn what copy is for: writing that asks a reader to take one action, honestly and clearly.
  2. Practice reader research and write simple reader profiles.
  3. Master features versus benefits, headlines and a few persuasive structures.
  4. Learn to edit hard and to write claims you can back up.
  5. Build a portfolio of six to ten labeled samples.
  6. Set up as a freelancer with a contract, an invoice template and a tax plan.
  7. Choose a working niche and contact businesses, agencies and your own network.

A realistic pace is a few hours a week over about three months: one month practicing the craft, one month building samples and one month setting up and reaching out.

What does a copywriter actually do

Copywriters write words that help someone choose, book, buy, donate or sign up. That covers web pages, product descriptions, emails, ads, brochures, social posts and scripts, plus small jobs like menu descriptions and checkout pages. Copywriting is not the same as copyright, which is about legal ownership of creative work, and it has nothing to do with hype.

Start with the reader, not the product

Good copy begins before you write a word. Find out who the reader is, what problem they have, what they want instead and what might stop them from acting.

The best phrases often come straight from customers. Read reviews of the business and its competitors (three-star reviews are often the most detailed), ask staff what customers ask most, and if possible talk to a few real customers. Useful questions include what nearly stopped them from buying and how they would describe the service to a friend.

Put it all on one page: who the reader is, their main problem in their own words, what they want, their top three objections, where they will read your copy (often on a phone, in a hurry) and words to use or avoid.

Turn features into benefits with the so what test

A feature is a fact about the product. A benefit is what that fact means for the reader. To get from one to the other, keep asking "so what?" until you reach something the reader actually cares about.

  • Feature: open until 8 pm on weekdays. Benefit: book an appointment after work, without taking time off.
  • Feature: machine washable cover. Benefit: spills and muddy paws wipe away in one wash.
  • Feature: dog walks in groups of four or fewer. Benefit: your dog gets real attention and comes home tired and happy.

Specific beats vague every time. "Fast delivery" means little. "Order by 2 pm for next-day delivery across the mainland" lets the reader picture it and trust it.

Cut empty words that every business uses: quality, premium, passionate, innovative, world class, one-stop shop. If a sentence would fit any competitor's website just as well, it is not doing its job.

Write headlines and structure that persuade

A headline has one job: to make the right reader want to read the next line. Five reliable starting points are how to, question, direct benefit, news and a specific number used honestly.

  • How to: "How to train your puppy without giving up your weekends"
  • Question: "Still doing your accounts at midnight?"
  • Direct benefit: "Patient driving lessons for nervous beginners"
  • Number: "7 questions to ask before you hire a builder"

Write twenty versions before choosing, since the best ideas usually come after the obvious ones. Every headline is a promise, and the page must keep it.

Structures to keep in your toolbox

StructureBest for

These are tools, not formulas that guarantee results. Whatever the shape, open with the reader's situation, build your case with benefits, add real proof, answer objections honestly and finish with one clear call to action that starts with a verb, such as "Book a tour".

Then edit. Use everyday words, short sentences and active voice ("We send your order within 24 hours" beats "Your order will be dispatched by our team"). Read every draft aloud, and spell for the client's audience, US or UK.

Honest copy rules every beginner should know

Advertising rules vary by country, but in the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand marketing must not mislead. Clients usually approve copy and check regulated claims, but you should refuse to write anything you know is false.

  • Every factual claim must be true and possible to back up with evidence.
  • Testimonials must come from real customers, with permission, and reflect typical experience. Never invent reviews, even as placeholders.
  • Clearly disclose paid endorsements, affiliate links and sponsored content.
  • No fake urgency or scarcity, such as false countdowns or "only 2 left" when it is untrue.
  • State prices and offer conditions clearly, with no hidden fees.
  • Take extra care with health, money, green and "free" claims, and with marketing to children.
  • Write original work and only use images and quotes you have the rights to.
This is general information, not legal advice. Check the advertising standards body or consumer protection authority in your country, and recommend legal review for regulated fields such as finance, health, alcohol and legal services. Marketing emails generally need consent and an easy unsubscribe.

Build a copywriting portfolio with no experience

A starter portfolio needs six to ten strong samples, not dozens. Clients want to see that you can think, not that you have a long client list.

  • Two or three web pages, such as a home page and a service page.
  • A few product descriptions.
  • A short welcome email sequence or a newsletter.
  • Two or three short ads or social captions.
  • One or two before and after rewrites, with notes explaining your choices.

Spec samples for invented businesses, such as a mobile bike repair service or a family bakery, are a legitimate way to show skill as long as you label them clearly. A small volunteer project for a charity can add real-world experience, if you agree the scope and ask written permission to show the work.

Present each sample with a short title, one or two lines of context and a note on your approach. Never claim results you cannot prove. If a client gives you accurate figures and permission, share them in context. How to find your first copywriting clients

Before outreach, set up the basics: register as self-employed or as a business if required where you live, open a separate bank account, set money aside for tax, consider professional indemnity insurance and adapt a written contract. The contract should cover scope, revision rounds, deadlines, payment terms, a deposit, a kill fee, copyright transfer, confidentiality and portfolio permission.

Price by method rather than guesswork: per project, per piece, day rate or monthly retainer, with research and revision rounds built into the quote. Avoid per-word pricing for short, high-value copy such as headlines.

Where first clients come from

  • Your existing network: send ten personal messages saying what you now do and who you help.
  • Local businesses in a niche you understand, approached in person or with tailored emails.
  • Agencies that use freelancers for overflow work.
  • Freelance marketplaces and professional networking sites, with tailored proposals.

A good cold email goes to a named person, shows you looked at their business, offers one useful observation and asks for a short call. Watch for red flags: unpaid "test" pieces that are really free work, overpayment refund scams, requests for fake reviews, refusal to sign a contract, and payment only "after launch".

Frequently asked questions

Can you become a copywriter with no experience?

Yes. Many copywriters start with no paid experience at all. Learn the core skills, then build a portfolio of six to ten clearly labeled spec samples for invented businesses, plus one or two before and after rewrites with notes. A small volunteer project for a charity can help. Clients mainly want proof that you understand readers and can write clearly.

Do you need a degree to be a copywriter?

No. Clients and most employers care far more about your portfolio than a degree. Some in-house and agency roles list a degree in marketing, English or journalism, but strong samples, clear thinking and reliability often matter more. Former teachers, retail staff, admin workers and journalists all move into copywriting through practice and a good portfolio.

How long does it take to become a copywriter?

A realistic plan is about three months of part-time effort to learn the basics, build a starter portfolio and begin outreach. Landing steady clients usually takes longer, often many months, because trust and referrals build gradually. Practicing regularly, getting honest feedback and following up politely with potential clients speeds things up more than any shortcut.

What is the difference between copywriting and content writing?

Copywriting asks the reader to take a specific action, such as booking, buying, donating or signing up. Content writing mainly informs or entertains, such as articles, guides and blog posts, and builds trust over time. The skills overlap a lot, and many writers do both, but copy usually has one clear call to action.

How do freelance copywriters charge?

Common methods are per project, per piece, a day rate and a monthly retainer for ongoing work. Value-based pricing is another option once you have experience. Whatever you choose, include research, drafting, editing and a set number of revision rounds in the quote. Research what others in your market charge, and raise rates for new clients first.

Can I use AI writing tools as a copywriter?

They can help with brainstorming, but use them carefully. Check every fact, keep the human voice, never present invented facts or testimonials, and protect your client's confidential information. Some clients do not want these tools used on their projects, so ask first and follow their wishes. Your research, judgment and editing are what clients pay for.

Your first step today: pick one small local business you know well and run five of its features through the "so what?" test, then rewrite its main headline twenty ways. That single exercise is the start of your first portfolio sample.
