Emergency Kit Checklist: What Every Family Needs at Home

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If a storm warning or a long power cut has left you wondering what you would actually need, you are asking the right question at the right time. A good home emergency kit holds enough water, food, light, medicine and basic supplies to keep everyone in your household going for at least 3 days without power, running water or shops.

You do not need to become a survivalist or spend a fortune. Many items are already in your home, scattered across drawers and cupboards. This checklist follows mainstream advice from government emergency agencies and the Red Cross in the US and UK, and shows you how to build the kit a little at a time.

What is a home emergency kit for

A home kit is for sheltering in place: the days when you stay at home but the power is out, the taps are not safe or the roads are closed. The emergencies most families actually face are ordinary ones, such as winter storms, floods, heat waves and power outages, rather than dramatic disasters.

It is different from a grab-and-go bag (sometimes called a go bag), which is a lighter backpack for leaving home in a hurry. Ideally you have both. Start with the home kit, because it covers the situations you are most likely to meet.

Organize your kit into four groups so it is easy to build and easy to find things in the dark:

  1. Water and food
  2. Light, power and communication
  3. First aid, health and hygiene
  4. Tools, documents and comfort

How much water to store for an emergency

Water comes first. US official guidance recommends at least 1 gallon (about 4 liters) per person per day for at least 3 days, which covers drinking and basic hygiene. Many experts suggest keeping more, up to two weeks, if you have space. UK government guidance also encourages households to keep bottled water at home, and 4 liters per person per day is a practical figure to use.

HouseholdPer day3 days
1 person1 gal (about 4 L)3 gal (about 12 L)
2 people2 gal (about 8 L)6 gal (about 23 L)
4 people4 gal (about 15 L)12 gal (about 45 L)

Add extra for pets, children, nursing mothers, anyone unwell and hot weather, which can double what people need to drink.

The simplest option is sealed, store-bought bottled water. Keep it cool and dark, away from gasoline (petrol), paint and cleaning products, and replace it by the date on the pack. If you ever need to treat water, boiling at a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above about 6,500 feet or 2,000 meters) is the standard method. Only use household bleach if official instructions tell you to, and follow the latest official guidance exactly.

Emergency food checklist for 3 days

Choose non-perishable food that needs no cooking or very little, and that your family actually likes. A crisis is not the time to discover nobody will eat canned sardines. Think of it as a small extra pantry that you rotate into normal meals.

  • Canned foods such as beans, soup, fish, chicken, fruit and vegetables
  • A manual can opener (this one gets forgotten more than anything else)
  • Ready-to-eat foods such as crackers, nut butter, dried fruit, cereal and granola bars
  • Shelf-stable milk or milk alternatives
  • Baby formula and baby food if needed, plus food for any special diets
  • Pet food for at least 3 days
  • Paper plates, cups and cutlery

A simple no-cook day might be cereal with shelf-stable milk for breakfast, crackers with canned tuna and fruit for lunch, and canned beans with ready-to-eat rice pouches and vegetables for dinner. Write the use-by dates on the front of cans with a marker, and when you buy fresh stock, put it at the back and use the older items first.

Light, power and communication items

When the lights go out, these are the things you reach for first, so keep them at the top of the box or in a separate small bag.

  • Flashlights (UK: torches) or headlamps, one per person
  • A battery-powered lantern for a shared room
  • Spare batteries in the right sizes, kept in their packaging
  • A battery-powered or hand-crank radio (in the US, ideally one that receives weather alerts)
  • A power bank, kept charged, with a charging cable for each phone
  • A whistle to signal for help
  • A printed list of emergency contacts and phone numbers

Headlamps are a quiet favorite with parents. They leave both hands free, and children tend to love wearing them, which makes a dark evening feel more like camping than a crisis. Avoid candles where you can, especially around children and pets, because battery lights are far safer.

Carbon monoxide can kill. Never use a generator, grill, camp stove or charcoal indoors or in a garage, even with the doors open. In the US, guidance is to keep generators at least 20 feet (6 meters) from windows and doors. Fit carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup. If anyone feels dizzy, sick, sleepy or has a headache, get everyone into fresh air and call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

First aid, medicine and hygiene supplies

Health items are the most personal part of the kit, so take a few minutes to think about each person in your home.

  • A first aid kit, checked and restocked
  • A supply of prescription medications, plus copies of prescriptions and a written medication list
  • Pain relief, allergy medicine and anything else your family uses regularly
  • Spare glasses, contact lens supplies and hearing aid batteries
  • Hand sanitizer, wet wipes, soap, toothbrushes and toothpaste
  • Toilet paper, garbage bags (bin bags) and plastic ties for sanitation
  • Sanitary products, plus diapers (nappies) and wipes if needed
  • Face masks for dust or smoke

If someone relies on powered medical equipment or medicines that must stay cold, plan for that now, because these are the first things affected in an outage. A basic first aid course is one of the most useful things any adult in the household can do.

Ask about your medicines. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist about how much spare medication is sensible to keep and how to store it, especially for medicines that need the fridge or devices that need power. For emergencies call 911 in the US, or 999 or 112 in the UK. In the UK, 111 is the NHS non-emergency number.

Tools, documents and comfort items

The last group covers practical jobs, your paperwork and the small things that keep people warm and calm during a long wait.

  • A wrench or pliers to turn off utilities, used only if you know how and it is safe
  • Duct tape, plastic sheeting, work gloves and a multi-tool
  • A fire extinguisher, kept somewhere easy to reach
  • Copies of IDs, insurance details and medical information in a waterproof bag
  • Some cash in small bills (UK: small notes and coins), since card machines may be down
  • A local paper map, spare house and car keys and your written family plan
  • Blankets or sleeping bags, a change of warm clothes and sturdy shoes for each person
  • Rain gear, plus books, cards, puzzles and a comfort item for young children

Games and comfort items can look like a luxury on a list, but anyone who has spent a dark, cold evening with bored children knows otherwise. A deck of cards weighs almost nothing and buys hours of calm.

How to build your emergency kit on a budget

You do not need to buy everything this weekend. Spreading the work over about 12 weeks keeps the cost manageable, and a few items added to each grocery trip barely shows on the bill. Start by gathering what you already own.

WeeksFocusExample tasks
1GatherCollect flashlights, batteries and first aid items in one bin
2 and 3Water and foodAdd 1 day of water and no-cook food per person
4LightAdd headlamps or a lantern and spare batteries
5 and 6Water and foodAdd a second day, plus a can opener
7 and 8Health and radioRestock first aid, add hygiene items, a radio and a power bank
9Water and foodComplete 3 days for everyone
10 and 11Documents and comfortCopy documents, add blankets, clothes, games and cash
12CheckTest everything and write the date on the lid

If money is tight, focus on water, food, light, a radio and medications first. A partly built kit is far more useful than a perfect one you never get around to finishing. Many families are surprised by how much they already have once they look in the garage and kitchen drawers.

Where to store your kit and how to keep it ready

Keep the kit somewhere cool, dry, easy to reach and known to everyone in the household. A hall closet (UK: cupboard), the bottom of a wardrobe or a dry corner of a basement all work. Avoid attics and lofts, which get hot and are awkward in the dark, and keep everything upstairs or on a high shelf if you live in a flood-prone area.

Large plastic bins with lids are ideal. Split heavy water and food into one bin and everything else into another, and label them clearly so a babysitter or visiting grandparent could find them.

Check the kit every six months, perhaps on the day you test your smoke alarms:

  • Replace expired food, water past its date and out-of-date medicines
  • Test flashlights and radios, and recharge power banks
  • Keep spare batteries in their packaging, not inside devices, where they can leak
  • Update phone numbers and swap clothes that children have outgrown

Whenever you use something during a real outage, replace it within a week. The book Ready for Anything: The Family Emergency Preparedness Guide includes printable checklists for the home kit, grab-and-go bags and a 12-month preparedness plan if you want everything in one place.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important items in an emergency kit?

Water, food, light and medicine matter most. Store about 1 gallon (4 liters) of water per person per day for at least 3 days, no-cook food your family will eat, a flashlight or headlamp for each person with spare batteries, a battery or hand-crank radio, a first aid kit and a supply of regular medications as your doctor or pharmacist advises.

How long should an emergency kit last?

Official US and UK advice suggests planning for at least 3 days of sheltering at home without power, running water or shops. Many experts recommend building toward a longer supply, such as two weeks of water and food, if you have the space and budget. Start with 3 days, then add more gradually as part of your normal shopping.

What is the difference between an emergency kit and a go bag?

A home emergency kit is a larger supply for staying put, usually stored in bins at home. A grab-and-go bag is a backpack for each person to carry if you must leave quickly, with water, snacks, a flashlight, medicines, copies of documents, cash, a change of clothes and a phone charger. Most families benefit from having both.

How often should I check my emergency kit?

Every six months is a good rhythm. Check dates on water, food and medicines, test flashlights and radios, recharge power banks and update contact numbers. Replace anything you use in a real emergency within about a week. Pairing the check with another routine, such as testing smoke alarms or changing the clocks, makes it easier to remember.

Should I include cash in an emergency kit?

Yes, a small amount in low denominations is sensible. During power outages and network problems, card machines and cash machines may stop working, so cash helps you buy essentials. Keep it in a sealed bag with copies of your important documents, and store it somewhere secure that the adults in your home know about.

What should I add to an emergency kit for pets?

Pack at least 3 days of food and water with bowls, any medications, a leash (lead) or carrier, waste bags or litter supplies, copies of vaccination records, a recent photo of your pet and your vet's contact details. Ask your vet how much water your pet usually drinks, since needs vary with size, diet and weather.

Your first step today is to choose one bin and one spot for your kit, then walk through your home and gather every flashlight, battery and first aid item you already own into it. Add one day of water for each person on your next shopping trip, and you are already better prepared than you were this morning.
