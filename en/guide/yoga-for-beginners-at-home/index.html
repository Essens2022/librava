Yoga for Beginners at Home: How to Start With 10 Easy Minutes

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have always felt too stiff, too busy or too unsure to try yoga, home is a very good place to begin. The short answer: clear a small patch of floor, learn a handful of gentle poses such as Mountain Pose, Cat-Cow, Child's Pose and a bent-knee Downward-Facing Dog, and practice for about 10 minutes most days, staying in mild tension and never forcing anything.

This guide covers what you need (very little), how to set up, eight beginner poses step by step, simple breathing, a ready-made 10-minute routine, and a four-week plan to turn a few tries into a habit. If you cannot touch your toes, you are exactly the person this is written for.

Can you really learn yoga at home as a beginner?

Yes. Yoga, as most beginners use it, is simply movement, breathing and attention. You do not need a studio, special clothes or any flexibility to start. Being stiff is the reason to start, not a reason to wait.

In the first few weeks, many beginners notice small things: getting out of bed feels a little easier, a tight back eases, and a few slow breaths start to calm a busy mind. Yoga can help with flexibility, balance, strength and feeling calmer. It is not a weight-loss plan or a cure for illness, and it does not need to be religious; at home it can be a purely physical and breathing practice.

Yoga comes from India, where it developed over many centuries. The gentle poses most beginners learn today are one modern part of a much older tradition, and it is worth knowing that when you see the Sanskrit names.

What you need to start yoga at home

A clear patch of floor about 6 feet long and 3 feet wide (roughly 2 meters by 1 meter) is enough for every beginner pose. A spot near a wall is a bonus for balance poses.

A yoga mat stops slipping and cushions your knees, but a rug or towel on a non-slip floor will do for now. Avoid a loose towel on a smooth floor. You can swap studio props for things you already own:

Wear anything stretchy, such as leggings or sweatpants (UK: tracksuit bottoms), and practice barefoot so your feet grip. Eat lightly first: a small snack an hour before is fine, but after a full meal wait 2 to 3 hours.

The one safety rule every beginner needs

A stretch should feel like mild tension, like reaching for something on a high shelf. It should never feel sharp, pinching, burning or tingling. Rate the feeling from 1 to 10: aim for about 3 or 4, and come out if it climbs past 5.

  • Never force a pose and never bounce to go further.
  • Keep breathing smoothly. Holding your breath means you are pushing too hard.
  • In lunges and Warrior poses, keep your front knee over your ankle, not past your toes.
  • Pad your knees whenever you kneel, and do balance poses near a wall or chair.

Beginners do not need headstands, shoulderstands, plow pose or deep backbends. They put a lot of pressure on the neck and spine and are best left out at home.

Talk to your doctor first if you are pregnant, have recently had surgery, or have high or low blood pressure, glaucoma, osteoporosis, a joint replacement, back or neck problems, a heart condition or any other health concern. If you have glaucoma or uncontrolled blood pressure, ask about poses where your head goes below your heart. Stop and seek help for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, dizziness that does not pass or sudden severe pain; for chest pain call emergency services (911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

Easy yoga poses for beginners, step by step

These eight poses are a solid base. Hold each for 3 to 5 slow breaths unless noted.

Mountain Pose (Tadasana)

Stand with feet hip width apart. Spread your toes and feel your weight through the heel and the base of the big and little toes. Soften your knees, roll your shoulders back and down once, and lengthen up through the top of your head. Breathe for 5 slow breaths.

Cat-Cow

On hands and knees, hands under shoulders and knees under hips, breathe in and let your belly lower as you look slightly forward. Breathe out and round your back toward the ceiling, chin toward chest. Move slowly with your breath for 5 to 10 rounds.

Child's Pose (Balasana)

From hands and knees, bring your big toes together, widen your knees if that is comfier, and sit your hips back toward your heels. Rest your forehead on the floor or a cushion. This is your go-to rest pose whenever you need a break.

Downward-Facing Dog (Adho Mukha Svanasana)

  1. Start on all fours, hands slightly in front of your shoulders, toes tucked under.
  2. Breathe out, lift your knees and send your hips up and back.
  3. Keep your knees well bent so your back stays long, and let your heels lift.
  4. Let your head hang, neck relaxed. Lower your knees to come out.

For a gentler version that keeps your head higher than your heart, put your hands on a chair seat pushed against a wall.

Low Lunge, Warrior II and Tree Pose

For Low Lunge (Anjaneyasana), step one foot forward between your hands from all fours, with the back knee on a folded towel and the front knee over the ankle. For Warrior II, stand with feet wide, turn one foot out, bend that knee over the ankle and reach your arms out to the sides. For Tree Pose, stand side-on to a wall with a hand on it and rest the other foot's heel against your ankle, or its sole on your calf or inner thigh, never on the side of the knee.

Corpse Pose (Savasana)

Lie on your back, legs long, arms a little away from your sides, palms up. If your lower back complains, bend your knees or slide a cushion under them. Cover yourself with a blanket and simply rest for 1 to 5 minutes.

Simple breathing for yoga beginners

Breathing is the quiet half of yoga, and you can practice it sitting or lying down. Keep it gentle: no breath holding and no fast, forceful breathing. If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally.

  1. Belly breathing. One hand on your chest, one on your belly. Breathe in slowly and let the belly rise into your hand while the chest stays fairly still. Try 6 to 10 breaths.
  2. Counting the breath. Breathe in to a slow count of 4 and out to 4. Use 3 if 4 feels long. When you lose count, start again at 1.
  3. Longer exhale. Breathe in for 4 and out for 5 or 6, like letting air out of a balloon a little at a time. Five or six rounds is often enough to feel calmer, and it works well in bed.

This wake-up flow starts on the floor and brings you slowly to standing. Warm up first with a few shoulder rolls and wrist and ankle circles. Times are rough, so count slow breaths if you prefer.

If getting to the floor is hard right now, the same ideas work on a sturdy chair: seated cat-cow, seated side stretches, a gentle seated twist and a standing balance holding the chair back.

How often should beginners do yoga?

Little and often beats long and rare. A good start is 10 minutes on most days, with at least one easy day a week. A short practice done 25 days out of 30 does more than a long one done 6 times.

Pick a time and attach it to something you already do. Write one sentence on a sticky note: "After I ____, I will do ____ for ____ minutes." For example, after starting the coffee, do Cat-Cow and Downward-Facing Dog until it is ready.

Make starting easy by leaving your mat out and your props in a basket beside it. Mark each practice day with an X on a paper calendar, and jot down how you felt. If you miss a day, try not to miss two; the next day, even one minute in Child's Pose keeps the thread going.

Use this outline to build up gently. Every seventh day is a rest day, with an optional 5-minute stretch in bed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do yoga if I am not flexible?

Yes, and you are the ideal beginner. Flexibility is something yoga helps with over time, not something you need first. Bend your knees in forward folds and Downward-Facing Dog, use books as blocks and a belt as a strap, and aim for mild tension only. Flexibility varies a lot between people because of bone shape, age and history, so never compare your reach with anyone else's.

How long should a beginner yoga session be?

About 10 minutes is a great start, and even 5 minutes counts on busy days. Once that feels easy, try a 20-minute session a couple of times a week. Every session should include a short warm-up at the start and at least a minute of rest in Corpse Pose at the end. Consistency matters much more than length in the first month.

Is it better to do yoga in the morning or at night?

The best time is the one you will keep. Morning practice can ease stiffness after sleep, but your body will be tighter, so move gently. Evening practice suits people who want to unwind, and slow poses with a longer exhale can help you settle before bed. Avoid practicing right after a big meal, whatever time you choose.

What yoga poses should beginners avoid?

At home, skip headstands, shoulderstands, plow pose and deep backbends, which put heavy pressure on the neck and spine. Avoid forcing any pose, bouncing, or pushing your front knee past your toes in lunges. If you have glaucoma or uncontrolled blood pressure, ask your doctor before any pose where your head goes below your heart, such as Downward-Facing Dog.

Do I need a yoga mat to start?

No. A mat helps with grip and knee comfort, but a rug or folded towel on a non-slip floor works while you get started. Avoid a loose towel on a smooth, slippery floor, especially for standing poses. Use a folded blanket under your knees when kneeling, and keep a sturdy chair without wheels nearby for balance.

Can older adults start yoga at home?

Yes. Older beginners often do well because they tend to be patient and less tempted to push. Do balance poses beside a wall or chair, use chair yoga if the floor is hard to reach, and get up slowly after lying down. Check with your doctor first if you have osteoporosis, a joint replacement, heart or blood pressure problems or any other health concern.

Your first step today: find a patch of floor near a wall, gather a cushion, a belt and two thick books, and lie down for 2 minutes of belly breathing tonight. Tomorrow morning, try the 10-minute routine slowly, and notice how your body feels before and after.
