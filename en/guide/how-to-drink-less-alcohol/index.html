How to Drink Less Alcohol: A Practical Plan to Cut Back Safely

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If the evening glass of wine has quietly turned into most of the bottle, you are far from alone, and you do not need a label to want a change. To drink less alcohol, first count what you really drink for a week, then set a clear goal with a weekly limit and alcohol-free days, plan what you will do and drink instead at your usual drinking times, and have a few tools ready for cravings and social pressure.

This guide walks you through each step with numbers, scripts and simple routines. One thing comes first, though: for some people, cutting back too fast is not safe, so please read the safety note below before you start.

Is it safe to cut down on drinking?

For most people who drink moderately, cutting back gradually is safe. But alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous for people who drink heavily.

Do not stop suddenly without medical advice if you drink heavily every day, drink in the morning to feel normal, or get shaking, sweating, anxiety, nausea or trouble sleeping when you cut back. Withdrawal can cause seizures (fits), hallucinations or confusion. Talk to your doctor (GP) first, who can help you cut down safely. Call 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia or 111 in New Zealand for a seizure, severe confusion, seeing or hearing things that are not there, or chest pain. Also check with a doctor or pharmacist about any medicines you take, and before making big changes if you have a health condition.

This is general information, not medical advice or addiction treatment. Talking to a doctor, nurse or pharmacist about your drinking is always a sensible step.

How much do you really drink?

Most people underestimate their drinking, mainly because home pours are bigger than a standard drink and strengths vary. Learn what one drink means where you live.

Guidance changes, so check the current official advice where you live. For some risks, such as several cancers, there is no completely safe level: the less you drink, the lower the risk. Avoid alcohol completely when pregnant or trying to conceive and when driving.

Keep a one-week drink diary

For seven days, write down each drink, its size and strength, where you were, why you drank and how you felt. Pour your usual glass into a measuring jug once to see its real size. Most labels show units or standard drinks, which makes counting easier. Your diary will show your patterns, and that is where your plan starts.

Choose a goal that fits your life

  • Cutting back: fewer drinks each week with set limits.
  • Alcohol-free days: several days a week with no alcohol at all.
  • A 30-day break: a reset to see how you feel without it.
  • Stopping for good: some people find a clear line easier than limits.

If you cut back, write down three numbers: your weekly maximum, your daily maximum and your number of alcohol-free days. Then add rules that make them easier to keep:

  • Only drink with food, never alone at home or before a set time.
  • Use a smaller glass and buy single cans or small bottles.
  • Alternate every alcoholic drink with water or an alcohol-free one.
  • Never open a second bottle.

Step down steadily rather than dramatically. Going from 30 units to 5 overnight with no plan for your evenings is the classic way to give up by Friday.

Spot your triggers and change your evenings

Much drinking runs on autopilot. A cue (6 p.m., getting home, a stressful call) leads to a routine (pouring a drink), which brings a reward (relaxing, a treat, a pause). You keep the reward and change the routine.

TriggerTry instead
Wine o'clock after workA special alcohol-free drink in your usual glass, a shower, a change of clothes
StressA brisk walk, five minutes of slow breathing, writing it down
BoredomA planned evening activity: a class, a call, a project
LonelinessA standing phone call or meeting a friend somewhere without a bar
CelebrationsDecide your number before you go and have a treat that is not a drink

Change the cues too. Move alcohol out of sight, stop buying it with the weekly shop, and eat earlier. Stock your fridge with things you enjoy drinking, such as sparkling water with lime and mint, ginger and lime with soda, or iced tea topped with tonic.

How to stop alcohol cravings in the moment

A craving feels urgent, but it rises, peaks and falls, usually within minutes. These tools help you ride it out.

  1. The 15-minute rule. Do not say no forever, say "not for 15 minutes." Set a timer and do something else. Delaying is easier than refusing, and the urge has often faded by the time it rings.
  2. Urge surfing. Name the urge, breathe slowly, notice where you feel it in your body and rate it from 1 to 10 every few minutes. Watching the number climb and then drop shows you that urges pass.
  3. The HALT check. Ask: am I hungry, angry, lonely or tired? Eat something, talk it through, call someone or rest. Very often the real need is a sandwich and an early night.
  4. Quick distractions: wash up, walk round the block, text a friend or have a hot drink.

Write your three best tools on a small card and keep it where you usually drink.

Drinking less when you go out

Social events are where plans often wobble, so prepare before you leave home. Eat first, decide your number, choose your first drink before you arrive and arrange your way home. Never drink and drive.

  • Order an alcohol-free drink first, and alternate with water or soda.
  • Hold a drink you like so people stop offering.
  • Arrive a bit later or leave a bit earlier. Nobody notices as much as you think.

You do not owe anyone a reason, but short lines help: "No thanks, I'm good with this one," "I'm driving tonight," or "I've had my one, I'm on sparkling water now." If someone pushes, smile and repeat yourself. A note on alcohol-free beer and wine: many contain up to about 0.5% alcohol, so check labels, especially if you are pregnant, on certain medicines or recovering from alcohol dependence.

Unwind and sleep without a drink

If alcohol has become your off switch, you need a new one. A short walk after work, a shower, five minutes of breathing in for four counts and out for six, music or writing down the day's worries all signal that work is over.

Alcohol can make you drowsy but tends to disturb sleep later in the night. When you cut back, sleep may take a little time to settle. Keep a regular wake-up time, wind down for an hour before bed and skip caffeine late in the day.

If drinking is tied up with low mood or anxiety, talk to your doctor. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US or Canada, call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK, Lifeline on 13 11 14 in Australia, or call or text 1737 in New Zealand.

What to do after a slip

A slip is information, not failure. Avoid the "might as well" trap, where one extra drink turns into a lost weekend. Start again with your next drink or your next day, not next Monday.

Then do a quick review: what was the trigger, what time was it, how were you feeling and what would you do differently? High-risk times include holidays, celebrations, grief and big news, so plan for them ahead. If slips keep happening, or drinking feels hard to control, it is time for more support. A doctor can talk through options including counseling, peer support groups and medicines. Free confidential helplines include 1-800-662-4357 in the US, 0300 123 1110 in the UK, 211 for local services in Canada, 1800 250 015 in Australia and 0800 787 797 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to cut down on alcohol?

Start by counting what you really drink for a week, then set a clear weekly limit and a number of alcohol-free days. Plan what you will do and drink at your usual drinking times, because changing the routine matters more than willpower. Use smaller glasses, alternate with water and step down gradually. If you drink heavily every day, talk to a doctor first.

How many alcohol-free days a week should I have?

UK guidance advises several drink-free days a week, and New Zealand guidance advises at least 2. Many people find that 3 or 4 alcohol-free days a week is a realistic place to start, often on weeknights. Choose specific days in advance and plan something you enjoy for those evenings, so the gap does not feel empty. Check the current official guidance where you live.

Is it okay to stop drinking suddenly?

For most moderate drinkers, yes. But if you drink heavily every day, drink in the morning, or get shaking, sweating, anxiety, nausea or poor sleep when you cut back, do not stop suddenly. Alcohol withdrawal can cause seizures and confusion. See your doctor first, who can help you reduce safely or arrange supervised withdrawal.

How do I stop drinking every night?

Pick two or three weeknights to go alcohol-free and replace the ritual, not just the drink. Have an appealing alcohol-free drink in your usual glass, move alcohol out of sight, eat earlier and plan an activity for the time you usually pour. When an urge comes, wait 15 minutes and ask yourself if you are hungry, angry, lonely or tired.

What can I drink instead of alcohol?

Sparkling water with lime and mint, ginger and lime with soda, iced tea with tonic, a cucumber cooler or a hot spiced apple drink on winter nights all work well. Serve them in a nice glass with ice. Alcohol-free beers, wines and spirit alternatives can help too, but many contain up to about 0.5% alcohol, so read labels.

When should I get help for my drinking?

Get help if you often drink more than you planned, feel unable to cut back, need a drink to feel normal, have withdrawal symptoms, or if drinking is affecting your health, work, relationships or mood. A doctor, nurse or pharmacist is a good first step. Free confidential helplines, counseling, peer support groups and medicines a doctor can discuss are all options.

Your first step today is simple: start a one-week drink diary and pour your usual drink into a measuring jug to see its real size. Once you know your numbers, choose your goal and your alcohol-free days. If you want a day-by-day structure, Drink Less Made Simple includes a 30-day drink less challenge.
