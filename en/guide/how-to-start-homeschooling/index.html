How to Start Homeschooling: A Calm Step-by-Step Plan for Beginners

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe school has stopped working for your child, or maybe you have wondered about teaching at home for years and now feel ready to look properly. To start homeschooling, you check the rules where you live, withdraw from school in writing if your child is enrolled, choose a simple approach, set a gentle daily rhythm and launch with a short trial term rather than a full timetable on day one.

This guide takes you through each step in order, with sample schedules, a style chooser, a budget checklist and a first-term plan you can copy. It is general information, not legal advice, so always confirm the details with your local education office.

Decide if homeschooling fits your family

Before any paperwork, take an honest look at your week. Homeschooling takes time, patience and some money, and it works best when the adults in the house broadly agree. Ask your children what they think too. Their worries, often about friends, are easier to plan for when you hear them early.

You do not have to decide forever. Many families set a trial period of one term or one school year, then review. Before you start, write down three signs that things are going well (a calmer child, steady learning, a parent who is coping) and two signs that you need to rethink. Put a review date in your calendar.

Returning to school is usually possible later. How easy that is depends on local rules and school places, so ask about it now if your child is leaving a school with long waiting lists.

Check the homeschooling laws where you live

Homeschooling rules are set locally and they change, so a friend's experience or an old forum post is not enough. Go to your official source first, then use a local homeschool support group as a second opinion.

CountryThe general picture
United StatesLegal in all 50 states; some need no notice, others ask for notification, set subjects, records or testing
EnglandCalled elective home education; you usually write to the school to deregister, and the local authority may make informal enquiries
Scotland, Wales, Northern IrelandDifferent rules; in Scotland you usually need council consent to leave a state school
CanadaSet by each province and territory
AustraliaSet by each state and territory; registration is generally required
New ZealandAn exemption from enrolment is generally needed

Questions to ask your education office

  • Do I need to tell anyone, register or apply for an exemption, and when?
  • Are there required subjects, hours or days?
  • Must I keep attendance records, work samples or a portfolio?
  • Are there assessments, tests or annual reviews?
  • What happens to any support plan, therapy or funding if my child leaves school?
  • What is the process if we want to return to school later?

Write each answer down with the date and the source. That one page will save you hours of second guessing.

How to withdraw your child from school

If your child is enrolled, follow the official withdrawal process. In some places that is a simple letter to the school, in others you notify or apply to an education office, and in a few you need permission. Never just stop sending your child, because unexplained absence can be treated as truancy.

  1. Find the correct process from your official source.
  2. Complete it in writing, keep a copy and note the date.
  3. If staff ask to meet, listen politely, take notes and say you will follow the official process.
  4. If you can, leave at a natural break, such as the end of a term or semester.

If your child is distressed, their wellbeing comes first, and you can leave mid-term using the proper procedure. If your child has special educational needs or a disability, ask in writing what happens to their support plan, assessments and services before you deregister, as some are tied to school enrollment.

Coming out of school after a hard time? Many families take a few weeks of gentle deschooling, with reading, cooking, outdoor time and games, before formal lessons. Make sure it still meets your local legal duties. If your child shows persistent low mood, panic attacks, sleep or eating changes, or any talk of self-harm, contact your doctor (GP) promptly, and call your emergency number if they are in immediate danger.

Choose a homeschooling style that suits you

There is no single right way to homeschool, and most experienced families end up mixing approaches. Pick one as a base, borrow from others and test it for a few weeks before buying much.

We have several children and like themesUnit studies

We have a young child who loves hands-on workMontessori-inspired
My child is driven by deep interestsUnschooling or child-led
We want to pick and mixEclectic

A split that works for many beginners is a structured, step-by-step program for math (maths) and early reading, which build in sequence, and a more relaxed, story-based approach for history, science and the arts. Siblings may need different methods, and that is fine.

Plan your first homeschool year

Start with goals, not lessons. For each child, write two or three goals for the year in plain words, such as reads chapter books independently or knows times tables up to 10. Then list your core subjects (reading, writing, math) and a few extras like science, history, art, music, a language and physical activity.

Plan in terms or semesters rather than mapping every day of the year. A one-page sketch with your term dates, breaks, main topics and review dates is enough. Many families like a four-day teaching week, keeping the fifth day for a co-op, outings or catching up.

Leave room for life. Illness, visitors, house moves and great days out will happen, and a plan with slack in it survives them.

What a homeschool day looks like

Home lessons can be far shorter than school lessons because there is no lining up or waiting for thirty classmates. A useful guide to focus spans:

  • Young children: about 10 to 15 minutes on one task
  • Primary (elementary) ages: about 15 to 30 minutes
  • Teens: 40 minutes or more

Total formal learning time is often surprisingly small. A five-year-old might do 30 to 60 minutes of structured work a day, a nine-year-old two to three hours and a teen three to five hours, with reading, play, projects and outings on top. Check whether your area sets minimum hours or days.

Sample day for primary ages

WhenWhat
Morning startBreakfast, one chore, a read-aloud or song
First blockMath (maths), 20 to 30 minutes
Second blockReading and writing, 30 to 40 minutes
SnackBreak and outdoor time
Third blockRead-aloud history or science
AfternoonProjects, art, music, clubs or trips

Many families find a rhythm (math after breakfast, reading after snack) easier to keep than strict clock times. Put the hardest subject first, stop while attention is still good and close the learning part of the day with a ten-minute tidy and a quick chat about what went well.

Keep a minimum day list on the fridge for hard weeks: a read-aloud, ten minutes of math practice and some free reading. One lost day makes almost no difference over a year.

Homeschool on a budget

Costs vary so much between families and approaches that any single figure would mislead you. The biggest savings come from habits rather than bargains.

  • Start with your public library, including any online services that come with your card.
  • Buy one subject at a time, and only after a trial.
  • Have children write answers in a notebook so workbooks can be reused by siblings.
  • Buy secondhand, swap with other families and share costly items like microscopes.
  • Use museum free days, parks and nature spots as regular classrooms.
  • Turn everyday life into lessons: cooking for fractions, shopping for budgeting, the garden for biology.

Map every free or low-cost resource within 30 minutes of home and aim for at least ten. If you have teens, plan and save early for exam fees, which usually fall on the family.

Socialization and record keeping basics

The socialization question comes up constantly, and it has a practical answer. Put at least one regular social anchor on the calendar from the start: a homeschool group or co-op, a sports team, a club, a class or volunteering. Weekly beats occasional, because friendships grow from seeing the same faces again and again. Homeschooled children often mix with a wide range of ages, which many parents count as a bonus.

Even where records are not required, keep simple ones. They help if your child goes back to school, if you move somewhere stricter or if questions are ever raised. A box or folder per child holding a reading log, a few dated work samples each month, attendance or learning days where needed, and a short note at the end of each term is plenty.

Your first term homeschool plan

The first term is about building habits and learning how your children learn at home, not covering as much as possible. Do not start every subject on day one.

WeeksFocusWhat it looks like
1 to 2Settling inShort lessons, reading, library, outdoors
3 to 4Building rhythmAdd one core subject every few days
5 to 6First checkAsk what works, change one or two things
7 to 10Steady progressFull rhythm, add extras
11 to 12Wrap upProjects, file work, celebrate, take a break

At each term review, look at progress toward each child's goals, what they enjoyed and found hard, whether your rhythm suits everyone, how you are honestly feeling and any legal deadlines coming up. Keep what works, drop what does not and try one new thing. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a teaching degree to homeschool?

In most places, no. Many states, provinces and countries do not require any teaching qualification for parents, although a few areas set conditions or ask for a plan of your approach. Check your local rules to be sure. What matters most in practice is patience, a simple routine and a willingness to say let's find out together when you do not know an answer.

How many hours a day should you homeschool?

Usually far fewer than a school day. A rough guide is 30 to 60 minutes of structured work for a five-year-old, two to three hours for a nine-year-old and three to five hours for a teen, with reading, play, projects and outings on top. Some areas set minimum hours or days, so check your local requirements and keep a simple attendance log if needed.

Can I homeschool while working full time?

Many families do, though it takes planning. Common solutions include clustering core lessons into the days a parent is home, sharing teaching with a partner or grandparent, using evenings or weekends for some lessons and building independent work blocks for practice. Start by mapping your real week honestly, then choose an approach that needs little daily preparation.

Can my child go back to school after homeschooling?

Yes, in most places children move between home education and school regularly. How easy it is depends on local rules and available places. Some areas let you apply like any other family, while others may have waiting lists or placement checks. Keeping simple records of what your child has covered makes any return much smoother.

How much does it cost to homeschool?

It ranges from very little to quite a lot, depending on your approach, the number of children and how much you buy new. Families who rely on the library, secondhand books, free local resources and everyday life as lessons can keep costs low. The bigger costs tend to be classes, trips, technology and exam fees for teens, so budget for those first.

What is the best homeschool curriculum for beginners?

There is no single best one. Most beginners do well with a structured program for math and early reading, plus library books, read-alouds and projects for everything else. Try one subject for a few weeks before buying a full package, look at free samples and ask local homeschoolers what they use. Adjust once you see how your child learns.

Your first step today is simple: find your official local guidance on home education and write down the answers to the first three questions on the checklist above, with the date and source. Once you know your obligations, everything else, from choosing a style to planning a gentle first term, becomes far easier to enjoy.
