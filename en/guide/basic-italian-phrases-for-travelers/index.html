Basic Italian Phrases for Travelers: What to Say and How to Say It

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Your trip to Italy is booked, and your Italian begins and ends with "ciao" and "pizza". Good news: you need far less than you think. Learn about a dozen survival phrases first (good morning, please, thank you, excuse me, I would like, how much, where is, I don't understand, the check), say them out loud with the right stress, and then add one situation a day, such as the coffee bar or the train station, for about 10 minutes.

This guide gives you the phrases travelers really use, each with a simple pronunciation guide where the capital letters show the stressed syllable. You will also learn the few sounds that trip English speakers up, how the coffee bar and the bill work, what to say on trains and in an emergency, and a short plan to practice before you fly.

The 12 Italian phrases every traveler needs

If you learn nothing else before your flight, learn these. They cover greetings, food, money, getting help and finding the restroom (the toilet, in the UK), and they work everywhere from Milan to Palermo.

Copy them onto a small card and keep it in your wallet or phone case.

How to pronounce Italian words

Italian is spelled almost exactly as it sounds, so a handful of rules lets you read menus, signs and station boards out loud. The big habit to build is to say every vowel cleanly, including the last one. Pizza is [PEET-tsah], not "peetz", and gelato ends with a clear o.

LettersSoundExample
a, e, i, o, uah, eh, ee, oh, oovino [VEE-noh]
ce, cich, cheecena [CHEH-nah], dinner
ge, gij, jeegelato [jeh-LAH-toh]
che, chikchiesa [KYEH-zah], church
ghe, ghig as in gospaghetti [spah-GEHT-tee]
gnny as in canyongnocchi [NYOHK-kee]
glilli as in millionfamiglia [fah-MEE-lyah]
sce, scishuscita [oo-SHEE-tah], exit

Most words are stressed on the second to last syllable: amico [ah-MEE-koh]. When the stress falls on the last syllable, Italian writes an accent, as in caffè [kahf-FEH] and città [cheet-TAH]. Double consonants are held a moment longer, so nonno (grandfather) sounds different from nono (ninth).

Polite greetings and when to use ciao

A greeting does more for you in Italy than any other phrase. Say buongiorno when you walk into a small shop, café or pharmacy, and say goodbye when you leave. Buongiorno covers the morning and early afternoon, and buonasera takes over from mid or late afternoon. There is no exact hour, so copy whatever people say to you.

EnglishItalianSay it
Hello (neutral, any time)Salve[SAHL-veh]
Hi, bye (informal)Ciao[CHOW]
Good night (going to bed)Buonanotte[bwoh-nah-NOHT-teh]
Have a nice dayBuona giornata[BWOH-nah johr-NAH-tah]
I'm sorryMi dispiace[mee dees-PYAH-cheh]
My name is...Mi chiamo...[mee KYAH-moh]
Nice to meet youPiacere[pyah-CHEH-reh]

Ciao is informal. Use it with friends, children and people your age who greet you with it first. With shop staff, hotel staff and older people, start with buongiorno or buonasera.

Prego is worth knowing in all its meanings. It is "you're welcome", but a waiter saying prego with a wave means "have a seat", a person holding a door means "after you", and a shop assistant saying "Prego?" is asking how they can help.

How to order coffee in Italy

In Italy a bar is mainly a café. Many people drink their coffee standing at the counter, which is quick and often cheaper than sitting at a table, where you may pay more for being served. In some busy bars you pay first at the till (la cassa) and hand the receipt to the barista. If you are not sure, ask "Pago prima?" [PAH-goh PREE-mah], meaning "Do I pay first?"

EnglishItalianSay it
An espressoUn caffè[oon kahf-FEH]
A cappuccinoUn cappuccino[oon kahp-poot-CHEE-noh]
Espresso with a little milk foamUn caffè macchiato[oon kahf-FEH mahk-KYAH-toh]
Hot milk with espressoUn latte macchiato[oon LAHT-teh mahk-KYAH-toh]
A decafUn caffè decaffeinato[oon kahf-FEH deh-kahf-feh-ee-NAH-toh]
A croissantUn cornetto[oon kohr-NEHT-toh]
Do not order "a latte" and expect coffee. Latte simply means milk, so you may be handed a glass of it. Ask for un latte macchiato or a cappuccino instead.

Un caffè always means a small, strong espresso. Many Italians drink cappuccino mostly in the morning, but nobody will refuse to make you one after lunch, so order what you enjoy. A full order sounds like this: "Buongiorno, un cappuccino e un cornetto, per favore."

Italian phrases for restaurants

Greet the staff, say how many you are and point to dishes as you name them, so a pronunciation slip never matters. Vorrei (I would like) and prendo (I'll have) are both perfectly polite ways to order.

EnglishItalianSay it
A table for two, pleaseUn tavolo per due, per favore[oon TAH-voh-loh pehr DOO-eh pehr fah-VOH-reh]
Still or sparkling water?Acqua naturale o frizzante?[AHK-kwah nah-too-RAH-leh oh freed-DZAHN-teh]
What do you recommend?Cosa mi consiglia?[KOH-zah mee kohn-SEE-lyah]
I'll have...Prendo...[PREHN-doh]
Is service included?Il servizio è incluso?[eel sehr-VEE-tsyoh EH een-KLOO-zoh]
Can we pay by card?Possiamo pagare con la carta?[pohs-SYAH-moh pah-GAH-reh kohn lah KAHR-tah]

A menu runs from antipasto (starter) to primo (pasta, risotto or soup), secondo (meat or fish), contorno (a side of vegetables, ordered separately) and dolce (dessert). You do not need every course. A primo and a contorno is a completely normal meal.

The bill usually will not arrive until you ask for it, which is courtesy, not slow service. You may see coperto, a cover charge per person, and sometimes servizio, a service charge. Tipping is modest and not obligatory; many people round up or leave a few coins, and customs vary, so ask your host what locals do.

Food allergy and gluten free phrases

Italian endings change with the speaker: a man says allergico and a woman says allergica. The same goes for celiaco and celiaca.

EnglishItalianSay it
I'm allergic to peanutsSono allergico/allergica alle arachidi[SOH-noh ahl-LEHR-jee-koh / ahl-LEHR-jee-kah AHL-leh ah-RAH-kee-dee]
...to tree nuts...alla frutta a guscio[AHL-lah FROOT-tah ah GOO-shoh]
Is there milk in this dish?C'è latte in questo piatto?[CHEH LAHT-teh een KWEH-stoh PYAHT-toh]
I'm celiacSono celiaco/celiaca[SOH-noh cheh-LEE-ah-koh / cheh-LEE-ah-kah]
Gluten freeSenza glutine[SEHN-tsah GLOO-tee-neh]
I'm vegetarianSono vegetariano/vegetariana[SOH-noh veh-jeh-tah-RYAH-noh / veh-jeh-tah-RYAH-nah]
It's a serious allergyÈ un'allergia grave[EH oo-nahl-lehr-JEE-ah GRAH-veh]

Celiac disease is widely understood in Italy, and many places offer dishes senza glutine. Ask clearly every time anyway, because cross contact in a busy kitchen is always possible. The pattern "C'è... in questo piatto?" works for any ingredient you need to avoid.

If your allergy is serious, write the key sentences in Italian on a small card and show it before you order. Carry any medicine your doctor has prescribed, and ask your doctor or pharmacist before you travel about anything you need to take with you.

Asking for directions and taking the train

The quickest way to ask the way is "Scusi, per...?" plus the place: "Scusi, per la stazione?" [SKOO-zee pehr lah stah-TSYOH-neh] means "Excuse me, which way to the station?" Italians use this shortcut all the time.

EnglishItalianSay it
On the right / On the leftA destra / A sinistra[ah DEH-strah / ah see-NEE-strah]
Straight aheadSempre dritto[SEHM-preh DREET-toh]
Is it far?È lontano?[EH lohn-TAH-noh]
A ticket to FlorenceUn biglietto per Firenze[oon bee-LYEHT-toh pehr fee-REHN-tseh]
One way (single) / Round trip (return)Solo andata / Andata e ritorno[SOH-loh ahn-DAH-tah / ahn-DAH-tah eh ree-TOHR-noh]
Which platform?Quale binario?[KWAH-leh bee-NAH-ryoh]
Is this the train for Naples?È questo il treno per Napoli?[EH KWEH-stoh eel TREH-noh pehr NAH-poh-lee]
Do I need to validate the ticket?Devo convalidare il biglietto?[DEH-voh kohn-vah-lee-DAH-reh eel bee-LYEHT-toh]

At the station, Partenze means departures and Arrivi means arrivals. Find your train number first, then watch for the binario (platform), which often appears only shortly before departure.

  • High speed trains usually need a seat reservation, which comes with the ticket. Check your car (carrozza) and seat (posto).
  • Some paper regional tickets must be stamped in a machine before you board. Rules change, so read the ticket or ask at the station.
  • City bus tickets are often bought before you board, at a tabaccheria with a big "T" sign, a newsstand or a machine.

What to say when you don't understand

Asking is easy. The answer usually comes fast, so these replies matter just as much as your questions.

EnglishItalianSay it
Can you repeat, please?Può ripetere, per favore?[PWOH ree-PEH-teh-reh pehr fah-VOH-reh]
More slowly, pleasePiù lentamente, per favore[PYOO lehn-tah-MEHN-teh pehr fah-VOH-reh]
I speak a little ItalianParlo un po' d'italiano[PAHR-loh oon POH dee-tah-LYAH-noh]
I'm learningSto imparando[stoh eem-pah-RAHN-doh]

Listen for key words instead of every word: destra, sinistra, dritto, qui (here), lì (there) and numbers. Then repeat back what you understood, such as "A destra, poi dritto?" (Right, then straight on?). People will nod or correct you, and either way you get the right answer.

"Sto imparando" said with a smile is a small secret weapon. Most people slow down, use simpler words and often teach you a new one.

Emergency Italian phrases and a practice plan

In an emergency, short and loud is best. The general emergency number in Italy, as across the European Union, is 112. Check the current numbers before you travel and save them in your phone.

EnglishItalianSay it
Help!Aiuto![ah-YOO-toh]
Call an ambulance!Chiami un'ambulanza![KYAH-mee oon ahm-boo-LAHN-tsah]
I need a doctorHo bisogno di un medico[oh bee-ZOH-nyoh dee oon MEH-dee-koh]
It hurts hereMi fa male qui[mee fah MAH-leh kwee]
My wallet was stolenMi hanno rubato il portafoglio[mee AHN-noh roo-BAH-toh eel pohr-tah-FOH-lyoh]

For minor problems, a pharmacy (farmacia, with a green cross) is a good first stop: "Ha qualcosa per il mal di testa?" asks for something for a headache. Buy travel insurance that covers medical care, and keep a photo of your passport separate from the original.

To get ready, spend 10 minutes a day speaking out loud, over about three weeks:

  1. Week 1: sounds and stress, greetings, numbers 0 to 100, prices and question words.
  2. Week 2: the coffee bar, ordering a meal, allergies and the bill, trains, buses and directions, checking in.
  3. Week 3: shopping, small talk, sightseeing, the pharmacy and emergencies, then rehearse a full first day in Italy.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important Italian phrases for tourists?

Start with buongiorno (good morning), buonasera (good evening), per favore (please), grazie (thank you), scusi (excuse me), vorrei (I would like), quanto costa? (how much is it?), dov'è il bagno? (where is the restroom?), non capisco (I don't understand) and il conto, per favore (the check, please). These cover most of a normal travel day, and people respond warmly when you try.

Is it rude to say ciao in Italy?

It is not rude, but it is informal. Ciao is for friends, children and people your age who use it with you first. With shopkeepers, hotel staff, waiters and older people, say buongiorno or buonasera instead, and arrivederci when you leave. If you are unsure, salve is a polite, neutral hello that works at any time of day.

How do you order a coffee in Italian?

Say "Un caffè, per favore" [oon kahf-FEH pehr fah-VOH-reh] for an espresso, or "Un cappuccino, per favore" for a cappuccino. If you want a milky coffee, ask for un latte macchiato, because latte on its own means milk. In some bars you pay at the till first and hand the receipt to the barista, so ask "Pago prima?" if you are not sure.

Do you tip in restaurants in Italy?

Tipping in Italy is modest and not obligatory. Many people round up the bill or leave a few coins for good service, and habits vary between a quick pizza and a formal dinner. Check the bill first for coperto (a cover charge) and servizio (a service charge). If you are unsure, ask your hotel or host what locals usually do.

How long does it take to learn basic Italian for travel?

With about 10 minutes a day of saying phrases out loud, most people can greet, order food and coffee, buy tickets and ask for directions within two to three weeks. You will not be fluent, but you will handle common situations politely and catch key words in replies, such as numbers and directions. Short daily practice works better than one long session.

What is the emergency number in Italy?

The general emergency number in Italy is 112, the same as across the European Union. Calls are free and work from any phone. Say "Parla inglese?" to ask for English, and give your location by naming a street, square or landmark you can see. Numbers can change, so check the current ones before you travel and save them in your phone.

Your first step today: read the 12 survival phrases out loud right now, slowly, leaning on the capital letters, then copy them onto a card for your bag. Ten minutes a day from here will have you greeting, ordering your coffee and buying train tickets with a smile by the time you land.
