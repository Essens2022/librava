What to Do in Retirement: A Simple Plan for Full, Happy Days

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have recently retired and find yourself standing in the kitchen at ten in the morning wondering what the day is for, you are in good company. The short answer to what to do in retirement is this: build a loose weekly rhythm with 3 to 5 regular anchors, then fill the space around them with movement, people, something to learn and something that helps others.

This guide walks you through that plan step by step. You will find why the first year can feel strange, how to design a week you enjoy, ways to find purpose without a paycheck (a pay packet, in the UK), how to make friends later in life, and a list of ideas to try this month.

Why retirement can feel strange at first

Many people start with a honeymoon phase. You sleep in, take a walk at two on a weekday afternoon and finally read the books piled by the bed. Enjoy it. Your body and mind may be catching up on years of rushing.

The honeymoon runs on contrast, though. Doing nothing feels wonderful when it is a break from doing a lot, and it loses its flavor when there is nothing left to take a break from. Many people then hit an identity dip: restlessness, irritability or a flat feeling once the job title, the colleagues and the daily structure are gone.

This is a normal stage, not a sign that you retired too early. Work gave you a role, a reason to get dressed properly and people who expected you. When those vanish at once, it takes time for the rest of your life to catch up.

Start with who you are now

Before you fill the calendar, spend an hour on paper. Write down three things you will happily never do again and three things you genuinely miss about work. People often find they miss the team, the sense of being useful or the feeling of being good at something far more than the job itself.

Those answers are your best clues. If you miss teamwork, look for group activities. If you miss solving problems, look for a project or skills-based volunteering. If you miss learning, sign up for a class.

Then rate health, friendships, purpose, fun and learning from 1 to 10. Start with the lowest score.

Try writing a new answer to the question people always ask, "What do you do?" Something like "I coach a youth soccer team, I'm learning Spanish and I garden" is far more fun to say than "I used to be..."

How to structure your days in retirement

Think rhythm, not routine. A rhythm is a pattern you choose that repeats often enough to feel familiar but loosely enough to change without guilt. It saves you from deciding everything from scratch every morning, which is surprisingly tiring.

The backbone of a good rhythm is a set of weekly anchors. An anchor happens on a predictable day, gets you out of the house or at least out of your chair, and ideally involves someone who would notice if you did not show up. Most people do well with 3 to 5 a week. Fewer, and the days blur. Many more, and you have rebuilt a busy working week without the pay.

Here is a sample week to borrow from and change:

DayMorningAfternoonEvening
MondayWalk, then bills and adminErrandsReading
TuesdayStrength or balance classHobby timeCall family
WednesdayVolunteer shiftRest or gardenCook something new
ThursdayLong walk with a friendLunch outFree
FridayLibrary or a courseProject timeDinner with friends
SaturdayFamily or a local eventDay tripGames or a film
SundaySlow breakfast, faith or naturePlan the weekEarly night

Notice that "free" is written in on purpose. Open time feels better when you know you chose it.

Build yours in four steps

  1. Write in fixed commitments first, such as appointments, family care or part-time work.
  2. Add 2 or 3 anchors linked to what you miss and value.
  3. Add movement on most days and social contact several times a week.
  4. Block at least one afternoon or evening as completely free, and protect it.

Live with it for 3 or 4 weeks, then keep what gave you energy and drop what drained it.

Find purpose in retirement without a paycheck

Purpose does not have to be grand. For some people it is mentoring a young person in their old field. For others it is being the neighbor who checks on the elderly couple next door, keeping a community garden alive or writing down family stories for the grandchildren.

A simple way to find your own: on one page, answer three questions. What breaks your heart or makes you angry in your community? What are people always asking you to help with? What did you love doing before work and family took over? The overlap often points somewhere useful.

Most structured activity after work falls into four paths, and many people mix two:

  • Volunteering. Hands-on, people-facing, skills-based or leadership roles at schools, libraries, shelters, food banks, parks and local charities.
  • Part-time work. Your old field with lighter hours, or something new such as seasonal, shop, school or outdoor work.
  • Consulting or freelancing, selling your expertise by the project.
  • An encore career in a new, often purpose-driven field.

Try two very different volunteer roles for about a month before committing. After each session, rate your energy from 1 to 10. And go slowly: saying yes to every committee in the first few weeks is the fastest way to feel busier and less happy than you did at work.

How to make friends after you retire

Work handed you daily company without any effort. Once it ends, friendships can fade simply because nobody is arranging them anymore. Staying connected is one of the best things you can do for your mood and wellbeing, so treat it as part of the plan rather than a nice extra.

Start with the people you already have. Pick two friends or former colleagues you have lost touch with and send a short, warm message this week. You might say, "I was thinking about our lunches at the old office. Fancy a coffee next week?" Most people are glad to hear from you and were waiting for someone else to go first.

To make new friends, go where the same people gather regularly: a walking group, a choir, a class, a club, a volunteer shift. Friendship grows from repeated, easy contact, so a weekly group beats a one-off event. After a few weeks, take one small step from acquaintance to friend by inviting someone for coffee after the session.

Living with your partner all day

Retirement happens to a household. A partner who has had quiet weekday mornings for years may suddenly share every room, errand and lunch, and that can strain even happy couples.

Talk about expectations early. Each of you could describe your ideal ordinary Tuesday, then compare. You may find one of you pictures long lunches together while the other pictures a quiet morning alone.

  • Agree on some time alone each day, such as one partner walking while the other enjoys the house.
  • Have a mix of shared anchors and separate ones, so you have things to tell each other over dinner.
  • Look again at who does which chores now that both of you are home.
  • Have calm money conversations at a set time, not in the middle of a disagreement.

Stay active and keep your mind sharp

Movement deserves a place in your week because it supports the things you want to keep doing: carrying the shopping, playing on the floor with grandchildren, walking a coastal path. A balanced week usually includes three kinds of activity.

  • Walking most days, starting where you are and building up gently.
  • Strength two or more days a week, such as sit-to-stand from a chair, wall push-ups or a class.
  • Balance practice, such as standing on one leg while holding the kitchen counter, plus gentle stretching.

For your mind, learn something properly new rather than only doing puzzles. A language, an instrument, a craft or a course challenges you in ways a crossword does not. Conversation and good sleep matter just as much, so a regular bedtime and an evening wind down are worth keeping.

Talk to your doctor (GP, in the UK) before starting a new exercise plan if you have a heart condition, joint problems, diabetes or any other health concern. Also see your doctor if low mood, loneliness, poor sleep or loss of interest lasts more than a couple of weeks or affects daily life. Asking early is sensible, not a weakness.

20 things to do in retirement

Circle the ones that spark something and try just one this month.

  1. Join a walking group.
  2. Grow herbs or vegetables, even in pots.
  3. Read with children at a local school.
  4. Take a beginner art or watercolor class.
  5. Write the story of your childhood home.
  6. Learn an instrument you always wanted to play.
  7. Sing in a community choir.
  8. Mentor someone starting out in your old field.
  9. Try a gentle yoga or stretching class.
  10. Plan an off-season trip somewhere new.
  11. Take one grandchild at a time on a small adventure.
  12. Host a monthly potluck for neighbors.
  13. Start or join a book club.
  14. Learn to cook dishes from another country.
  15. Take one photo a day for a month.
  16. Record family stories with an older relative.
  17. Volunteer at an animal shelter.
  18. Learn chess, bridge or another strategy game.
  19. Explore your own town as a tourist would.
  20. Start a coffee group with former colleagues.

Keep money calm and avoid retirement scams

Money often feels different once the regular salary stops, even when the numbers are fine. A calm picture starts with a one-page income map listing every source in general terms, such as workplace or private pensions, Social Security or the State Pension, savings and any part-time earnings, with roughly when each arrives.

Next, sketch a simple monthly spending plan covering essentials, fun and giving. Add small sinking funds for costs that tend to ambush retirees, like car repairs, home maintenance, dental work and gifts, and keep an emergency fund. This is general information, not personal financial advice, so for bigger decisions talk to a qualified, fee-transparent adviser.

Retirees are a common target for phone, email, romance and investment scams. Scammers are friendly and persuasive, and anyone can be fooled. A few firm rules help:

  • Never give personal or bank details to an unexpected caller, even one who sounds official.
  • Hang up and call back on a number you find yourself.
  • Be wary of any pressure to act today, secrecy or unusual ways to pay.
  • Talk it over with someone you trust before moving money.

Frequently asked questions

What do most people do when they first retire?

Many people rest, travel or catch up on jobs around the home for the first few weeks or months. That honeymoon phase is worth enjoying. After it fades, the people who settle best tend to add a few regular weekly commitments, such as a class, a volunteer shift or a standing walk with a friend, and keep the rest of the week open. Small experiments work better than one big plan.

Is it normal to feel depressed after retirement?

Feeling flat, restless or a little lost after retiring is common, because you lose a role, daily structure and workplace company all at once. It usually eases as you build a new rhythm and new connections. If low mood, poor sleep or loss of interest lasts more than a couple of weeks or affects daily life, talk to your doctor and reach out to someone you trust.

How do I stop being bored in retirement?

Boredom usually comes from too little structure and too little contact with people. Choose 3 to 5 weekly anchors that involve others, such as a group walk, a class or a volunteer role, and add one thing you are learning from scratch. Then try a new activity each month for a short trial. Boredom tends to fade once your week has a shape and something to look forward to.

How long does it take to adjust to retirement?

There is no set timeline. Some people feel at home in their new life within a few months, while others take a year or more, and it is common to loop back through adjustment after a move, a health change or a partner retiring. Writing a few lines each evening about what felt good helps you spot what works and speeds things up.

What can I do in retirement to earn extra money?

Common options include part-time work in your old field, seasonal or shop work, tutoring, pet care, consulting on a project basis or turning a hobby into a small side business. Before you start, check how earnings might affect your pension, Social Security in the US or the State Pension in the UK, and your tax, since rules vary. A qualified adviser or the relevant government agency can explain your situation.

How do retirees make new friends?

The most reliable route is joining something that meets regularly, such as a walking group, choir, class, club or volunteer team, because friendship grows from repeated easy contact. After a few sessions, invite one person for coffee. Also reach out to former colleagues and old friends with a short, warm message. Aim for at least one social plan every week.

Your first step today is small: choose one weekly anchor, such as a walk with a friend, a class or a trial volunteer shift, and write it on your calendar (your diary, in the UK) for a specific day.
