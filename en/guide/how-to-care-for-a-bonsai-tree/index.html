How to Care for a Bonsai Tree: A Simple Plan for Beginners

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe someone gave you a little tree in a ceramic pot with a sticker that said indoor bonsai, and you have a quiet feeling you are about to kill it. The short answer: find out whether your tree is an indoor or outdoor species, give it the brightest spot that suits it, water only when the top of the soil starts to dry (then soak it until water runs out of the holes), and keep it in gritty, free-draining soil.

A bonsai is not a special dwarf plant. It is an ordinary tree or shrub kept small by pruning and a shallow pot, and it needs what any tree needs, just with less room for error. Most bonsai deaths come from three things: the wrong place, the wrong watering and the wrong soil. Fix those and you are ahead of most owners.

Find out if your bonsai is indoor or outdoor

This is the single biggest decision, and gift shop labels often get it wrong. Most classic bonsai trees, such as juniper, pine, Japanese maple, larch, beech and hawthorn, are hardy outdoor trees. They need sun, rain, wind and a cold winter rest. Kept indoors they decline slowly for months, then die, often just after you decide they look fine.

A smaller group of tropical and subtropical trees tolerate indoor life in bright light. Use this quick check:

What you seeLikely treeWhere it lives
Short needles or prickly, scale-like spraysJuniperOutdoors all year
Small glossy oval leaves, thick pale trunkFicus (fig)Indoors, or outside in warm weather
Small toothed leaves, flaky barkChinese elmIndoors or outdoors
Round fleshy leaves on red stemsDwarf jade (Portulacaria)Indoors, or outside in warm weather
Leaves arranged in a ring like an umbrellaDwarf umbrella tree (Schefflera)Indoors

The juniper sold as an indoor bonsai is the most common beginner loss of all. If yours is a juniper, move it outside today to a bright spot sheltered from hard frost. If you cannot identify your tree, take a clear photo of a leaf and the trunk to a local nursery or bonsai club.

Give your bonsai enough light

Light comes before everything else, because leaves need it to make the energy that feeds the whole tree, roots included. A tree in poor light grows long, weak shoots with big pale leaves and becomes easy prey for pests.

Indoor trees

  • Put the tree right on the brightest windowsill, not across the room. In the Northern Hemisphere that is usually a south-facing window; in Australia and New Zealand, a north-facing one.
  • Keep it away from radiators, heaters and air vents, and never on a sill above a radiator.
  • If your windows are small or shaded, add a full-spectrum LED grow light about 6 to 12 inches (15 to 30 cm) above the leaves, on a timer for about 12 to 14 hours a day.
  • Turn the pot a quarter turn every week or two so it grows evenly.

Outdoor trees

Junipers, pines and Chinese elm grown outside want at least 5 or 6 hours of direct sun in the growing season. Japanese maples prefer morning sun and afternoon shade. Raise trees on a bench or shelf at about waist height, away from slugs and with good air flow, and shelter them from strong wind, which can dry a small pot in hours.

How often to water a bonsai

There is no correct schedule, and watering by the calendar is how most bonsai die. How fast a pot dries depends on weather, season, pot size, soil and how actively the tree is growing. A tree that needs water every three days in a cool spring might need it twice a day in a summer heatwave, and an outdoor tree in winter may need it only every week or two.

The rule is to water when the top of the soil just starts to dry, then water thoroughly. Here is how to check:

  1. Look. Many bonsai soils turn paler as they dry.
  2. Touch. If the top quarter inch (about half a centimeter) feels dry and loose, it is time.
  3. Use a chopstick. Push a plain wooden chopstick halfway into the soil near the edge of the pot and leave it there. If it comes out dark and damp, wait. If it is dry, water.
  4. Lift. A watered pot is noticeably heavier than a dry one, and after a few weeks you can tell by weight.

When you water, use a fine rose or gentle spray and water the whole surface until it runs freely from the drainage holes. Wait a minute and do it again, so any dry patches soak up properly. If a pot has dried out completely, set it in a bowl of water just over the soil line until the bubbles stop, then lift it out to drain.

Little sips do more harm than good. A splash wets only the top layer while the roots below stay dry. Always water until it drains, then let the surface start to dry before the next time.

Use the right bonsai soil and pot

Ordinary potting compost or garden soil stays soggy in a shallow pot and suffocates the roots. Bonsai need a free-draining mix of hard particles about 1/8 to 1/4 inch (3 to 6 mm) across, sieved to remove dust. Common ingredients are akadama (a baked clay), pumice, lava rock, calcined clay and fine bark.

  • Most outdoor deciduous trees and conifers: roughly equal parts akadama, pumice and lava rock. Junipers and pines like a little more pumice and lava.
  • Indoor tropical trees: equal parts akadama, pumice and fine bark, or calcined clay, pumice and bark.
  • Dwarf jade: a grittier, faster-draining mix with extra pumice or lava.

If akadama is hard to find, a mix of pumice, lava or calcined clay with a little bark grows good trees too. Whatever pot you use, it must have drainage holes, covered with a small square of mesh to keep the soil in.

Feed your bonsai in the growing season

A bonsai lives in a small amount of gritty soil that gets flushed through every time you water, so it runs short of nutrients. A balanced fertilizer (roughly equal N, P and K on the label) suits most trees. A general houseplant or garden fertilizer at half strength works perfectly well; you do not need a special bonsai product.

SeasonOutdoor treesIndoor tropical trees
Early springStart as buds open, unless just repottedResume regular feeding as light improves
Late spring and summerLiquid feed every 1 to 2 weeks, or solid feed monthlyLiquid feed every 1 to 2 weeks
Fall (autumn)Lighter, low nitrogenReduce slightly
WinterStop while dormantMonthly at half strength if growing, or pause

In Australia and New Zealand, shift the months by six. Always water a dry tree before feeding, never feed for about 3 to 4 weeks after repotting, and do not feed a sick tree hoping to revive it. Fix the cause first.

Prune to keep your bonsai small and healthy

There are two kinds of pruning. Maintenance pinching is light and regular, and keeps the shape tidy. Structural pruning removes larger branches occasionally to set the design. Beginners only need the first kind for now. Cutting a shoot tip makes the buds below it grow, which builds fine twigs over time.

  • Junipers: let shoots extend, then trim the longest ones with sharp scissors just above a fork. Do not pinch with fingers or shear flat like a hedge.
  • Pines: in late spring, pinch the strongest new candles back by about half to two-thirds.
  • Broadleaf trees such as maples and elms: let shoots grow four to six pairs of leaves, then cut back to one or two pairs.
  • Tropical trees such as ficus: let shoots reach six to eight leaves, then cut back to two or three.

Never take more than the tree can recover from in one year, and avoid heavy pruning on a weak tree.

Wear gloves when pruning junipers and ficus, whose sap and foliage can irritate skin. Some species are toxic if chewed, including ficus sap for cats and dogs, jade plant, azalea and yew. Keep trees and trimmings out of reach of pets and children, and call your vet if a pet chews one.

When and how to repot a bonsai

Repotting refreshes broken-down soil and gives roots new room. Young trees usually need it every 2 to 3 years and older trees every 3 to 5 years or more, mostly in early spring as the buds swell. Repot when water no longer soaks in, roots circle and fill the pot, or the soil has turned to mush, not just because you dislike the pot.

  1. Prepare a pot with mesh over the holes and tie-in wires threaded through.
  2. Ease the tree out and comb the roots out gently with a root hook or chopstick.
  3. Trim no more than about a quarter to a third of the roots, and less on a weak tree.
  4. Set the tree in place, tie it in firmly so it cannot rock, and work fresh soil in with a chopstick to remove air pockets.
  5. Water until the water runs clear.

Afterward, keep the tree out of strong sun, wind and frost for a few weeks, and wait 3 to 4 weeks before feeding.

Why your bonsai leaves are turning yellow or brown

Most problems come from light, water, soil or a recent move, so ask what has changed before you reach for a spray.

Gentle fixes deal with most pests. If you do use a pesticide, choose one approved for ornamental plants where you live and follow the label exactly. A quick close look at the leaves, trunk and soil once a month catches most trouble early.

Bonsai care through the seasons

Read the season by what the tree is doing, not by the date. Spring is for repotting as buds swell and for the first feeds. Summer is for trimming new growth and watching the watering closely, sometimes twice a day. Fall (autumn) means lighter feeding and preparing hardy trees for the cold.

In winter, hardy outdoor trees stay outside to go dormant, but their roots are far less cold hardy in a pot than in the ground. Many need shelter below about 20°F (-7°C) or in long freezes. An unheated shed or garage with a window, a cold frame, or pots buried to the rim in mulch against a sheltered wall all work. Do not bring them into a heated house. Check the soil every week or two, since dormant trees still must not dry out.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I water my bonsai?

Water whenever the top of the soil just starts to dry, not on a fixed schedule. That might be every few days in cool weather and once or twice a day in summer heat for a small pot. Check with your finger or a wooden chopstick pushed into the soil. When you water, soak the whole pot until water runs from the drainage holes, then repeat once.

Can a bonsai tree live indoors?

Only certain species. Ficus, Chinese elm, dwarf jade (Portulacaria) and dwarf umbrella tree (Schefflera) tolerate indoor life if they get the brightest window or a grow light and stay away from heaters. Junipers, pines, maples and most other classic bonsai are outdoor trees that need a cold winter rest, and they slowly die if kept inside.

Why is my bonsai dying?

The usual causes are the wrong place, the wrong watering or the wrong soil. An outdoor tree such as a juniper kept indoors, a tree too far from the window, watering on a schedule, and dense compost that stays wet account for most losses. Check the tree type, move it to better light, test the soil before watering, and repot into gritty mix in spring if needed.

Why are my bonsai leaves turning yellow?

Yellow leaves with wet soil usually mean overwatering or roots sitting in dense soil. Pale new leaves with green veins can mean the tree is short of nutrients, which half-strength feeding in the growing season fixes. A few yellow leaves after a move or in fall can be normal. Check the soil moisture with a chopstick before changing anything else.

Should I mist my bonsai?

Misting is fine and helps keep dust off the leaves, but the effect lasts only minutes, so it does not replace proper watering. For indoor tropical trees, a humidity tray works better: a shallow tray of pebbles with water just below the top of the stones, with the pot standing on the stones, not in the water. Keeping trees away from heaters helps most.

When should I repot my bonsai?

Most bonsai are repotted in early spring as the buds swell. Young trees usually need it every 2 to 3 years and older ones every 3 to 5 years or more. Repot when water no longer soaks in, roots fill the pot or the soil has broken down. Remove no more than a quarter to a third of the roots, and wait 3 to 4 weeks before feeding.

Your first step today is to identify your tree and put it in the right place: a juniper or other hardy tree goes outside, and an indoor species goes right onto your brightest windowsill. Then push a chopstick into the soil and use it to decide when to water for the next week.
