How to Get Rid of a Stiff Neck: Gentle Steps That Help Fast

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you woke up this morning unable to turn your head, or your neck has locked up after a long day at the screen, you are in very good company. The short answer is to keep it gently moving: small, comfortable turns, nods and tilts every hour or two, a wrapped heat pack for stiffness, a short walk, and pain relief if a pharmacist says it suits you. Most stiff necks start to ease within a few days.

This guide gives you a simple plan for the first three days, step by step stretches for when things calm down, tips for sleeping, working and driving, and the warning signs that mean you should get help straight away.

Why you woke up with a stiff neck

Most everyday neck pain is not caused by serious damage. Common triggers are staying in one position for a long time (a desk, a phone, a long drive), sleeping awkwardly, doing something unusual such as painting a ceiling, and stress, which makes the shoulder and neck muscles tighten up.

A stiff neck can feel alarming, especially when one direction hurts a lot. But hurt does not always mean harm. The muscles and joints have become sensitive and guarded, and they usually settle as you get them moving again. Scans are rarely needed for this kind of everyday neck pain.

It is also normal for neck pain to come and go. A stiff spell that returns months later is common and is not a sign that something is wearing out.

Check first. This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor (GP), physical therapist (UK: physiotherapist) or pharmacist before starting new exercises or medicines, and straight away if your symptoms are severe, new or unusual for you. Read the warning signs section below before you start.

Keep your neck gently moving

Holding your neck still feels sensible, but it tends to make stiffness worse. Gentle movement helps more, and neck collars are generally not advised for everyday neck pain. Start small. Sit in a supportive chair with your feet flat and your shoulders relaxed, and breathe normally.

  1. Small turns. Turn your head to the right only as far as feels comfortable, then back to the middle. Repeat to the left. Do 5 each way.
  2. Small nods. Lower your chin a little toward your chest, then lift it back to level. Do 5.
  3. Small tilts. Let one ear move a short way toward your shoulder, then return. Repeat on the other side. Do 5 each way.
  4. Shoulder rolls. Roll both shoulders slowly up, back and down. Do 5.

Repeat this little set every hour or two while you are awake. A kitchen timer is a handy nudge. If one direction is very painful, do the others and try that one again later with an even smaller movement. You may notice your range improves a little each time.

Mild stiffness or discomfort is fine. Stop if you feel sharp pain, pain spreading down your arm, numbness, tingling, dizziness or weakness.

Heat or ice for a stiff neck?

Both can help, and you can use whichever feels better. Heat is usually the favorite for stiffness: a wrapped hot water bottle or heat pack for about 15 to 20 minutes relaxes tight muscles. A warm shower in the morning does a similar job before you start moving.

A wrapped cold pack for about 10 to 15 minutes can be soothing if the area feels hot or sore after a strain. Some people alternate.

  • Never put heat or cold directly on your skin; always wrap it in a cloth.
  • Do not use it on numb skin, and never fall asleep with it in place.
  • Take extra care with diabetes, poor circulation or thin skin.

For pain relief, ask a pharmacist (chemist) or doctor which medicine is right for you, follow the label and do not exceed the dose. Anti-inflammatory painkillers are not suitable for everyone, for example if you have stomach ulcers, kidney or heart problems, asthma that worsens with them, take blood thinners or are pregnant. Acetaminophen (paracetamol) has a daily maximum and is in many combination products, so check labels.

A simple plan for the first three days

Having a rough shape for the day stops you freezing on the sofa or overdoing it out of frustration.

Time of dayWhat to do
On wakingRoll onto your side to get up, warm shower, small movement set
MorningLight tasks, movement set every hour or two
MiddayShort easy walk, wrapped heat or cold if helpful
AfternoonChange position often, avoid long spells looking down
EveningWarm pack, movement set, slow relaxed breathing
BedtimeBack or side sleeping with a supportive pillow

A short walk is worth adding even on day one. Let your arms swing and look ahead rather than down at the pavement (sidewalk). For now, avoid heavy lifting, long sessions hunched over a phone or laptop, and anything that involves holding your head in one awkward position. Each day, aim to do a little more of your normal routine than the day before.

Stiff neck stretches once it starts to ease

When the worst has calmed down, usually after a few days, move on to proper stretches. Go slowly, stop at a mild stretch, hold for about 20 to 30 seconds and repeat 2 or 3 times. No bouncing, and never pull your head with your hands.

  • Chin tucks. Sit tall and draw your chin straight back, as if making a double chin, keeping your eyes level. Hold 3 to 5 seconds and repeat 8 to 10 times.
  • Neck turns. Turn your head to look over one shoulder until you feel a gentle stretch, hold, then turn the other way.
  • Side bends. Keeping your nose pointing forward, tilt one ear toward your shoulder. Hold the chair seat with the opposite hand to keep that shoulder down.
  • Forward nod. Lower your chin toward your chest until you feel a mild stretch at the back of the neck.
  • Upper shoulder stretch. Hold the chair seat with your right hand and tilt your left ear toward your left shoulder. Repeat on the other side.
  • Doorway chest stretch. Place your forearms on a door frame at shoulder height and step one foot forward until you feel a stretch across your chest.

These make a good 5-minute morning routine. If you notice tingling down your arms or back when you nod forward, stop and get advice.

How to sleep with a stiff neck

Side or back sleeping is usually more comfortable than lying on your front, which twists your neck for hours. The aim is a pillow height that keeps your neck roughly in line with the rest of your spine.

  • Side sleepers usually need a little more height to fill the gap between the shoulder and the head.
  • Back sleepers usually need less, so the chin is not pushed toward the chest.
  • A small rolled towel tucked inside the pillowcase can give extra support under the neck.
  • To get up, roll onto your side, drop your legs over the edge and push up with your arms.

There is no single best pillow for everyone, so experiment with what you already have before buying anything. Poor sleep and stress both make pain and muscle tension feel worse, so a calm wind-down before bed is worth the effort.

Working, driving and screens with a stiff neck

Long spells in one position are a common trigger, so small changes at your desk can stop the stiffness coming back.

  • Put your screen about an arm's length away, with the top at or slightly below eye level. Raise a laptop and use a separate keyboard and mouse if you can.
  • Keep your shoulders relaxed and your elbows near 90 degrees, with feet flat on the floor or a footrest.
  • Stand up and move every 30 minutes or so.
  • Hold your phone higher, closer to eye level, and use both hands. Use the speaker or a headset rather than wedging the phone between ear and shoulder.
  • Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away for about 20 seconds.

When driving, adjust your seat and headrest, and only drive if you can turn your head enough to check safely around you. On long drives, take regular breaks.

Stiff neck warning signs that need urgent help

Call emergency services straight away (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia, 111 in New Zealand) for:

  • Chest pain, or pain or pressure spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back with sweating, breathlessness, nausea or lightheadedness. This could be the heart, even if it feels like a pulled muscle.
  • A sudden, severe headache, the worst you have ever had.
  • A stiff neck with fever, headache, a rash, dislike of bright light or vomiting.
  • Neck pain after a fall, car accident or other serious injury.
  • Sudden weakness, numbness or drooping on one side of the face or body, slurred speech, trouble swallowing, double vision or sudden dizziness.

See a doctor the same day (in the UK, NHS 111 can advise) for spreading or worsening numbness, tingling or weakness in the arms or hands, clumsy hands or new trouble walking, loss of bladder or bowel control, or severe constant pain that is worse at night.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a stiff neck last?

Most stiff necks start to feel noticeably better within a few days, and many settle completely within one to two weeks. Some take a few weeks. Progress is often uneven, with a good day followed by a stiffer morning. See a doctor or physical therapist if it is not improving after a few weeks, is getting worse or spreads into your arm.

Should I massage or crack a stiff neck?

Gentle self massage of the shoulders with your fingertips can feel soothing, but avoid trying to crack or click your own neck. It does not fix stiffness and can make sore tissues more irritated. Small, comfortable movements every hour or two, heat and a short walk are safer ways to loosen things up.

Is it better to rest a stiff neck or move it?

Moving it gently is usually better. Holding your neck still, or wearing a collar, tends to make it stiffer and slower to recover. Use small turns, nods and tilts within comfort, change position often and keep up light daily tasks. Avoid heavy lifting and long periods looking down for the first few days.

Can stress cause a stiff neck?

Yes, stress and poor sleep can make neck and shoulder muscles tighten and make pain feel worse. Try dropping your shoulders on a slow breath out, letting your jaw relax with your teeth apart, and taking short walks. If stress, worry or low mood lasts more than a couple of weeks, talk to your doctor.

When should I worry about a stiff neck?

Get emergency help for a stiff neck with fever, headache, a rash, dislike of bright light or vomiting, a sudden severe headache, neck pain after a serious injury, or any chest pain spreading to the arm or jaw. See a doctor the same day for spreading numbness, tingling or weakness in your arms, or trouble walking.

What is the best sleeping position for a stiff neck?

Lying on your back or side is usually most comfortable, with a pillow that keeps your neck roughly in line with your spine. Side sleepers often need a slightly higher pillow than back sleepers. Front sleeping twists the neck for hours, so try to avoid it while your neck is sore.

Your first step today is the small movement set: 5 gentle turns, nods and tilts each way plus 5 shoulder rolls, repeated every hour or two, with a short walk and a wrapped heat pack this evening.
