How to Start Running: A Gentle 10-Week Plan From Walking to 5K

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you can barely jog to the end of the street right now, you are exactly where most new runners start. The short answer: begin with three 30-minute run/walk sessions a week on non-consecutive days, running for just 1 minute and walking for 1.5 minutes at a pace slow enough to talk, and lengthen the running bits a little each week until you can run for 30 minutes or 5K (3.1 miles) in about 10 weeks.

This guide gives you the full week-by-week plan, how to find an easy pace, a 5-minute warm-up, what gear really matters, and how to tell normal soreness from pain that needs attention. Age, size and a lack of sporty history are not barriers. Patience is the main ingredient.

Can anyone start running?

Most adults can start running safely with a gentle run/walk approach, including people over 50 and people carrying extra weight. Run/walk is real running, not a lesser version. Plenty of experienced runners use walk breaks in long races on purpose.

A few myths stop people before they begin. You are not too old: beginners in their 60s and 70s complete run/walk plans every year, they simply may need repeat weeks. You do not need to lose weight first; the plan starts with short running bits so your joints adapt gradually. And for most healthy people, easy running at a sensible build-up is not the knee-wrecker it is made out to be.

What running does well: it builds fitness in your heart and lungs, can lift your mood and gives you a regular, simple routine. What it does not do is promise a particular body shape or weight change. Set a reason that is your own, such as playing with your kids without puffing or finishing a local 5K, and write it down.

Check with your doctor first if you have a heart, lung or joint condition, high blood pressure or diabetes, are pregnant or recently gave birth, take regular medication, have ever had chest pain, dizziness or fainting, or have been inactive for a long time with risk factors. During any run, stop and get urgent medical help (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, or your local emergency number) for chest pain or tightness, pain spreading to your arm, jaw or back, severe breathlessness that does not settle, fainting, or a racing or irregular heartbeat.

What you need to start running

Shoes are the one thing worth spending on. If you can, get fitted at a specialist running shop, where staff can watch you walk or jog and suggest a few pairs. Choose the pair that feels comfortable straight away, with about a thumb's width of space beyond your longest toe. Replace them roughly every 300 to 500 miles (500 to 800 km).

  • Moisture-wicking socks and clothes, since cotton holds sweat and rubs
  • A well-fitting sports bra if you need one
  • Bright or reflective clothing and a light for dark mornings and evenings
  • A watch or phone timer for the intervals

Where to run matters less than you think. Parks, quiet streets, a local track or a treadmill all work. Outdoors, run facing oncoming traffic where there is no sidewalk (pavement), tell someone your route, and keep one ear free of music near roads.

The 10-week beginner running plan

Do each week's session three times, with at least one rest day between sessions. Every session starts with a 5-minute brisk walk and ends with a 5-minute easy walk, both included in the total time.

1Run 1 min, walk 1.5 min, 8 times30 min
2Run 1.5 min, walk 1.5 min, 7 times31 min
3Run 2 min, walk 1.5 min, 6 times31 min
4Run 3 min, walk 2 min, 5 times35 min
5Run 4 min, walk 2 min, 4 times, then run 2 min36 min
6Run 5 min, walk 1.5 min, 4 times36 min
7Run 7 min, walk 2 min, 3 times37 min
8Run 10 min, walk 2, run 10 min, walk 2, run 5 min39 min
9Run 15 min, walk 2 min, run 10 min (third session: run 20, walk 2, run 5)37 min
10Session 1: run 20, walk 1, run 8. Session 2: run 25 min. Session 3: run 30 min or 5K35 to 40 min

The running parts are deliberately short at first. Your heart and lungs adapt within weeks, but tendons, bones and joints take longer, so a gentle build protects you from the shin and knee aches that make many beginners quit.

Repeat a week whenever you need to. If you could not finish the running intervals in two or more sessions, missed two sessions, are still sore when the next run comes round, or had a cold or poor sleep, do that week again. Some people take ten weeks and others take fourteen, and they reach the same finish line.

How to run without getting out of breath

New runners almost always go too fast. The fix is the talk test: while running, you should be able to speak in short sentences. If you could sing a song, you can speed up a touch. If you can only gasp a word or two, slow down.

At first your easy jog may be barely faster than a brisk walk, and that is fine. It still trains your heart and lungs, you finish every interval, and you recover in time for the next session. Your easy pace speeds up on its own over the weeks.

  • Stand tall and relaxed, looking ahead rather than at your feet.
  • Take short, quick steps and let your feet land roughly under your body, not far out in front.
  • Keep your arms bent loosely at about 90 degrees, swinging forward and back, not across your chest.
  • Breathe through your mouth and nose, whatever feels natural. Breathing out fully helps calm a rising panic.

For a side stitch, slow to a walk, breathe out fully, press your fingers into the sore spot and lean slightly forward. Avoid big meals in the two hours before running. Chest pain, or pain spreading to your arm, neck, jaw or back, is not a stitch: stop and get help.

A 5-minute warm-up and cool-down

Every session begins with brisk walking, as if you are late for a bus but not running for it. In the last minute or two, add a few easy moves:

MoveHow many
Brisk walk, swinging your arms3 to 5 minutes
Leg swings, holding a wall or tree10 each leg
Hip circles, hands on hips5 each direction
Ankle circles5 each direction, each foot
Walking knee lifts10 each leg
Heel walks, then toe walks10 steps each

Keep every movement smooth and comfortable, never jerky. Long, still stretches are better saved for after your run.

Finish each session with 5 minutes of easy walking until your breathing settles. Then, if you like, hold a few gentle stretches for about 20 to 30 seconds each: calves against a wall, the front of the thigh while holding a support, and the back of the thigh with your heel on a low step. You should feel a mild pull, never pain.

Strength exercises for beginner runners

Two short strength sessions a week on non-running days, or after an easy run, help your legs and hips cope with the extra load. About 20 minutes is enough. Go through this list once, rest a minute or two, then repeat.

  • Bodyweight squats, 10 to 15 (easier: sit down to a chair and stand up)
  • Lunges, 8 to 10 each leg, holding a wall if needed
  • Step-ups on a low step, 8 to 10 each leg, holding a rail
  • Calf raises, 12 to 15
  • Glute bridges, 10 to 15
  • Plank, 15 to 30 seconds (easier: on your knees or hands on a table)
  • Side plank, 10 to 20 seconds each side
  • Single-leg balance, 20 to 30 seconds each leg, fingertips on a wall

Move slowly and with control, and skip any exercise that causes sharp pain. A little muscle soreness for a day or two after the first sessions is normal.

Sore after running, or something more?

Normal training soreness is a dull, even ache in the muscles, often in both legs, that shows up a day or two after a new session, eases as you warm up and fades within 2 to 3 days.

Usually normal sorenessNeeds attention
Dull, general muscle acheSharp or stabbing pain
Both legs, spread outOne spot, one side
Eases as you warm upGets worse as you run
Gone in 2 to 3 daysLasts more than a few days
Walking normallyLimping or changing your stride

A simple 0 to 10 scale helps. A mild ache of 2 or 3 that stays the same or eases during a run and is gone by morning is often fine to run with at an easy pace. Anything that climbs during the run, goes above about 3 or 4, or is worse the next day means stop, rest and get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist if it does not settle.

Most beginner niggles, such as sore shins, achy knees and tight calves, come from doing too much too soon. Going slower, repeating weeks and keeping rest days between runs prevent most of them. As a rough guide, increase your total running time by no more than about 10 percent a week.

Fitting running into real life

The best time to run is the one you can keep. Many beginners find early mornings easiest because nothing else has crowded the day yet. Put your three sessions in your calendar like appointments, such as Monday, Wednesday and Saturday.

  • In heat, run early or late, slow down and drink to thirst before and after.
  • In cold, wear layers you can remove and take a little longer over the warm-up.
  • In the dark, wear bright or reflective clothing and a light, and choose well-lit routes.
  • On a treadmill, set a slight incline of about 1 percent and use the same plan.

Eat a light snack, such as a banana or toast, an hour or two before running if you are hungry, and a normal balanced meal afterward. Sleep well; it is when your body adapts. Keep a simple log of each session and how it felt. The book Running for Beginners Made Simple covers the whole plan in more depth, including race day for your first 5K.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a beginner run?

Three times a week is the sweet spot for most beginners, with at least one rest day between sessions. That gives your muscles, tendons and joints time to adapt while still building fitness steadily. On the days between, gentle walking, cycling, swimming or a short strength routine are good choices. Running every day as a new runner raises the risk of aches and burnout.

How long does it take to run 5K as a beginner?

With three run/walk sessions a week, many beginners can run for 30 minutes or 5K (3.1 miles) in about 10 weeks. Others take 12 to 14 weeks because they repeat a few weeks, which is completely normal. Finishing time at your first 5K often falls somewhere between 30 and 45 minutes, and walking breaks are perfectly fine.

Why do I get out of breath so fast when running?

Usually because you are running faster than your current fitness allows. Slow down until you can speak in short sentences, even if that feels like a shuffle. Short running intervals with walk breaks also help, because they let your breathing recover before it runs away from you. Your easy pace will naturally speed up over the weeks as your heart and lungs adapt.

Is it okay to walk during a run?

Yes. Walk breaks are a planned, sensible part of beginner training, not cheating. They let you cover more total time running with less strain, and they reduce the risk of aches. Many experienced runners also use walk breaks in long races. In a first 5K event, taking short walking breaks is common and completely accepted.

What should I eat before running in the morning?

For an easy 30-minute session, many people run comfortably on an empty stomach or after a small snack such as a banana, a slice of toast or a few crackers. Allow at least an hour after a light snack and about two hours after a big meal to avoid stitches. Drink some water when you wake up, and eat a normal balanced breakfast afterward.

Do I need special shoes to start running?

Proper running shoes are the one piece of gear worth buying. They cushion and support your feet in ways that fashion trainers or old sneakers often do not. A specialist running shop can watch you jog and suggest options. Pick the most comfortable pair, leave a thumb's width of space at the toe, and replace them every 300 to 500 miles (500 to 800 km).

Today, put three 30-minute slots in your calendar for this week on non-consecutive days, and lay out your shoes for the first one. Then walk briskly for 5 minutes, run gently for 1 minute, walk for 1.5 minutes, and repeat eight times. That first session is the hardest part of the whole plan.
