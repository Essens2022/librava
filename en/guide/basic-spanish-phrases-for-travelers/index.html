Basic Spanish Phrases for Travelers: The Ones You Will Use

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your trip to Spain or Latin America is coming up and your Spanish stops at "hola", you have more time than you think. The short answer: learn about a dozen survival phrases first (hello, please, thank you, excuse me, I don't understand, where is, how much, I would like, the check, help), say them out loud with the right stress, and add one situation a day, such as restaurants or transport, for 10 minutes.

This guide gives you the phrases travelers use most, each with a simple pronunciation guide where the capital letters show the stressed syllable. You will also find the key sounds, polite habits, words that change between Spain and Latin America, and a short practice plan.

The 12 Spanish phrases every traveler needs

If you learn nothing else, learn these. They cover greetings, help, food, money and finding the restroom (the toilet, in the UK), and they work in every Spanish-speaking country.

How to pronounce Spanish words correctly

Spanish is one of the most predictable languages to pronounce. Once you know a few rules, you can read almost any word aloud.

  • Vowels never change. a is "ah", e is "eh", i is "ee", o is "oh", u is "oo". Keep them short and crisp.
  • The h is always silent: hola is [OH-lah], hotel is [oh-TEL].
  • The j, and g before e or i, sound like a strong English h: jamón is [hah-MOHN].
  • ll usually sounds like the y in "yes": calle (street) is [KAH-yeh].
  • ñ sounds like "ny" in canyon: mañana is [mah-NYAH-nah].
  • v and b sound the same, like a soft English b: vino is [BEE-noh].

For stress, words ending in a vowel, n or s are stressed on the second-to-last syllable. Words ending in other consonants are stressed on the last syllable. A written accent mark overrides both rules, so café is [kah-FEH].

In most of Spain, c before e or i and the letter z sound like "th" in think, so gracias becomes [GRAH-thyahs]. In Latin America they sound like s. Both are correct, and the s version is understood everywhere. Do not worry about rolling your r perfectly; a firm tap is understood almost everywhere.

Polite greetings and when to use usted

Greeting people properly wins you more goodwill than any other phrase. Say hello when you walk into a small shop or café, and goodbye when you leave.

Good morningBuenos días[BWEH-nohs DEE-ahs]

Spanish has two ways to say "you". Use the polite usted with strangers, staff, police, shopkeepers and older people. Use the friendly tú with friends, children and people your age once things feel relaxed. When in doubt, choose usted; nobody is offended by too much respect, and in Spain people will often invite you to switch.

Spanish phrases for restaurants and ordering food

Ordering food is where most travelers want Spanish first. The polite request "Quisiera..." (I would like) works for almost anything on the menu.

A table for two, pleaseUna mesa para dos, por favor[OO-nah MEH-sah PAH-rah dohs, por fah-BOR]
The menu, pleaseLa carta, por favor[lah KAR-tah, por fah-BOR]

Can I pay by card?¿Puedo pagar con tarjeta?[PWEH-doh pah-GAR kohn tar-HEH-tah]

Men say alérgico and women say alérgica. If an allergy is serious, add "Es muy grave" (it's very serious) and consider carrying a written card in Spanish. In many restaurants the check does not arrive until you ask for it, which is good manners, not slow service.

Tipping (la propina) varies a lot between countries, cities and types of restaurant, and it changes over time. Check current local habits before you go, and look for "servicio incluido" on your bill so you do not tip twice.

Asking for directions and getting around

"¿Dónde está...?" [DOHN-deh es-TAH] means "Where is...?" and gets you almost anywhere: la estación (the station), la salida (the exit), la parada de taxis (the taxi stand or rank). Add "por favor" and you are done.

Is it far? Is it near?¿Está lejos? ¿Está cerca?[es-TAH LEH-hohs / es-TAH SEHR-kah]
To the rightA la derecha[ah lah deh-REH-chah]
To the leftA la izquierda[ah lah ees-KYEHR-dah]
Straight ahead (Spain)Todo recto[TOH-doh RREK-toh]
Straight ahead (Latin America)Derecho[deh-REH-choh]

Round trip (return)De ida y vuelta[deh EE-dah ee BWEL-tah]

Watch out for derecha (right) and derecho (straight on in Latin America). They sound alike, so if you are unsure, point and ask again. And when you want to say you are taking a bus, use tomar. The verb coger is normal in Spain but vulgar slang in much of Latin America, while tomar is polite everywhere.

How to understand the answer

Asking is the easy part. The reply often comes fast, so these phrases are just as important as the questions:

  • ¿Puede repetir, por favor? [PWEH-deh rreh-peh-TEER] means "Can you repeat, please?"
  • Hablo un poco de español [AH-bloh oon POH-koh deh es-pah-NYOHL] means "I speak a little Spanish", which warns people to keep it simple.
  • ¿Cómo se dice...? [KOH-moh seh DEE-seh] means "How do you say...?"

Listen for the key words rather than every word: numbers, left, right, here (aquí), there (allí), near and far. Asking yes-or-no questions also helps, because you only need to catch sí or no. Most people are happy to slow down once they see you are trying.

Numbers come up constantly, in prices, times and platform numbers. Practice counting 1 to 20 out loud while you make your morning coffee or tea; it is the most useful 2 minutes of practice you can do.

Emergency Spanish phrases for travelers

In an emergency, short and loud is best. Nobody will judge your grammar.

I need a doctorNecesito un médico[neh-seh-SEE-toh oon MEH-dee-koh]
Call an ambulanceLlame a una ambulancia[YAH-meh ah OO-nah ahm-boo-LAHN-syah]
Where is the hospital?¿Dónde está el hospital?[DOHN-deh es-TAH el ohs-pee-TAHL]
My wallet was stolenMe robaron la cartera[meh rroh-BAH-rohn lah kar-TEH-rah]
I lost my passportPerdí mi pasaporte[pehr-DEE mee pah-sah-POR-teh]
I have travel insuranceTengo seguro de viaje[TEN-goh seh-GOO-roh deh BYAH-heh]
Emergency numbers differ by country: 112 works in Spain and across the European Union, and 911 is used in Mexico and several other countries in the Americas. Check the number for every country you visit and save it in your phone before you travel. Buy travel insurance and keep a copy of your passport apart from the original.

For theft, you will usually need a police report (una denuncia) for your insurance. At a pharmacy, "Necesito algo para..." (I need something for...) followed by the problem works well.

Spanish words that change in Latin America

Spanish is one language with many accents, and you will be understood everywhere. A few everyday words do change, so it helps to recognize both:

In Spain you will also hear vosotros for "you all" among friends. Latin America uses ustedes for everyone, and you can use ustedes anywhere. In Mexico, "ahorita" can mean right now or sometime later, so a little patience helps.

A 2-week plan to learn travel Spanish

Ten minutes a day, said out loud, beats a two-hour cram before your flight. Here is a simple plan:

  1. Days 1 to 2: the survival 12, out loud, twice a day.
  2. Days 3 to 4: vowel sounds and stress rules; read street words such as plaza, playa and mercado aloud.
  3. Days 5 to 6: greetings and counting 1 to 100.
  4. Days 7 to 8: restaurant phrases; role-play ordering at home.
  5. Days 9 to 10: directions and transport.
  6. Days 11 to 12: shopping, the pharmacy and emergencies.
  7. Days 13 to 14: review everything and record yourself on your phone.

Label a few things at home with sticky notes (la puerta, la mesa, el agua), listen to Spanish radio or songs while you cook, and practice short dialogues with a partner. The book Easy Spanish for Travelers stretches this into a 21-day plan with a phrasebook and an emergency card you can copy.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important Spanish phrases for travelers?

Start with hola (hello), por favor (please), gracias (thank you), perdón (excuse me), no entiendo (I don't understand), ¿dónde está...? (where is...?), ¿cuánto cuesta? (how much is it?), quisiera (I would like), la cuenta, por favor (the check, please) and ¡ayuda! (help). Those few phrases handle most daily situations, and people respond warmly to anyone who tries.

How do you say where is the bathroom in Spanish?

Say "¿Dónde está el baño?" [DOHN-deh es-TAH el BAH-nyoh]. It works in every Spanish-speaking country. Add "por favor" to be polite. In Spain you may also see signs for "aseos" or "servicios", and the answer often includes "al fondo" (at the back) or "arriba" (upstairs) or "abajo" (downstairs).

Can I learn travel Spanish in two weeks?

You can learn enough for the basics. With 10 minutes a day of saying phrases out loud, most people can greet, order food, ask for directions and handle prices within two weeks. You will not be fluent, but you will manage common situations politely and understand many of the replies, especially numbers and directions.

Should I use usted or tú when traveling?

Use usted with strangers, staff, shopkeepers, police and older people, and tú with friends, children and people your age once things are relaxed. If you are unsure, choose usted, since nobody minds extra respect. If someone uses tú with you, it is usually fine to use tú back, which happens quickly in Spain.

Is Spanish in Spain different from Spanish in Mexico?

It is the same language with different accents and some different everyday words. Spain uses coche for car and zumo for juice, while Mexico uses carro and jugo. Spain often pronounces c and z as "th" and uses vosotros. Phrases from any good travel guide will be understood in both places.

How do I ask someone to speak more slowly in Spanish?

Say "Más despacio, por favor" [mahs des-PAH-syoh, por fah-BOR], which means "slower, please." You can also ask "¿Puede repetir, por favor?" to have something repeated, or start with "Hablo un poco de español" so people know to keep it simple. Most people are glad to help once they see you are trying.

Your first step today: read the survival 12 out loud right now, slowly, following the capital letters, then copy them onto a small card for your bag. Ten minutes a day from here will have you greeting, ordering and asking directions with confidence by the time you land.
