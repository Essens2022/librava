How to Make Sourdough Starter: A Simple Day-by-Day Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have a bag of flour, a jar and a bit of patience, you already have almost everything you need for your own sourdough starter. The short answer: mix equal weights of whole grain flour and lukewarm water, keep the jar somewhere around 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C), and every day remove most of it and feed the rest with fresh flour and water until it reliably doubles within 4 to 8 hours, which usually takes one to two weeks.

This guide walks you through the whole process with exact weights, a day-by-day table, what you will see (including the scary quiet phase), how to tell when it is ready, and how to fix a lazy or worrying starter. No special equipment, no secret ingredients.

What you need to make a sourdough starter

A starter is simply flour and water that wild yeasts and bacteria have moved into. They are already on the flour and in your kitchen. Your job is to give them food, warmth and a regular routine so the right ones take over.

  • A digital kitchen scale. Cups are too uneven for this; weighing in grams keeps the ratio steady.
  • Two clean glass jars of about 16 to 32 fl oz (500 ml to 1 liter). Hot soapy water is enough, there is no need to sterilize them.
  • Whole grain rye or whole wheat (wholemeal) flour to start, plus bread flour for later.
  • Lukewarm water, around 80°F (27°C). If your tap water is heavily chlorinated, use filtered water.
  • A spoon or small spatula, a rubber band and a strip of tape and a pen.

Whole grain flour gives a new starter the best chance because the bran and germ carry more nutrients and more of the microbes you want. Rye is especially lively. Once the starter is established you can feed it mostly white flour.

Weigh each empty jar and write its weight on the tape. That way you can check how much starter is inside without scraping it out.

Sourdough starter day by day

Each feeding works the same way: weigh the amount of starter you are keeping into a clean jar, add the flour and water, stir until no dry flour is left and scrape down the sides. Put the rubber band at the level of the starter so you can see the rise. Cover loosely with the lid resting on top, not screwed tight.

DayWhat to doWhat you might see
1Mix 50 g whole grain flour with 50 g waterThick, wet paste, little change
2Keep 50 g, add 50 g whole grain flour and 50 g waterOften a big burst of bubbles
3 to 4Same as day 2Flat and quiet, odd smells
5 to 7Keep 50 g, add 25 g whole grain flour, 25 g bread flour, 50 g waterSmall bubbles return, tangy smell
8 to 9Keep 25 g, add 50 g flour mix and 50 g water, once or twice a dayRises and falls more predictably
10 to 14Same as day 8Doubles within about 4 to 8 hours

The part you remove each time is called discard. For the first few days it is very young, so put it in the compost or trash. From about day 5 you can collect it in a lidded jar in the fridge for pancakes or crackers.

Try to feed at about the same time each day, such as 8 in the morning. Being an hour or two late now and then does no harm. When you switch to twice a day, 8 in the morning and 8 in the evening is easy to remember.

Why your starter goes quiet on day 3 or 4

Almost everyone panics at this point. On day 2 the jar may double and look wonderfully alive. Then on day 3 or 4 it sits flat, with a few sad bubbles, and maybe smells like nail polish, vinegar or old socks.

That early burst is mostly bacteria that love fresh flour, not the yeast you want. As the jar gets more acidic they die back, and the acid-loving yeasts and lactic acid bacteria slowly multiply in the background. The quiet phase is a normal stage, not a dead starter.

Do not start over. Keep feeding on schedule and keep the jar warm. In most kitchens bubbles return somewhere between day 5 and day 7. Throwing it out on day 4 just puts you back at day 1 with the same quiet phase ahead.

Around day 5 you should see small bubbles spread through the starter, and the smell should shift toward something pleasantly sour, like yogurt, beer or green apples. Adding some bread flour from this point gives you a starter that behaves more like the one you will bake with.

How do you know your sourdough starter is ready?

Your starter is ready to bake with when it reliably doubles in volume within roughly 4 to 8 hours of a feeding at warm room temperature, two or three days in a row. One good day is not enough. You are looking for a steady pattern.

Other signs of a ready starter:

  • Bubbles of different sizes all the way through, not only on top
  • A domed or slightly flattened top at its peak
  • A pleasantly sour smell rather than a harsh one
  • A stretchy, web-like inside when you pull a spoon through it, a bit like thick mousse

You may have heard of the float test, where a spoonful dropped into water should float. A floating starter is usually active, but a whole grain or wetter starter can sink even when it is strong. Trust the doubling over the float.

A simple way to track it: after each feeding, note the time and room temperature on a sticky note, then check the rubber band at 4, 6 and 8 hours. Three days of notes will tell you more than any photo online.

What to do if your starter is not rising

Temperature is the first thing to check. A starter sitting at 64°F (18°C) can barely move for a week, then start doubling within days once it is moved to a spot nearer 76°F (24°C). Cool kitchens are the most common reason for a slow start.

Work through these fixes one at a time:

  1. Warm it up. Try the inside of a switched-off oven with only the light on (check it does not get too hot and leave a note so nobody preheats it), the top of the fridge, or near a warm appliance.
  2. Go back to whole grain. Feed only rye or whole wheat for a few days for extra nutrients.
  3. Check your weights. A runny starter may have too much water and a stiff one too little. Weigh every feeding.
  4. Change the water. If you suspect chlorine in your tap water, switch to filtered.
  5. Give it time. Ten to fourteen days is perfectly normal in a cool home.

The opposite problem looks like this: the starter rises well but peaks in only 2 to 3 hours and then collapses. That means it is hungry, not weak. Give it a bigger feed, for example keep 20 g and add 60 g flour and 60 g water, so the food lasts longer.

Hooch or mold on sourdough starter

A layer of gray, brown or dark liquid on top is called hooch. It is alcohol made by yeast that has run out of food, and it is harmless. Pour it off for a milder flavor or stir it back in for a tangier one, then feed. A strong acetone or nail polish smell means the same thing: your starter is very hungry, so feed it, possibly twice in a row.

Mold is different and you should not try to save it.

What you seeWhat it meansWhat to do
Gray liquid on topHungry (hooch)Pour off or stir in, then feed
Acetone or alcohol smellVery hungryFeed, maybe twice
Fallen, streaky jar, sharp smellPast its peakFeed before using
Fuzzy spots, pink or orange streaksMold or spoilageThrow it all out
Food safety first. If you see fuzzy mold of any color, or pink or orange streaks, throw the whole starter away, wash the jar in hot soapy water and start again. Do not scrape off the top layer, and never taste a starter that looks or smells questionable. Keep discard in the fridge, not on the counter, and use it within about a week.

How to feed and store your starter after it is ready

Once your starter is established, keeping it alive takes a few minutes a day or even a week. Feedings are written as ratios of starter to flour to water by weight. More fresh food means a longer wait before it peaks.

RatioExampleTime to peak at 75°F (24°C)
1:1:130 g, 30 g, 30 gAbout 3 to 5 hours
1:2:220 g, 40 g, 40 gAbout 4 to 6 hours
1:5:510 g, 50 g, 50 gAbout 8 to 12 hours

Keep it small. About 100 g of starter is plenty for most home loaves, and it saves a lot of flour.

  • If you bake three or more times a week, keep it on the counter and feed it once or twice a day.
  • If you bake once a week or less, feed it, leave it out for 1 to 2 hours, then refrigerate it and feed it weekly.
  • Before baking with a fridge starter, give it one to three feedings at room temperature until it doubles again within 4 to 8 hours.

Once it is going well, spread about 30 g of active starter very thinly on parchment (baking paper) and let it dry for a few days. Crumble the flakes into a small airtight jar. That dried backup can bring your culture back if the main jar ever goes wrong.

Your first week sourdough starter checklist

Most of the work happens in the first week, and none of it takes more than five minutes a day. Use this as a quick routine:

  • Day 1: label two clean jars, weigh 50 g whole grain flour and 50 g lukewarm water, mix, cover loosely.
  • Every day: keep 50 g, discard the rest, feed, scrape the sides, move the rubber band.
  • Every day: write down the time, the room temperature and how high it rose.
  • Days 3 and 4: expect a flat, quiet jar and keep going.
  • From day 5: add some bread flour and start saving discard in the fridge.
  • When it rises and falls within about 12 hours: switch to keeping 25 g and feeding twice a day.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to make a sourdough starter?

Most starters take about 7 to 14 days to become strong enough for bread. In a warm kitchen around 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C) it can be closer to a week. In a cool home it can take two weeks or a little longer. The calendar matters less than the pattern: wait until it doubles within about 4 to 8 hours of feeding, several days in a row.

Why did my sourdough starter rise on day 2 and then stop?

That early rise comes mostly from bacteria that thrive in fresh flour, not from the yeast you want. As the starter becomes more acidic those bacteria die back, which leaves the jar flat for a few days. It is a normal quiet phase. Keep feeding on schedule and keep it warm, and new bubbles usually appear by day 5 to 7.

Can I use all-purpose flour to make a sourdough starter?

Yes, all-purpose (plain) flour can work, but it is usually slower to get going. Whole grain rye or whole wheat flour carries more nutrients and microbes, so it gives a new starter a stronger start. A good approach is to begin with whole grain for the first few days, then switch to a mix or to white bread flour once the starter is rising well.

Do I have to throw away sourdough discard?

In the first four days, yes, because the starter is too young to be useful. After that you can save discard in a lidded jar in the fridge and use it within about a week for pancakes, crackers, flatbreads or waffles. Do not keep discard at room temperature for more than a few hours, and do not pour it down the sink, where it can harden in the pipes.

What is the liquid on top of my sourdough starter?

The gray, brown or dark liquid is called hooch. It is alcohol and water made by yeast that has run out of food, and it simply means your starter is hungry. Pour it off or stir it back in, then feed as usual. If it keeps appearing, feed more often or use a bigger feed. Hooch is harmless, unlike fuzzy mold or pink or orange streaks.

Can I keep my sourdough starter in the fridge?

Yes, and it is the easiest option if you bake once a week or less. Feed the starter, leave it at room temperature for 1 to 2 hours, then refrigerate it with the lid on. Feed it about once a week. Before baking, give it one to three feedings at room temperature until it doubles within roughly 4 to 8 hours again.

Today, label two clean jars, weigh out 50 g of whole grain flour and 50 g of lukewarm water, stir them together and set a daily reminder for the next feeding. Keep it warm, keep notes and keep going through the quiet days, and within a week or two you will have a lively starter ready for your first loaf.
