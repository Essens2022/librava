PCOS Diet Plan: What to Eat, Easy Swaps and a Sample Week

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have just been diagnosed with PCOS, or you have lived with it for years, you have probably been told a dozen different things about what you should eat. The short answer: there is no single PCOS diet, but regular, balanced meals built on vegetables, protein, fiber-rich carbohydrates and healthy fats are the pattern most often recommended, because they give a slower, steadier rise in blood sugar.

This guide skips the banned-food lists and the miracle teas. You will find how to build a PCOS-friendly plate, how to handle carbs without fear, a simple meal rhythm, swaps that are easy to keep, a sample week of meals and the signs that mean it is time to call your doctor.

Why food matters with PCOS

PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) is a common hormonal condition, affecting about 1 in 10 women of reproductive age. It looks different in almost everyone: irregular periods, acne, extra hair growth, thinning scalp hair, tiredness, cravings or difficulty getting pregnant can all be part of it. You can have PCOS at any size.

Food comes up so often because many people with PCOS, though not all, have insulin resistance. Insulin moves sugar from your blood into your cells. When cells respond to it less easily, the body makes more insulin, and higher insulin can push the ovaries to make more androgens, which can add to symptoms such as irregular periods and acne. Insulin resistance can also make energy swing and cravings feel louder.

Food and habits cannot cure PCOS or guarantee pregnancy. What they can do is support your overall health, help steady your energy and, for some people, ease some symptoms. They work alongside medical care, not instead of it. PCOS also raises the long-term risk of type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol and high blood pressure, so the same eating pattern helps protect your heart and blood sugar over the years.

Build a balanced PCOS plate

The simplest PCOS diet tool is a picture you can carry in your head. Take an ordinary dinner plate, about 9 to 10 inches (23 to 25 cm) across, and fill it roughly like this:

  • Half vegetables, cooked or raw: broccoli, peppers, spinach, cabbage, green beans, tomatoes, mushrooms.
  • A quarter protein: beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, fish, chicken, lean meat or Greek yogurt.
  • A quarter fiber-rich carbohydrate: brown rice, oats, barley, quinoa, whole wheat (wholemeal) pasta or potatoes with their skins.
  • A little healthy fat: olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado or oily fish.

No scales or counting needed. A chili or curry that mixes everything in one bowl still counts; just check that it has plenty of vegetables, some protein and some fiber-rich carbs. If you are hungrier after a long walk or a busy shift, have a bigger plate in the same proportions.

Beans and lentils are a quiet hero here. They count as both protein and fiber-rich carbohydrate, so a lentil soup or bean chili can fill two quarters of the plate at once.

Carbs and PCOS without the fear

Carbohydrates are not the enemy, and a very low-carb or keto diet is not required for PCOS. The question is which carbs you choose most of the time and what you eat with them.

Fiber-rich carbs are digested more slowly, so the sugar they contain enters your blood more gradually. Good everyday choices include oats, brown rice, barley, bulgur, quinoa, rye bread, whole grain crispbreads, corn tortillas, beans, chickpeas (garbanzo beans), lentils and whole fruit rather than juice.

Pairing makes as much difference as the choice itself. A slice of white toast on its own goes down fast and you may feel hungry soon after. The same slice with eggs, tomatoes and a little avocado becomes a meal that lasts, because protein, fat and fiber slow the whole plate down. So you do not have to give up white rice or pasta; enjoy them with protein and vegetables, and lean toward the whole grain versions most days.

If you want to try a very low-carb diet, fasting or cutting out whole food groups, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian first. There is no need to avoid dairy or gluten unless you have a diagnosed allergy, intolerance or celiac disease.

Protein and healthy fats at every meal

Including some protein at breakfast, lunch and dinner is one of the simplest changes for steadier energy and fewer afternoon cravings. Protein keeps you full and supports muscle, and muscle helps your body use blood sugar.

  • Plant options: beans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu, tempeh, edamame, hummus, and nuts and seeds in smaller amounts.
  • Animal options: eggs, chicken, turkey, lean beef, pork or lamb, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese and fish.

A rough portion of meat or fish is about the size and thickness of your palm. For beans or tofu, think one or two generous cupped handfuls. Aim for fish about twice a week, including one oily fish such as salmon, sardines, mackerel or trout.

For fats, let olive oil, rapeseed (canola) oil, nuts, seeds, avocado and oily fish do most of the work. Since PCOS raises the risk of high cholesterol over time, keep fatty and processed meats, butter and cream to smaller amounts.

When to eat with PCOS

A regular meal rhythm matters as much as what is on the plate. Long gaps without food often lead to shaky, irritable afternoons and strong cravings by evening. That is not a willpower problem; it is what bodies do when they go too long without fuel.

A simple daily rhythm:

  1. Breakfast within a couple of hours of getting up.
  2. Lunch about 4 to 5 hours later.
  3. A snack in the afternoon if dinner is still a long way off.
  4. Dinner, then a natural break overnight.

Good snacks are small balanced plates: a boiled egg with cherry tomatoes, hummus with carrot and cucumber sticks, Greek yogurt with a few nuts, an apple with a small handful of almonds, or whole grain crispbreads with cottage cheese. Snacks are optional; if you are not hungry, skip them.

Skipping meals to "save up" for later tends to backfire. If you take a medicine that lowers blood sugar, skipping meals can also be risky, so ask your doctor about meal timing.

Smart swaps and drinks

You do not need a new kitchen, just a few swaps you can keep for months. These add fiber and help meals digest more slowly.

Instead ofTry
White riceBrown rice, or half white and half brown
White pastaWhole wheat pasta, or half and half
White breadWhole grain, seeded or rye bread
Sugary breakfast cerealSavory oatmeal (porridge) or eggs on whole grain toast
Fries (chips)Roasted potato or sweet potato wedges with skins
Regular soda (fizzy drinks)Sparkling water with lemon or cucumber

Drinks are an easy place to start. Sugary sodas, sweetened iced teas, energy drinks and big glasses of fruit juice deliver a lot of fast sugar with nothing to slow it down. Keep them for occasional treats. Water, unsweetened tea and coffee, and milk or unsweetened fortified plant drinks are good everyday choices. When shopping, pick the bread or cereal with whole grain as the first ingredient and more fiber.

Habits that support a PCOS diet

Food works best alongside a few everyday habits:

  • Movement you enjoy. Adults are generally advised to aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking or cycling, plus strength work on two days. A 10 minute walk after dinner counts. Chair squats and wall push-ups build muscle without a gym.
  • Sleep. Most adults need 7 to 9 hours. Poor sleep can increase hunger and cravings the next day. Loud snoring and daytime sleepiness are worth mentioning to your doctor, because sleep apnea is more common with PCOS.
  • Small daily breaks from stress, such as a short walk outside, a few minutes of slow breathing or a call with a friend.
  • Ten minutes of weekly planning: pick two or three dinners, one make-ahead breakfast, and cook a pot of grains and a tray of roasted vegetables on your free day.

This approach is weight neutral. For some people with a higher weight, a modest change in weight can improve some symptoms, but that is a decision to make with your doctor, and steadier energy and better blood pressure can come whether the scale moves or not. Disordered eating is more common with PCOS, so if eating feels out of control or full of guilt, please tell your doctor.

When to see a doctor about PCOS

Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before making big diet or exercise changes, especially if you take metformin or any medicine that lowers blood sugar. Never stop or change a prescribed medicine on your own, and ask before trying supplements such as inositol, berberine or cinnamon, since some can affect blood sugar or interact with medicines.

Book an appointment if your periods stop for 3 months or more (and you are not pregnant), your periods are very heavy, you are trying to conceive, hair growth increases quickly or your voice deepens, you notice signs of high blood sugar such as strong thirst, passing urine often or blurred vision, or low mood lasts more than a couple of weeks.

Get urgent medical help for sudden, severe pain in the lower abdomen or pelvis (especially with fever, vomiting or fainting), very heavy bleeding such as soaking a pad every hour for several hours, or a possible pregnancy with one-sided pain or bleeding. Call 911 in the US or 999 in the UK in an emergency.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best diet for PCOS?

There is no single best diet for PCOS. The pattern most often recommended is regular, balanced meals with plenty of vegetables, protein at every meal, fiber-rich carbohydrates such as whole grains and beans, and healthy fats like olive oil and nuts. It is similar to general heart-healthy eating, which also helps with the long-term risks linked to PCOS. The best plan is one you can keep, so build it around foods you enjoy.

What foods should I avoid with PCOS?

No food has to be banned. It helps to keep sugary drinks, sweets and large portions of very refined carbohydrates for occasional treats, and to eat fatty and processed meats in smaller amounts. White bread, rice and pasta can still fit, ideally paired with protein and vegetables. You only need to cut out dairy or gluten if you have a diagnosed allergy, intolerance or celiac disease.

Is keto good for PCOS?

A keto or very low-carb diet is not required for PCOS. Many people do well with moderate amounts of fiber-rich carbohydrates paired with protein and fat, which is easier to keep up long term. Very restrictive diets can be hard to sustain and may feed food guilt. If you want to try keto, fasting or another strict plan, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian first, especially if you take medicines that lower blood sugar.

Can diet cure PCOS?

No. Diet cannot cure or reverse PCOS, and it cannot guarantee regular periods or pregnancy. Balanced meals, regular movement, good sleep and not smoking support overall health, can help with insulin resistance and may ease some symptoms for some people. Results vary from person to person. Food works best alongside the medical care your doctor recommends, not as a replacement for it.

How many meals a day should I eat with PCOS?

Most people do well with three regular meals a day, plus a snack if there is a long gap before the next meal. Eating breakfast within a couple of hours of waking and leaving about 4 to 5 hours between meals helps avoid the energy crashes and strong cravings that follow long stretches without food. Snacks are optional, so skip them if you are not hungry.

Do I need supplements for PCOS?

Most people do not need supplements to eat well with PCOS. You may hear about inositol, cinnamon, spearmint tea or berberine, but evidence is limited or mixed and products vary in what they contain. Some can affect blood sugar or interact with medicines. If you take metformin, your doctor may check your vitamin B12 over time. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement.

Start with one change today: build tonight's dinner as half vegetables, a quarter protein and a quarter fiber-rich carbs with a little olive oil, then repeat that plate once a day this week.
