Basic Car Maintenance Checklist: An Easy 10-Minute Monthly Check

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you drive a car but have never opened the hood (bonnet), you are in good company, and you are probably paying for it in ways you cannot see. The basic car maintenance checklist is short: once a month, walk around the car, check all four tire pressures and the tread, check the oil, coolant, brake fluid and washer fluid levels, then test every light and the wipers.

That routine takes about 10 minutes once you know it, and it catches most of the small problems that turn into big bills. This guide shows you each step in plain words, what the results mean, and which warning lights mean you should stop driving.

Before you start your car check

Your owner's manual is the real expert on your car. It lists the right oil grade, fluid types, service intervals and tire pressures, and it overrules any general advice, including this guide. If you have lost it, many makers offer a digital copy.

Find two labels today. The tire pressure sticker is usually inside the driver's door jamb, sometimes in the fuel flap or glove box. The battery date is often printed on a sticker or stamped on the case. Both tell you a lot in seconds.

  • Park on firm, level ground with the parking brake (handbrake) on.
  • Check fluids with the engine off and cool, ideally first thing in the morning.
  • Keep a tire gauge, a clean rag, gloves and a flashlight (torch) in the car.
  • Find your spare tire or inflator kit, jack and wheel wrench now, not on a dark roadside.

The 10-minute monthly car check

Do the steps in the same order every time. After two or three months, anything unusual jumps out because you know what normal looks like.

  1. Walk around the car. Look for a tire sitting lower than the others, new dents, cracked lights, anything hanging underneath and chips in the windshield (windscreen).
  2. Look at the ground where you park for drips.
  3. Check all four tire pressures with the tires cold, plus the spare if you can reach it.
  4. Look at the tread and sidewalls for wear, cuts and bulges.
  5. Open the hood and check oil, coolant, brake fluid, washer fluid and power steering fluid if your car has a reservoir.
  6. Glance at the battery for white or green crust on the terminals or a loose cable.
  7. Test all the lights and run the wipers and washers.
  8. Start the car and make sure the warning lights go out after a few seconds.
  9. Write the date and mileage in a simple log.

Do an extra check before a long road trip, before winter and summer, and after hitting a deep pothole or scraping a curb (kerb) hard.

How to check tire pressure and tread

Correct tire pressure is the easiest win in car care. It affects grip, braking, fuel use and how long tires last. Use the figure on the door jamb sticker, never the maximum printed on the tire sidewall.

  1. Check when the tires are cold, before driving or after no more than a mile or two.
  2. Unscrew the valve cap and press the gauge firmly and squarely onto the valve. A long hiss means it is not seated.
  3. Compare the reading with the sticker. Add air in short bursts and recheck, or press the pin in the valve to let some out.
  4. Screw the cap back on.

If the same tire is low again next month, it probably has a slow puncture. A tire pressure warning light is a backup, not a replacement for your gauge, because many systems only warn you once a tire is well below where it should be.

Tread depth

The legal minimum is 1.6 mm across the central part of the tire in the UK, and many US states use 2/32 of an inch. Many tire experts suggest replacing earlier, at around 3 mm or 4/32 inch, because wet grip drops sharply as tread gets thin. A cheap tread depth gauge is the most accurate check. The raised wear bars in the grooves also show when a tire is at the limit.

The coin test is a rough guide. In the US, put a penny head-first into a groove; if you can see the top of the head, tread is likely 2/32 inch or less. In the UK, if you can see the outer band of a 20p coin in the main grooves, the tread may be close to the limit. Any bulge on a sidewall means internal damage, so replace that tire before you drive any distance.

How to check engine oil and other fluids

Oil keeps the engine's metal parts from grinding together, and an engine without enough of it can be destroyed in minutes. It is the fluid cars most often run low on.

  1. Pull out the dipstick and wipe it clean.
  2. Push it fully back in, then pull it out again.
  3. The oil should sit between the minimum and maximum marks.
  4. Fresh oil is amber. Dark is normal for older oil. Milky or foamy oil needs checking by a mechanic.

If it is low, add the exact grade your manual specifies, about half a quart or half a liter at a time, then wait a minute and recheck. Do not overfill. Some newer cars have no dipstick and show the oil level in a dashboard menu.

Never open the coolant cap on a warm engine. The system is pressurized, and hot coolant can spray out and scald you. Check the level through the side of the reservoir instead, and wait until the engine is completely cold before opening anything.

Checking lights, wipers and the battery

Most of your lights cannot be seen from the driver's seat. Ask someone to stand outside while you switch on low and high beams (main beams), front and rear turn signals (indicators), brake lights, reverse lights and fog lights. On your own, park facing a garage door or shop window at dusk and use the reflection, or back up to a wall and watch your mirrors for the brake light glow.

Spray the washers and run the wipers for a few sweeps. Streaks, skipping or squealing mean the blades are due for replacement, which most people can do in a few minutes using the manual.

A typical car battery lasts about 3 to 5 years. Heat wears it out and the first cold morning exposes the weakness. Watch for slow cranking, clicking when you try to start, dim lights at start-up or a swollen case. If yours is over four years old, have it tested before winter; many shops do this free.

Car warning lights that mean stop

As a general rule, red means stop safely as soon as you can, amber or yellow means get it checked soon, and green or blue simply means a system is on. Symbols vary, so check your manual.

  • Oil can (red). Low oil pressure. Pull over safely and switch off. Check the level. If it is fine and the light stays on, do not drive; call for a tow.
  • Thermometer in liquid (red). Overheating. Stop, switch off and let it cool for at least 30 minutes before checking coolant.
  • Circle with an exclamation mark (red). Check the parking brake is fully off. If the light stays on, there may be low brake fluid or a fault, so drive slowly to a safe spot and call a professional.
  • Battery shape (red). The charging system has failed and the car is running on battery alone. Switch off extras and head to a garage or safe place.
  • Engine outline (amber). Steady means book a check soon and make sure the gas cap (fuel cap) is tight. Flashing means a serious misfire, so ease off and get it seen right away.
  • Horseshoe with an exclamation mark (amber). Low tire pressure. Check all four with a gauge.

Brake warning signs not to ignore

Brakes give plenty of warning before they fail. The trick is not talking yourself out of the signs.

  • A high squeal when braking is often the built-in wear indicator telling you the pads are thin.
  • Grinding or metal scraping usually means pads are worn through. Stop driving if you can.
  • A soft, spongy pedal, or one that sinks slowly at a stop, is a serious warning.
  • A pulsing pedal or a shaking steering wheel when braking often means uneven discs.
  • The car pulling to one side when you brake can mean a sticking part or uneven wear.

A light squeak on the first few stops of a damp morning is usually surface rust and fades quickly. Anything that lasts, or any change in how the pedal feels, should be checked by a professional.

Car maintenance schedule beyond the monthly check

Your monthly check covers what you can see. Some jobs follow the manual's schedule, usually given in miles (or kilometers) and months, whichever comes first.

JobTypical timing
Oil and filter changeAs your manual or service reminder says
Tire rotationOften every 5,000 to 7,500 miles (8,000 to 12,000 km)
Battery testYearly once it is over 4 years old
Wiper bladesWhen they streak or squeal
Winter and summer prepBefore each season and long trips

Keep a small log in the glove box (glove compartment) with the date, mileage, work done and cost. It helps you spot patterns, avoid paying twice for the same job and shows a buyer the car has been cared for. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack. A jack is for lifting, not holding. Use proper axle stands (jack stands) on firm, level ground with wheel chocks and the parking brake on. Electric and hybrid cars carry high voltage, so leave anything beyond basic checks to a professional.

Frequently asked questions

What basic maintenance does a car need?

Every car needs regular oil and filter changes, correct tire pressures, enough tread, topped-up fluids, working lights and wipers, and a healthy battery. A 10-minute check once a month covers most of what you can see yourself. Bigger jobs like oil changes and tire rotation follow the schedule in your owner's manual, usually by miles and months, whichever comes first.

How often should you check your car's oil?

Check it at least once a month and before any long drive, with the car on level ground and the engine off. Older cars, and cars that have just had work done, deserve a look every couple of weeks. If the red oil pressure light ever comes on while driving, pull over safely and switch off straight away rather than waiting.

What tire pressure should my car have?

Use the pressure on the sticker inside the driver's door jamb or in your owner's manual. Do not use the number on the tire sidewall, which is the maximum the tire can hold, not the right pressure for your car. Check with a gauge when the tires are cold, because driving warms the air and makes a low tire read higher.

Which car warning lights are serious?

Red lights are the serious ones. The oil pressure light, engine temperature light, brake warning light and battery or charging light all mean you should stop safely as soon as you can or get help quickly. A flashing check engine light also needs urgent attention. Steady amber lights usually mean book a check within a few days.

How do I know when my tires need replacing?

Replace tires when the tread nears the legal minimum, which is 1.6 mm in the UK and commonly 2/32 inch in US states, though many experts suggest around 3 mm or 4/32 inch for safer wet grip. Also replace any tire with a sidewall bulge, deep cut or cracking. A tread depth gauge or the built-in wear bars give the clearest answer.

Can I do car maintenance myself with no experience?

Yes, the basic checks need no mechanical skill. Checking tire pressure, tread, oil, coolant and washer fluid, testing lights and replacing wiper blades are all easy jobs with your owner's manual open beside you. Leave brakes, high-voltage parts on electric and hybrid cars, and any work under the car to professionals unless you are trained and properly equipped.

Your first step today is to find your owner's manual and the tire pressure sticker on the door jamb, buy a simple tire gauge, and do your first 10-minute check this weekend. Write down what you find, and next month you will already know what normal looks like for your car.
