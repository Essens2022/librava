Work From Home Tips: How to Stay Focused, Comfortable and Calm

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your kitchen table has become your office and your days blur into evenings at the laptop, you are far from alone. The most useful work from home tips come down to five things: a work-only spot set up to suit your body, a clear start and finish to the day, planned blocks of focus, firm limits on after-hours messages and regular contact with other people.

This guide shows you how to do each of those with what you already have, from screen height to shutdown rituals to what to say when your manager messages at 10 p.m. It works for full-time remote workers, hybrid workers and freelancers alike.

What quietly goes wrong when you work from home

People love the lack of commute, the flexibility and the quiet. Four problems tend to creep in, though, often so slowly you barely notice them.

  • Always on. The laptop is always there, so work leaks into evenings and weekends.
  • Feeling cut off. The casual chat of an office disappears, and loneliness can build.
  • Distraction everywhere, from laundry and deliveries to pets and family.
  • Sitting all day, because there is no walk to a meeting room or the train.

Knowing your own work style helps you decide which to tackle first. Track one ordinary week: when your energy peaks, how much quiet you need, how much structure suits you and how much contact keeps you well. Then pick the one problem that costs you most and start there.

Set up a home workspace in any home

You do not need a spare room. A corner of the bedroom, a landing or the end of the dining table can all work if you make them feel like a work zone. The key is a spot you can return to each day, with your things kept together and a clear signal that work is happening there.

  • Face a window side-on rather than with it behind or in front of your screen, to cut glare.
  • Use a box, basket or shelf so you can pack work away at night if you share the room.
  • Tame noise with a closed door, soft furnishings or simple earplugs or headphones.
  • Keep the room comfortably warm or cool, since you will be still for long periods.
  • Avoid overloaded sockets (power points) and trailing cables, and check extension leads are in good condition.

A small cue that marks the start of work, like a desk lamp you only switch on during working hours, helps your brain change modes even when the office is also the kitchen.

Home office ergonomics without expensive gear

Aches in the neck, shoulders, back and wrists are some of the most common complaints of home workers, and most come from a setup that does not fit you. You can fix much of it with books, cushions and a separate keyboard.

CheckWhat to aim for
Screen heightTop of the screen at or slightly below eye level
Screen distanceAbout an arm's length away (20 to 30 inches, 50 to 75 cm)
ArmsElbows bent at about 90 degrees, shoulders relaxed
WristsStraight while typing and using the mouse
FeetFlat on the floor or on a footrest (a box works)
BackLower back supported by the chair or a rolled towel
LaptopRaise it on a stand or books and add a separate keyboard and mouse for long sessions
LightFrom the side, not behind the screen

Then keep moving. Change position often, take a short movement break every 30 to 60 minutes and use the 20-20-20 habit for your eyes: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away for 20 seconds. If you have a standing desk, alternate sitting and standing rather than standing all day.

If pain persists, or you have numbness, tingling or headaches that do not settle, see a doctor (GP) or a physical therapist (physiotherapist) rather than trying to diagnose yourself, and keep up regular eye tests. In the UK, employers' health and safety duties, including workstation assessments, generally apply to home workers too. Elsewhere rules differ, so check your contract and your employer's remote work policy.

A work from home routine that starts and ends well

Without a commute, days have no natural edges. A simple routine gives them back.

Try a fake commute: a 10 to 20 minute walk around the block before work and another after. It gets you into daylight, gives you thinking time before the messages start and puts a clear gap between work and home. If a walk is not possible, walking the dog, the school run or tea on the back step can do the same job.

Then spend 10 minutes planning. Check your calendar, choose your top one to three tasks and block time for the most important one during your best energy hours.

A 10-minute shutdown ritual

  1. Note what you finished today.
  2. Write where you left off and tomorrow's main task.
  3. Reply only to what truly cannot wait.
  4. Close work programs and turn off work notifications on your phone.
  5. Tidy the desk and switch off your work lamp or close the door.
  6. Say a closing phrase such as "That's the day done," then take your evening walk.

It feels silly at first. Repeated every day, it trains your brain to let go of work.

How to stay focused working from home

Focus at home is harder than it looks, because nobody can see you drifting and the fridge, the washing and your phone are all within reach.

Time blocking gives important work a slot. Give your main task a block of 60 to 120 minutes in your best energy window, add smaller blocks for email and messages, and leave some empty space for the unexpected. Treat focus blocks like meetings with yourself.

Inside a block, use focus sprints. Set a timer for 25 to 50 minutes, work on one task only, then take a 5 to 10 minute break away from screens. After three or four sprints, take a longer break. If a stray thought pops up, write it on a notepad and carry on.

  • Put your phone in another room or out of sight during sprints.
  • Turn off non-urgent notifications and check messages at set times.
  • Batch small tasks like replies, forms and admin into one block.
  • Stuck on starting? Commit to a single 10-minute sprint.

Handle household distractions and children

Housework creep is real. A simple rule helps: household jobs only in breaks, and only ones that take under five minutes, such as switching on the washing machine. Save the rest for after your shutdown.

If you share your home with a partner or housemates, hold a short household meeting. Agree who uses which space, when calls happen and a do-not-disturb signal, like a closed door or headphones on. You might say, "Between 9 and 11 I'm on focus time. Unless something's urgent, could we catch up at lunch?"

With children at home, lower the bar on hard days and plan around them. Put focus work in nap times, early mornings or after bedtime, split shifts with a partner if you can, and keep quieter tasks for when children are around. Be honest with your manager about childcare gaps. A short message such as "I'm covering childcare this afternoon, so I'll be online 9 to 1 and again from 7 to 9" usually lands better than trying to hide it.

How to separate work and home life

Decide your working hours first and share them. Put them in your email signature or status, and set your status to away when you finish.

After-hours messages are the biggest threat to switching off. You do not have to reply at night to be good at your job. The next morning, you might say, "Morning! I saw your message from last night. I'm on it now and will have it to you by 11." If they push back, try, "I don't check messages in the evening, so if something is truly urgent after hours, a quick call is best."

  • Keep work apps off your personal phone if your role allows, or at least silence them out of hours.
  • Take real holidays and sick days. Set an out-of-office message and do not peek.
  • Put the laptop out of sight once you finish.

Use employer-approved devices and tools, lock your screen when you step away and keep home Wi-Fi protected with a strong password, so work and family life stay safely separate.

Beat loneliness when working remotely

Remote work can feel lonely even for people who enjoy quiet. Signs include dreading calls, feeling flat by midweek, talking much less than you used to or realizing you have not left the house in days.

Plan connection rather than leaving it to chance. A simple weekly plan might include one virtual coffee chat with a colleague with no agenda, one call with a friend or family member, one in-person activity outside work, such as a class or walking group, and one day working somewhere other than home, such as a library or coworking space, if you can.

If you work hybrid, make office days count by booking them for collaboration, meetings and lunch with people, and keep deep focus for home days.

If low mood, anxiety or stress lasts more than a couple of weeks or affects sleep, work or relationships, talk to a doctor (GP) or a qualified mental health professional. Many employers offer confidential employee assistance services. In a crisis, call or text 988 in the US or Canada, call Samaritans on 116 123 or 999 in an emergency in the UK, Lifeline 13 11 14 in Australia, or call or text 1737 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

How do I stay productive working from home?

Plan your day in 10 minutes each morning, choosing one to three priorities, and block time for the main one in your best energy hours. Work in focus sprints of 25 to 50 minutes with short breaks, keep your phone out of sight and check messages at set times. A shutdown ritual at a fixed time stops work spreading into the evening and keeps you fresher for tomorrow.

How should I set up my desk at home to avoid back pain?

Put the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away. Keep elbows bent at about 90 degrees, wrists straight, feet flat on the floor or a footrest and your lower back supported. Raise a laptop on books and use a separate keyboard and mouse. Move every 30 to 60 minutes, and see a doctor or physical therapist if pain persists.

How do I switch off after working from home?

Finish with a short shutdown ritual: note what you did, write tomorrow's main task, close programs, turn off work notifications and tidy the desk. Then mark the change with an evening walk or another activity away from your workspace. Put the laptop out of sight, set your status to away and agree with your manager how truly urgent issues reach you out of hours.

How can I work from home with kids?

Match tasks to your children's rhythm: save focused work for naps, early mornings, school hours or after bedtime, and do lighter tasks when they are around. Split shifts with a partner where possible, use any childcare available and be honest with your manager about when you can be online. On hard days, lower the bar and focus on the one task that matters most.

How do I stop feeling lonely working from home?

Build contact into your week on purpose: a regular no-agenda video chat with a colleague, a call with a friend, an in-person activity outside work and, if possible, a day working from a library or shared workspace. Use any office days for people rather than solo tasks. If loneliness or low mood lasts more than a couple of weeks, talk to a doctor or counselor.

Can I claim tax relief for working from home?

It depends on where you live, whether you are employed or self-employed and your employer's arrangements. Some countries offer allowances or deductions for home working costs, but the rules vary and change often. Check official tax guidance for your country or speak to an accountant before claiming anything, and ask your employer whether they provide equipment or an allowance.
