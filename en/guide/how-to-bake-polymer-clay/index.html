How to Bake Polymer Clay: Temperature, Timing and Easy Fixes

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

You have shaped your first earrings or charms, and now the oven feels like the scary part. The short answer: bake polymer clay at exactly the temperature and time printed on its package, check the real heat with a separate oven thermometer, preheat fully, cover the pieces with a foil tent, and time the bake by the thickest part of the clay.

Most beginner disappointments, like pieces that snap, turn brown or bubble, come from an oven that runs hotter or cooler than its dial. This guide walks you through a calm, repeatable bake in your home oven, plus what to do when something goes wrong.

What temperature to bake polymer clay at

Polymer clay is not one product with one recipe. Every brand, and often every line within a brand, has its own formula that the maker has tested to cure properly at a certain temperature for a certain time. That number is on the package, and it is the only one you should bake by.

To give you a sense of what is normal, many polymer clays bake somewhere around 230 to 275°F (110 to 135°C), often for about 15 to 30 minutes per 1/4 inch (6 mm) of thickness. Treat that range as background only. A clay that wants 230°F can scorch at 275°F, and a clay that wants 275°F will stay weak at 230°F.

  • Write the temperature and time on a sticky label and keep it with each block of clay.
  • If you mix clays from two different lines, bake at the lower temperature and lean toward the longer time, staying within what both packages allow.
  • Liquid polymer clay often has its own instructions, so check its bottle rather than assuming.
  • When in doubt, bake a small scrap first and test it.

Why you need an oven thermometer

Home oven dials are often inaccurate. An oven running 25°F (about 15°C) away from its setting is very common, and the heat swings up and down as the element switches on and off. For a casserole that hardly matters. For clay, it is the difference between a strong earring and a brittle one.

A separate oven thermometer is a cheap dial or digital gauge that sits inside the oven. Put it on the rack right next to where your clay will sit, because ovens have hot and cool spots. Keep it as a clay tool, separate from your kitchen things.

Map your oven before your first real bake

  1. Place the thermometer on the middle rack where your tray will go.
  2. Set the oven to your clay's package temperature.
  3. Read the thermometer every 5 minutes for about 30 minutes.
  4. Nudge the dial up or down in small steps until the reading holds steady at the package temperature, never above it.
  5. Write down the dial setting that works and tape the note near the oven.

That one evening of testing saves you a lot of snapped and scorched pieces later.

How to set up your tray and foil tent

Your baking setup has two jobs: support the clay evenly and shield it from direct heat. A good setup also keeps clay residue away from the parts of the oven that touch food.

  • Base. Use a dedicated baking tray or a plain ceramic tile with a sheet of plain printer paper or cardstock on top. Paper stops the shiny spots clay picks up from glossy surfaces. A tile lets you make pieces right on it and slide everything into the oven without moving them.
  • Cover. Fold a sheet of aluminum foil (aluminium foil in the UK) over the tray like a little roof, crimping the edges down so it does not touch the clay. Or put the tile inside a disposable foil pan and clip a second upturned pan on top with binder clips.

The cover protects the clay from the heating element and evens out temperature swings, which is why it is one of the best ways to prevent browning. Label the tray or tile for clay only.

Safety first. Never bake polymer clay at the same time as food, and never use trays, knives, rollers or pasta machines for food once they have touched clay. Do not use a microwave for polymer clay. Bake in a well-ventilated room, and if you use your home oven, wipe the inside with a damp cloth afterward.

How long to bake polymer clay by thickness

Package times are usually given per thickness, so measure the thickest part of the piece with a ruler until you can judge by eye. Flat earrings and a chunky figurine need very different bakes.

PieceTypical thicknessHow to time it
Flat earrings1/8 in (3 mm)Package minimum or a little more
Pendants and coasters1/4 in (6 mm)Package time for 1/4 in
Small figurines1/2 in (12 mm)Package time per 1/4 in, doubled
Pieces on foil coresVariesTime the clay layer, not the core

Many clay makers say the clay will not overbake as long as the temperature stays correct, and plenty of experienced makers bake a little longer than the minimum for extra strength. A useful rule: within reason, more time at the right temperature tends to help, while more temperature tends to hurt. Treat the minimum time as a floor, especially for thick or important pieces.

For figurines built over a scrunched foil armature, time the bake by the thickness of the clay shell. The foil heats through quickly, so a piece that looks thick can still bake evenly.

Step by step baking routine for a home oven

  1. Check the package temperature and time for the clay you used.
  2. Put your oven thermometer on the rack next to where the tray will sit.
  3. Open a window or run the extractor fan (cooker hood in the UK).
  4. Preheat fully, usually 15 to 30 minutes, until the thermometer reading is steady. Watch the thermometer, not the oven's ready beep, because many ovens blast extra heat while warming up.
  5. Slide in the tented tray only once the temperature has settled.
  6. Set a timer for the thickest part of the clay and stay where you would notice smoke or a burning smell.
  7. When the timer goes, turn off the oven and let the pieces cool.
  8. Wash your hands and wipe down your work surface while you wait.

A faint smell during a normal bake is common. A sharp, acrid burning smell is not, and the section on scorching below tells you what to do.

Cooling and the bend test

Polymer clay is slightly soft and flexible while hot and firms up as it cools. Leave pieces on a heat-safe surface, or in the oven with the door slightly open, for about 15 to 30 minutes before you handle them properly.

Bake a thin scrap alongside every batch. Once it is fully cool, give it a gentle bend. A properly cured thin piece should flex a little before it breaks, and slightly thicker pieces should feel solid and tough. If the scrap snaps instantly with almost no give, the batch probably needed more heat or more time.

A very common mistake is pressing pieces while they are still hot, deciding they are too soft, and baking again at a higher temperature. Hot clay is always a bit soft, so judge only once it is cool.

Why polymer clay breaks after baking

Underbaking is the most common beginner problem. Signs include pieces that snap like a cracker, edges that crumble when you sand them, earring holes that crack when you add a jump ring, and a chalky feel.

The usual causes are easy to fix:

  • The oven runs cooler than the dial says.
  • The clay went in before the oven had fully preheated.
  • The bake was timed for the thin part instead of the thickest part.
  • Pieces came out early.
  • The clay was not conditioned enough. Well-conditioned clay bends without cracking when you fold a pencil-thick log in half.

The good news is that you can usually rebake underbaked clay. Put it back in at the correct, thermometer-checked temperature for the full package time. Polymer clay can normally be baked more than once, which is also how makers bake detailed figurines in stages, adding fresh parts with a thin layer of liquid clay between bakes.

Burnt, brown or bubbly polymer clay

Overbaked clay usually shows as color change. White and pale colors go yellowish or tan, translucent clay turns amber, and you may see dark spots, shiny patches or blisters. Slight darkening on pale colors is mostly cosmetic. A foil tent, paper underneath and a steady temperature help prevent it.

Raised blisters come from air trapped while conditioning or rolling. To fix one, carefully prick or slice it open, press in a tiny bit of fresh or liquid clay, smooth it level, rebake and sand.

If clay scorches or smokes, act straight away. Overheated polymer clay can release irritating fumes, including hydrogen chloride. Turn off the oven, open windows and doors, leave the room with any children and pets, and let it air out before you return. Leave the clay to cool in the closed oven rather than carrying a smoking tray through the house, then throw the scorched pieces away and wipe the oven and tray.

Afterward, work out the cause. Most scorching comes from a spike during preheating, a missing tent, or pieces sitting too close to the element. Small countertop ovens (toaster ovens in the US, mini ovens in the UK) can run hot for exactly that reason, so a thermometer and tent matter even more there.

Frequently asked questions

Can you bake polymer clay in a regular oven?

Yes, a regular home oven works well for polymer clay. Use a dedicated tray or tile with paper underneath, cover the pieces with a foil tent or a covered foil pan, check the heat with a separate oven thermometer and wipe the oven afterward. Never bake clay and food at the same time, and keep a well-ventilated room. A dedicated small oven with accurate temperature control is ideal if you bake often.

Can you bake polymer clay in the microwave?

No. Do not use a microwave for polymer clay. Microwaves heat unevenly and cannot hold the steady, controlled temperature that polymer clay needs to cure safely and strongly. Use a conventional home oven or a small countertop oven, set to the temperature on your clay's package and checked with a separate oven thermometer, with the pieces covered by a foil tent.

How do I know when polymer clay is done baking?

You cannot tell by looking while it is hot, because polymer clay stays slightly soft until it cools. Bake for the package time based on the thickest part, let everything cool for 15 to 30 minutes, then bend a thin scrap baked alongside. A well-cured thin scrap flexes a little before breaking. If it snaps instantly, rebake the batch at the correct temperature.

Can you rebake polymer clay?

Yes, polymer clay can usually be baked more than once. That lets you rescue underbaked pieces by returning them to the oven at the correct, thermometer-checked temperature for the full package time. It also lets you bake in stages, firming a base or body first and then adding delicate details with a thin layer of liquid clay before baking again.

Why did my white polymer clay turn yellow?

Yellowing or browning usually means the clay got too hot, often during an oven spike while preheating or because the pieces were uncovered. Check the real temperature with an oven thermometer, preheat fully before the clay goes in, bake under a foil tent and keep paper under the pieces. Slight darkening on pale colors is mostly cosmetic, while blackening means the clay overheated.

Is it safe to bake polymer clay in the same oven as food?

It is common to use a home oven, but never bake clay and food at the same time. Bake clay on its own tray or tile under a foil tent or inside a covered foil pan, ventilate the room, and wipe the oven with a damp cloth afterward. Keep every tool that touches clay for clay only. Many regular makers eventually buy a dedicated small oven.

Your first step today is simple: put an oven thermometer on the middle rack, run a 30 minute empty test at your clay's package temperature, and write down the dial setting that gives the right reading.
