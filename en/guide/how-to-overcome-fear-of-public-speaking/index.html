How to Overcome Fear of Public Speaking: A Calm, Practical Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your heart races and your mouth goes dry at the thought of standing up in front of people, you are far from alone, and you can get much better at it. The short answer: accept that nerves are a normal body response, calm them with slow breathing and a pre-talk routine, build your talk around one clear message, rehearse out loud a few times, and practice in small steps that slowly get bigger.

The goal is not to feel zero nerves. It is to feel them and speak well anyway. This guide gives you the practical steps for presentations, meetings, toasts and video calls, including what to do in the first ten seconds and what to do if your mind goes blank.

Why public speaking feels so scary

When you face something that feels risky, your body releases stress hormones that speed up your heart, quicken your breathing and send energy to your muscles. That is why you get shaky hands, a dry mouth, butterflies and a voice that wobbles. Your body is not failing you. It is trying to help you rise to a challenge.

Two facts make that feeling easier to live with. First, the people listening notice far less than you think. A racing heart makes no sound, and a dry mouth does not show. Psychologists call our habit of overestimating how much others notice the spotlight effect. Second, most audiences are on your side. They want the next few minutes to go well, because that is better for them too.

So stop aiming to hide every sign of nerves. Audiences forgive nerves easily. What they find harder to forgive is a speaker who seems not to care, so aim for prepared and sincere rather than flawless.

How to calm nerves before a presentation

Breathing is the fastest calming tool you have, and nobody will notice you using it. Try the slow exhale:

  1. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 4.
  2. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of 6 to 8, as if cooling a spoonful of hot soup.
  3. Pause briefly at the bottom of the breath.
  4. Repeat 4 to 6 times.

The breath out must be longer than the breath in. Do not force a huge inhale, which can make you lightheaded. Practice this on calm days, such as while the kettle boils, so your body knows the pattern when you need it.

Then ground yourself. Stand with both feet flat, notice your weight pressing into the floor and press down gently for a few seconds. Or name a few things you can see and hear. Both pull your mind out of imagined disasters and back into the room.

Finally, use up the jittery energy. In a quiet spot, shake out your hands and arms for 10 to 15 seconds, roll your shoulders, clench your fists for five seconds and let go, and give a big yawn to loosen your jaw.

Call it excitement instead of trying to be calm

Nerves and excitement feel almost the same in your body: faster heartbeat, extra energy, a fluttery stomach. The main difference is the label your mind puts on it. Telling yourself "calm down" right before you speak rarely works, because your body does not believe it, and now you are nervous and failing to calm down.

A smaller step is easier to accept. Try saying, quietly or in your head:

  • "I feel excited. My body is getting ready."
  • "This energy will help me speak with life."
  • "I care about this, and that is why I feel it."

It may feel a little fake the first few times. With repetition, many people find the words start to match the feeling, and the dread before regular meetings eases.

Build a simple pre-talk routine

A routine takes the guesswork out of the hours before you speak. Instead of wondering what to do with your nerves, you follow familiar steps, and over time the routine itself tells your body you know what you are doing.

Day beforeLight rehearsal, pack what you need, set an alarm
Night beforeStop practicing an hour before bed and rest
Morning ofEat a normal meal, go easy on caffeine
1 hour beforeCheck the room, the tech and your water
10 to 15 minutes beforeBody and voice warm-up

Plan your first ten seconds exactly, because that is when nerves usually peak. Walk to your spot or unmute yourself, plant your feet, look at a friendly face, breathe out slowly, then say an opening line you know by heart. The short pause feels long to you but looks calm and confident to everyone else.

Know your one message and use a simple structure

Much of speaking fear is really fear of not knowing what to say. Clarity fixes a lot of it. Before you write anything, finish this sentence: "If people remember only one thing, it should be..." That is your one message, and every part of the talk should support it.

Then pick one of three simple shapes:

  • Problem, solution, next step: for proposals, requests and pitches. Spend most of your time making people feel the problem.
  • Past, present, future: for updates and talks about change.
  • Three points: for explaining or teaching. Say the three points up front, cover each, then repeat them at the end.

For example, a 90-second request for a second office printer could run: "We lose about forty minutes a day waiting at one printer. A second machine upstairs would end that. I need approval by Friday to order it." Keep it shorter than you think. Nobody complains when a talk ends a few minutes early.

How to rehearse a presentation the right way

Reading your notes silently is not rehearsal. Your mouth, voice and timing need practice too. Stand up, speak out loud and time yourself honestly.

  • Spread practice over several days. Short, spaced runs beat one long cram the night before.
  • Record at least one run on your phone. Listen an hour later and note one thing that went well and one thing to change.
  • Do one run for a friend and ask what they remember and where they got lost.
  • Memorize your opening only, so the hardest first minute runs on autopilot. Work from a short outline of key words for the rest.
  • On the day itself, say your opening twice and stop rehearsing. Over-rehearsing in the final hours tends to raise anxiety.

When you speak, go slower than feels right. Adrenaline speeds you up and distorts your sense of time, so a pace that feels slightly slow to you usually sounds just right. Pause after key points. A pause looks confident, not lost.

What to do if you go blank while speaking

Going blank happens to experienced speakers too, and the audience almost never notices as much as you think. First, pause and take one slow breath out. A few seconds of silence looks thoughtful from the outside. Then use one of these recovery moves:

  • Repeat your last sentence. It often brings the next one back and sounds like emphasis.
  • Summarize what you have covered so far, which usually leads you into the next point.
  • Check your notes calmly. That is what they are for.
  • Skip ahead. Nobody else knows your plan.
  • Be honest: "I've lost my thread for a moment, let me check my notes." Said calmly, it sounds human.

A sip of water also buys you a natural few seconds. If someone asks a question you cannot answer, say so and offer to follow up, which builds more trust than guessing.

Build confidence with small steps

Confidence follows action, not the other way around. The most reliable way to get less afraid is many small, manageable speaking reps.

Write a ladder of 5 to 8 speaking situations, from least scary to most scary. For example: asking one question in a small team meeting, giving a two-minute update, presenting a short item to 30 people, running a training session, presenting to senior leaders. Stay on each rung until it feels manageable, then step up. Slipping back a rung after a rough day is normal.

Speak early in meetings, within the first few minutes, so the tension does not build while you wait. Look for low-pressure groups where people practice speaking together. After each talk, write down what went well and one thing to try next time, so you can see your progress. These tools are for the everyday nerves most people feel. If anxiety about speaking (or anything else) is overwhelming, causes panic attacks, keeps you from things that matter or affects your sleep and daily life, please talk to a doctor or a qualified therapist. Anxiety is very treatable, and support works well alongside practical tools.

Frequently asked questions

Can fear of public speaking be cured?

For most people, the fear does not vanish completely, but it becomes small and manageable. Many confident speakers still feel butterflies. What changes is how quickly you settle and how well you speak despite the nerves. Regular small practice, a calming routine and solid preparation make the biggest difference. If anxiety is severe, a doctor or therapist can help.

How do I stop my hands and voice from shaking when presenting?

Use up the extra energy first: shake out your hands, roll your shoulders and clench and release your fists before you start. Then do a few slow breaths with a long exhale. While speaking, hold something steady such as your notes, plant your feet and slow down. Shaking is usually far less visible to listeners than it feels to you.

What should I say at the start of a presentation?

Open with a line you have memorized and practiced until it feels easy. A short, relevant story, a surprising number or a question your audience cares about all work well. Before you speak, pause, plant your feet, look at a friendly face and breathe out slowly. Then deliver your opening and move straight to your one message.

How many times should I rehearse a presentation?

For a 10 to 20 minute talk, aim for about four or five full runs out loud spread over several days, including one recorded run and one in front of a friend. The day before, do one relaxed run plus your opening and closing. On the day itself, just say your opening twice and stop rehearsing.

Is it okay to use notes when speaking?

Yes. Notes are a sensible safety net, not a sign of weakness. Use a short outline of key words rather than full paragraphs, because key words are much easier to scan under stress. Memorize only your opening line. Glancing down at your notes to find the next point looks natural to an audience.

Why do I talk so fast when I am nervous?

Adrenaline speeds up your body and distorts your sense of time, so your normal pace feels slow and pauses feel endless. Listeners are hearing your ideas for the first time and need you to slow down. Mark two or three places in your notes where you tend to rush, and slow down most on numbers and key points.

Start today with the slow exhale: breathe in for 4 and out for 6 to 8, five rounds, once a day this week. Then use it right before your next meeting and say one thing early, even a single question. Small reps like that are how lasting confidence is built.
