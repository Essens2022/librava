How to Start a Raised Bed Garden: A Simple Beginner's Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have tried a few pots or a weedy plot and given up, a raised bed is one of the easiest ways to grow your own vegetables. The short answer: choose a level spot with at least 6 to 8 hours of sun near a tap, build one bed about 4 by 8 ft (1.2 by 2.4 m) and 10 to 12 inches (25 to 30 cm) deep, fill it with roughly 60 percent topsoil and 40 percent compost, then plant cool-season crops before your last frost and warm-season crops after it.

This guide walks you through each step for gardeners in the US and the UK: siting, building, filling, timing, spacing, watering and the most common beginner mistakes. One well-planned bed can keep you picking something from late spring into autumn.

Why start with a raised bed?

Raised beds solve many of the problems that make beginners quit. The soil warms up and dries out earlier in spring, so you can plant sooner. Drainage is better, so roots do not sit in cold, wet ground. You start with clean soil, so there are far fewer weeds, and because you never walk on the bed, the soil stays loose with no digging at all.

They are also kinder to backs and knees, tidy to look at, and productive, because you can plant in a close grid instead of long rows with paths between them.

There are honest downsides. Building and filling a bed costs money up front, mostly for soil. Raised beds also dry out faster in hot weather, so you will water more often than an in-ground plot. Both are easy to plan for once you know.

Pick the right spot for your raised bed

Sun comes first. Fruiting crops such as tomatoes, peppers, zucchini (courgette) and beans need at least 6 to 8 hours of direct sun a day. Watch your yard or garden at 9 a.m., noon and 4 p.m. to see where the light really falls. Leafy greens, herbs and many roots will manage with a little less.

Then check the practical points:

  • Water within easy reach, ideally a tap close enough for a short hose.
  • Level ground, or plan to level the bed itself.
  • Some shelter from strong wind.
  • Away from large trees, whose roots will move into rich bed soil.
  • Close to the kitchen door if you can, because a bed you pass every day gets looked after.

Start with one or two beds. It is far better to manage one bed well in your first year than to struggle with four. Leave paths wide enough for a wheelbarrow, about 2 ft (60 cm) or more.

How to build a raised garden bed

A common size is 4 by 8 ft (about 1.2 by 2.4 m). The key rule is width: never make a bed wider than you can comfortably reach the middle, about 4 ft (1.2 m) if you can reach from both sides or 2 ft (60 cm) against a wall or fence. A depth of 10 to 12 inches (25 to 30 cm) is enough for most vegetables.

Untreated wood such as cedar or larchEasy to cut, natural lookLasts years, not decades
Galvanized steelLong lasting, slim wallsCan heat up in strong sun
Stone or brickVery durableMore work and cost

A simple weekend build:

  1. Mark out the rectangle with string and stakes.
  2. Cut the grass short and remove any tough perennial weeds.
  3. Screw the boards to a short post in each corner.
  4. Set the frame in place and check it is level.
  5. Lay flattened cardboard over the grass inside the frame, overlapping the edges well, then wet it.
Avoid old railway sleepers (railroad ties) and any wood treated with creosote for food beds. Wear gloves and eye protection when cutting, and lift heavy bags and boards with bent knees and a straight back, or get a helper.

What soil to put in a raised bed

A simple, widely used mix is about 60 percent topsoil and 40 percent compost by volume. The topsoil gives body and minerals; the compost adds food, softness and water-holding power. If your topsoil is heavy, try roughly 50 percent topsoil, 30 percent compost and 20 percent coarse material such as fine bark or grit. Ready-made raised bed mixes also work; just ask what is in them.

Do the sum before you order. A 4 by 8 ft bed that is 1 ft deep needs 4 x 8 x 1 = 32 cubic feet, about 0.9 cubic meters or roughly 900 liters. Order 10 to 15 percent extra, because new soil settles.

  • Do not fill a bed with potting mix alone; it shrinks and dries out fast.
  • For a deeper bed on a budget, fill the bottom with logs, branches and garden waste, then add at least 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) of finished soil and compost on top. Raw material close to the roots ties up nitrogen and stunts plants.
  • Top up with 2 to 3 inches (about 5 cm) of compost every year.

Wear gloves when handling manure-based products, and wash your hands afterward.

When to plant in a raised bed

Your planting calendar depends on two dates for your area: the average last spring frost and the first autumn frost. In the US, you can find them from your USDA hardiness zone, your local extension office or a frost date lookup. In the UK, the last frost is often in April or May depending on region; local allotment societies and national gardening organizations publish guides.

GroupCropsWhen to plant
Cool-seasonLettuce, spinach, peas, radishes, carrots, beets, onions, cabbage familyFrom a few weeks before the last frost
Warm-seasonTomatoes, peppers, squash, cucumbers, beans, basilAfter the last frost, once soil is warm

Do not rush tender plants out on the first warm weekend. Nights below about 50°F (10°C) can stunt tomatoes, peppers and basil, and a plant set out two weeks later into warm soil often overtakes the early one. Harden off young plants first by putting them outside for a few hours a day, building up over a week or so.

How many plants per square foot

The square grid method makes planning simple. Divide the bed into 1-foot (30 cm) squares, so a 4 by 8 bed has 32 squares, and plant each square with a set number of plants. These are good starting points:

Tomato, pepper, eggplant (aubergine), broccoli, cabbage1
Lettuce, chard, basil, parsley4
Cucumber (on a trellis)2

Put tall crops and trellises on the north side of the bed so they do not shade the rest. A simple first-year layout: two tomatoes and a cucumber trellis along the north edge, peppers and basil in front, one zucchini sprawling over a corner, then bush beans, chard and kale, with lettuce, spinach, beets, onions, carrots and radishes filling the sunny southern half.

Seeds or young plants?

Some crops are easiest sown straight into the bed: radishes, carrots, beets, peas, beans, spinach and lettuce. Carrots in particular dislike being moved. Others need a long, warm start, so most beginners buy them as young plants or start them indoors on a sunny windowsill: tomatoes, peppers, eggplant and, often, the cabbage family.

When buying young plants, choose sturdy, dark green ones with no yellow leaves, no pests under the leaves and no roots circling thickly out of the pot. Short and stocky beats tall and leggy. Sow seeds at about two to three times their own width deep, keep the surface damp until they sprout, and label every square so you know what is coming up.

How often to water a raised bed

A good rule of thumb is about 1 inch (2.5 cm) of water a week, from rain and watering together, and more in hot or windy weather. Raised beds drain well, so check them often in summer.

  • Use the finger test: push a finger into the soil up to the second knuckle. If it feels dry at that depth, water.
  • Water deeply at the base of the plants, not over the leaves, ideally in the morning.
  • A few deep soaks a week grow deeper roots than a quick sprinkle every day.
  • Drip lines or soaker hoses laid along the rows save a lot of time.
  • Mulch with straw, compost or shredded leaves to keep moisture in and weeds down.

Steady watering also prevents blossom end rot, the dark sunken patch at the bottom of tomatoes, which is linked to uneven moisture. For feeding, compost does most of the work; heavy feeders such as tomatoes benefit from a tomato feed once they start to flower, following the label.

For pests, start gently: hand-pick slugs and caterpillars, use netting or row covers against birds and cabbage white butterflies, and encourage helpful insects with flowers such as marigolds. If you use slug pellets, choose pet-safe ones and keep children and pets away. The book The Easy Raised Bed Vegetable Garden covers pests, supports and autumn beds in more detail.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best size for a raised garden bed?

A bed about 4 by 8 ft (1.2 by 2.4 m) is a popular first size. It gives you 32 square feet to plant and you can reach the middle from both sides. If the bed sits against a wall or fence, keep it about 2 ft (60 cm) wide. Length matters less; just leave paths wide enough for a wheelbarrow.

How deep should a raised bed be for vegetables?

A depth of 10 to 12 inches (25 to 30 cm) is enough for most vegetables, including tomatoes, beans, lettuce and most root crops, especially when the bed sits on loosened ground covered with cardboard. Deeper beds are easier on your back but need much more soil, so fill the lower part with logs and garden waste to save money.

What do you put in the bottom of a raised garden bed?

On grass, lay flattened cardboard, with tape and labels removed, overlapping the edges well, then wet it. It smothers the grass and breaks down within months. In deeper beds, you can add logs, branches and garden waste above it, as long as at least 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) of good soil and compost sits on top.

How much soil do I need for a 4 by 8 raised bed?

Multiply length by width by depth. A 4 by 8 ft bed that is 12 inches deep needs 32 cubic feet, about 1.2 cubic yards or 0.9 cubic meters (roughly 900 liters). A 10-inch bed needs about 27 cubic feet. Order 10 to 15 percent more, since new soil settles, and consider buying in bulk once you need more than a bed or two.

What should a beginner plant in a raised bed?

Start with crops you like to eat that are reliable: lettuce, radishes, spinach, peas, bush beans, zucchini (courgette), tomatoes, chard, kale, beets and basil. Mix quick cool-season crops for spring with a few warm-season favorites for summer. Planting things you will actually cook means you visit the bed daily, which is when problems get spotted early.

Do raised beds need to be watered every day?

Usually not. Aim for about 1 inch (2.5 cm) of water a week, given as a few deep soakings rather than daily sprinkles. In hot, dry or windy weather, raised beds dry faster and may need water more often. Use the finger test, and add mulch to keep moisture in and cut how often you need to water.

Your first step this weekend: look up your average last spring frost date, then watch where the sun falls at 9 a.m., noon and 4 p.m. and mark out a 4 by 8 ft rectangle in the sunniest level spot near a tap. Write down five vegetables you love to eat, and you already have the start of a plan.
