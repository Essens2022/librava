How to Set Up a Fish Tank for Beginners: A Step-by-Step Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have just bought a tank, or are standing in the store wondering where to begin, the most important thing to know is that fish should not go in on setup day. The short answer: choose a tank of at least 20 gallons (75 liters), set it up with a filter, heater and conditioned water, cycle it for about 4 to 8 weeks until it turns ammonia into nitrate within 24 hours, then add a few hardy fish and change 20 to 30 percent of the water every week.

This guide takes you through each stage in order, with a shopping checklist, setup steps, a cycling plan, a beginner fish table and a weekly routine that takes about an hour. Follow it and your first fish have a far better chance of living a long, healthy life.

What size fish tank is best for beginners?

Bigger is easier. A larger volume of water dilutes waste and holds its temperature, so mistakes happen slowly instead of overnight. For a first tropical community tank, aim for at least 20 gallons (about 75 liters).

Small bowls and tiny tanks are poor homes. They swing in temperature, build up waste fast and usually have no room for a filter or heater. A betta needs at least 5 gallons (20 liters), heated and filtered. Fancy goldfish need a large tank, often 30 gallons (115 liters) or more for the first one, and common goldfish belong in a pond.

Forget the old inch of fish per gallon rule. It ignores how much waste a fish produces (its bioload), how big it grows as an adult and how much swimming space it needs. A thick-bodied fish makes far more waste than a slim fish of the same length.

Water weighs about 8.3 lb per US gallon (1 kg per liter). A full 20 gallon tank with gravel and rock weighs well over 200 lb, so use a proper aquarium stand on a solid, level floor, ideally near a wall.

Fish tank setup checklist

Take this list to the store so you buy everything at once.

  • Glass tank of 20 gallons (75 liters) or more, with a well-fitting lid to stop jumpers
  • Aquarium stand rated for the full weight
  • Filter rated for your tank size or slightly larger
  • Adjustable heater with a thermostat, plus a separate thermometer
  • LED aquarium light and a plug-in timer
  • Substrate about 2 in (5 cm) deep (sand if you want corydoras)
  • Driftwood, smooth stones or caves for hiding places
  • A liquid test kit for ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH
  • Water conditioner that treats both chlorine and chloramine
  • A gravel vacuum (siphon) and two buckets used only for the aquarium
  • An algae scraper, a soft net and old towels
  • GFCI (US) or RCD (UK) protection and a power strip mounted off the floor
  • A pure ammonia source for fishless cycling

Liquid test kits are more accurate than strips. For a coldwater tank you can skip the heater but keep the thermometer.

Where to put your aquarium

A full tank cannot be moved, so choose the spot carefully before anything goes in.

  • Out of direct sunlight, which heats the water and feeds algae
  • Away from radiators, heaters, air vents and drafty doors
  • Close to a protected outlet (socket), with no extension cords across the room
  • On a solid floor, with space behind for cables and about 12 in (30 cm) above to reach in
  • Away from busy hallways and slamming doors that startle fish

Check the stand with a spirit level front to back and side to side, and shim the stand, never the tank. With a secondhand tank, fill it a third of the way with plain water and leave it for a few hours to check for leaks. Finding a drip now costs you an hour instead of a carpet.

How to set up a fish tank step by step

  1. Rinse the substrate. Stir gravel or sand in an aquarium bucket with tap water and pour off the cloudy water until it runs mostly clear. Do not pour sand down a drain.
  2. Spoon it in about 2 in (5 cm) deep at the front, sloping to 3 in (7.5 cm) at the back.
  3. Place rocks and wood. Set heavy rocks on the glass or a thin layer of gravel so they cannot topple. Soak driftwood first so it sinks, and expect a harmless tea tint from tannins.
  4. Fill halfway by pouring gently onto a plate or your hand, and add water conditioner dosed for the whole tank.
  5. Plant rooted plants with the crown just above the gravel. Tie java fern and anubias to wood or rock, because burying their thick stem (rhizome) makes it rot.
  6. Fit the filter and heater, with the thermometer at the far end. Fill to the top.
  7. Make a drip loop in every cable, a U-shaped dip below the socket so water drips onto the floor instead of into the plug.
  8. Plug in the filter first. Wait 15 to 20 minutes before plugging in the heater, then set it to about 77 to 78°F (25 to 26°C) for tropical fish. Put the light on a timer for 6 to 8 hours a day.

Never plug in a heater while it is out of the water, and unplug everything before your hands go in the tank. Then let it run empty for a day or two and check the temperature holds steady and nothing leaks.

How to cycle a fish tank before adding fish

Fish waste and leftover food make ammonia, which is toxic. Beneficial bacteria turn ammonia into nitrite, also toxic, and a second group turns nitrite into nitrate, which is far less harmful and is removed with water changes. A new tank has none of these bacteria, which is why clear water is not the same as a safe tank.

Fishless cycling grows the bacteria before any fish move in:

  1. Test your tap water and write down the results.
  2. Keep the filter running nonstop and the heater at about 77 to 82°F (25 to 28°C).
  3. Add pure ammonia a few drops at a time until the test reads about 2 ppm. Note how many drops it took.
  4. Test every two or three days. When ammonia drops near 0, dose back up to 2 ppm.
  5. Nitrite will rise, often staying high for a week or more, then fall as nitrate rises.
  6. When a full dose shows 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite 24 hours later, two or three days in a row, the tank is cycled.

Then do a large water change of 50 to 75 percent to bring nitrate under 20 ppm, and add your first small group of fish within a day or two. If nothing happens for weeks, check the temperature and pH. In soft water the pH can crash below about 6.5 and stall the bacteria, and a 30 to 50 percent water change usually restarts things. Bottled bacteria starters can help, but trust your test results, not the label.

Keep household ammonia in its labeled bottle, out of reach of children and pets, and never mix it with bleach or other cleaners. Use ammonia with no added soap or scent.

Best fish for a beginner tank

Pick fish for their adult size, not their shop size, and match them to your tap water instead of chasing numbers with chemicals. These are hardy choices for a cycled 20 gallon (75 liter) tank unless noted.

A sample first stocking for soft to moderate water in 20 gallons: 8 harlequin rasboras, 6 corydoras on sand, 1 honey gourami and 2 nerite snails. Add them in small groups over several weeks so the bacteria keep up.

Leave these in the store for now: common plecos (they reach 18 to 24 in), iridescent sharks, oscars and other large cichlids, red-tail sharks, dyed fish and goldfish mixed with tropical fish.

How to add new fish to your tank

  1. Turn the tank light off and dim the room.
  2. Float the sealed bag for 15 to 20 minutes to match temperature.
  3. Open the bag and add about a quarter cup (60 ml) of tank water every 5 minutes, four to six times.
  4. Net the fish gently into the tank and pour the bag water down a drain, never into your aquarium.
  5. Keep the light off for the rest of the day.

A small separate quarantine tank, where new fish spend a few weeks before joining the others, catches illness before it spreads. Never release unwanted fish or plants into ponds, rivers or lakes, and never flush them. It harms wild fish and is illegal in many places.

Weekly fish tank maintenance in about an hour

Feed small amounts once or twice a day, only what the fish finish in 1 to 2 minutes, and remove leftovers. Overfeeding is behind a lot of cloudy water and ammonia spikes.

  1. Test the water, especially nitrate, which should stay under 20 ppm and never over 40.
  2. Unplug the heater, and the filter if the water will drop below its intake.
  3. Scrape algae off the inside glass.
  4. Vacuum a third to a half of the gravel while removing 20 to 30 percent of the water.
  5. Match new water to the tank temperature within about 2°F (1°C) and add conditioner.
  6. Pour it in gently, restart the filter, and plug in the heater after 15 minutes.

Every 2 to 4 weeks, swish the filter sponge in a bucket of removed tank water. Never rinse filter media under the tap, since chlorine kills the bacteria you worked so hard to grow. If ammonia or nitrite ever reads above 0, change 30 to 50 percent of the water right away and test again the next day.

Aquarium water can occasionally cause infections in people, especially through cuts. Never start a siphon by mouth, cover cuts or wear long gloves, wash your hands with soap after tank work and supervise children. If a cut near the tank becomes red, swollen or slow to heal, see your doctor and mention the aquarium.

Frequently asked questions

How long should you wait before putting fish in a new tank?

Wait until the tank is cycled, which usually takes 4 to 8 weeks with fishless cycling. The tank is ready when it can turn a dose of about 2 ppm ammonia into 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite within 24 hours, two or three days in a row. Running the filter for a day or two only lets you check the equipment. It does not make the water safe for fish.

Can I put fish in a tank the same day?

It is best not to. A brand new tank has none of the bacteria that break down fish waste, so ammonia builds up within days and can poison the fish. This is often called new tank syndrome. If you already have fish in an uncycled tank, test daily and change 30 to 50 percent of the water whenever ammonia or nitrite reads above 0, and feed very lightly.

Do I need a heater in my fish tank?

Most popular beginner fish are tropical and need a steady 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C), so yes, use an adjustable heater with a thermostat and a separate thermometer. A few coldwater-tolerant fish, such as white cloud mountain minnows, live happily in an unheated tank in a room that stays roughly 64 to 72°F (18 to 22°C). Goldfish also prefer cooler water.

How often should I change the water in my fish tank?

Change about 20 to 30 percent of the water once a week in a typical cycled community tank. Vacuum part of the gravel at the same time, match the new water's temperature and treat it with conditioner for chlorine and chloramine. If your nitrate test creeps above 20 ppm before the week is up, change a little more water or change it more often.

Is tap water safe for a fish tank?

Tap water is fine once you treat it with a water conditioner that neutralizes chlorine and chloramine, both of which harm fish and filter bacteria. Test your tap water once so you know its pH and hardness, then choose fish that suit it. Chasing different numbers with chemicals usually causes more swings and stress than simply matching fish to the water you have.

Why is my new fish tank cloudy?

Cloudiness in the first day or two is usually fine dust from the substrate, and it settles or filters out on its own. Rinsing gravel well before setup prevents most of it. If a new tank turns milky later, it is often a bacterial bloom linked to cycling or overfeeding. Test ammonia and nitrite, feed less and avoid scrubbing the filter while it clears.

Your first step today is to choose the spot and write down your tank size before you buy a single fish. Then set the tank up, start fishless cycling and keep a simple log of every test result, because patience in these first few weeks is what keeps fish alive.
