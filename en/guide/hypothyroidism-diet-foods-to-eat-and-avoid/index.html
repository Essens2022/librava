Hypothyroidism Diet: Foods to Eat, Foods to Avoid and Myths

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have just been told your thyroid is underactive, the internet probably handed you a long list of foods to fear. The short answer: there is no special hypothyroidism diet, and very few foods need avoiding. A balanced, fiber-rich diet supports your health, and the things that matter most are timing your thyroid tablet well and skipping extra iodine products such as kelp.

This guide explains what to eat, what genuinely needs care around your medicine, the truth about broccoli, soy, gluten and iodine, and simple meal ideas for days when your energy is low. It also lists the signs that mean you should call your doctor.

What hypothyroidism means for your food

Your thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland in your neck. It makes hormones that set how fast your body uses energy, a bit like the dial on a central heating system. Hypothyroidism means it makes too little hormone, so things slow down: tiredness that sleep does not fix, feeling cold, constipation, dry skin, low mood, brain fog and weight gain are common.

In countries where people get enough iodine, the most common cause is Hashimoto's thyroiditis, an autoimmune condition where the immune system gradually damages the thyroid. It is diagnosed with blood tests, mainly TSH and free T4, and usually treated with a daily thyroid hormone tablet such as levothyroxine.

No food, diet, supplement or herbal remedy can replace thyroid hormone or cure Hashimoto's. What food can do is support steady energy, regular digestion and heart health while your medicine does its job. That matters, because untreated hypothyroidism can raise cholesterol and constipation is common.

The best foods for hypothyroidism

The eating pattern that suits most adults suits you too. Picture a dinner plate split roughly like this:

  • About half vegetables, cooked or raw, plus some fruit across the day.
  • About a quarter protein: chicken, fish, eggs, beans, lentils, tofu, lean meat or cheese.
  • About a quarter starchy food, ideally whole grain: oats, brown rice, whole wheat (wholemeal) pasta, barley, whole grain bread or potatoes with skins.
  • A little healthy fat: olive oil, nuts, seeds or avocado.

A few food groups deserve a special place:

  • Fiber-rich foods such as beans, lentils, oats, vegetables and whole grains, which help with the constipation many people have.
  • Oily fish such as salmon or mackerel about twice a week, which supports heart health.
  • Dairy, eggs and fish, which supply iodine naturally as part of a normal varied diet.

A soup with bread, a stew, a curry or a sandwich with a side salad all count. You do not need to measure anything.

Foods to avoid with hypothyroidism

Here is the honest list. Very few foods need avoiding altogether. Most of the care is about timing and amounts.

Food or productWhat to do
Kelp, seaweed tablets, iodine dropsAvoid unless your doctor advises them
Coffee, tea, milkFine, but not at the same time as your tablet
Calcium and iron supplements, antacidsUsually at least 4 hours apart from your tablet
Soy foods, high-fiber meals, grapefruit juiceFine, but keep amounts steady day to day
Biotin supplementsTell your doctor, as biotin can upset blood test results
Processed meats, fried food, salty snacksOccasional extras, for heart health

The one to take seriously is extra iodine. Your thyroid needs iodine, but most people in the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand get enough from ordinary food. Too much can make thyroid problems worse, including Hashimoto's. Many "thyroid support" products contain kelp or iodine, so read labels. Occasional sushi wrapped in nori is fine, and there is no need to add extra salt for iodine.

How to eat around your thyroid medication

How you take your tablet affects how much of it your body absorbs, and that affects your blood test results and dose. The usual advice:

  1. Take your tablet with a full glass of water, first thing in the morning, on an empty stomach.
  2. Wait 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, coffee, tea or milk.
  3. Take calcium, iron, antacids and multivitamins with minerals at least 4 hours later.
  4. Do it the same way every day.

Some doctors prefer a bedtime dose, about 3 to 4 hours after your last meal. Both can work. Consistency matters most, because your doctor sets your dose based on how your body absorbs the tablet under your normal routine.

A sample morning: tablet with water at 6:15, shower and get dressed, breakfast and coffee at 7:00, any calcium or iron supplement your doctor advised from 11:00. Keeping the tablet box beside a clean glass by the kettle helps you see it before you reach for coffee.

Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before changing your diet in a big way or starting any supplement, and never change your dose, brand or timing on your own. Your leaflet and your own care team's advice always come first. Ask your pharmacist to check the timing if you take several medicines.

Broccoli, soy and gluten myths

Some of the most repeated advice online is also the least helpful.

Broccoli, cabbage and kale

Cruciferous vegetables contain natural substances that, in very large amounts, can interfere with how the thyroid uses iodine. In practice, problems are linked to very large amounts of raw vegetables or juices in people who also lack iodine. Normal portions, especially cooked, are healthy and fine. Roast that broccoli.

Soy

Tofu, edamame and soy milk are fine in normal food amounts. Keep your intake fairly steady and keep your gap after the tablet. If you start drinking soy milk every day, mention it to your doctor so they can recheck your levels if needed.

Gluten

A gluten-free diet is only needed for celiac (coeliac) disease. Celiac disease is more common with autoimmune thyroid disease, so if you have ongoing diarrhea, bloating, unexplained weight loss or anemia, ask about testing. Keep eating gluten until you are tested, because cutting it out first can make results unreliable. Outside celiac disease, there is no good evidence that going gluten-free helps Hashimoto's.

Fiber, fluids and constipation

Constipation is common with an underactive thyroid and can linger after treatment starts. Fiber and fluids are the two simplest helpers.

  • Include some fiber at every meal: vegetables, fruit, beans, lentils, oats, barley, brown rice, whole grain bread and potatoes with skins.
  • Increase fiber gradually over a few weeks to avoid bloating and wind.
  • Drink plenty of fluids as you go, since fiber works best with water.
  • Take a short walk after dinner. Gentle movement helps digestion.

Once you find a level that suits you, keep it fairly consistent, because a big jump in fiber can change how well your tablet is absorbed. See your doctor if constipation is severe, sudden or painful, comes with blood or weight loss, or lasts more than a few weeks.

Easy meals when your energy is low

Tired evenings are when good intentions turn into toast. Plan for them on purpose.

  • Cook once, eat three times. On a good day, make a double batch of soup or stew, eat one portion and freeze the rest in labeled containers.
  • Keep shortcuts without guilt: frozen vegetables, canned (tinned) beans, ready-grated cheese and bags of salad.
  • Choose one-pot and one-tray meals on tired days, and sit on a stool to chop.
  • If Thursday is always your worst day, make it a freezer night or sandwich night in advance.

A week of simple ideas might include: savory oatmeal (porridge) with spinach and an egg, red lentil and sweet potato soup, baked salmon with roasted broccoli and potatoes, turkey and bean chili from the freezer, a Spanish potato and onion omelet, a chickpea and roasted vegetable tray bake, and a classic cottage pie.

For a quick tray bake, toss chickpeas, chunks of pepper, red onion and sweet potato in olive oil, cumin and paprika and roast at 400°F (200°C) for about 30 minutes, then serve with a spoon of yogurt. Hypothyroidism, weight and supplements

Some weight gained because of an underactive thyroid often comes off once levels are treated, but not always and not for everyone. A sensible approach is regular meals with protein and fiber, gentle movement you enjoy and patience while your dose settles, rather than crash diets.

Selenium, zinc, vitamin D, "adrenal support" products and "thyroid support" blends are widely promoted. The evidence for most is limited or mixed, some can be harmful in excess, and some interact with medicines or affect blood tests. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before starting any of them. Strict elimination diets for autoimmune conditions are best tried only with a doctor or registered dietitian, so you do not miss important nutrients.

If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, tell your doctor straight away. Thyroid hormone needs often rise very early in pregnancy, and iodine advice depends on your situation.

When to call your doctor

Contact your doctor if symptoms come back or get worse, or if you notice signs your dose may be off:

  • Possibly too high: racing or irregular heartbeat, shakiness, anxiety, trouble sleeping, feeling hot and sweaty, diarrhea or weight loss you did not intend.
  • Possibly too low: worsening tiredness, feeling cold more often, returning constipation or old symptoms coming back.
  • A lump or swelling in your neck, or hoarseness lasting more than a few weeks.

Get urgent medical help for chest pain; a very fast or irregular heartbeat with dizziness, fainting or shortness of breath; neck swelling that makes it hard to swallow or breathe; or severe confusion, extreme drowsiness or a very low body temperature. In an emergency call 911 in the US or 999 in the UK.

Frequently asked questions

What foods should I avoid with hypothyroidism?

Very few foods need avoiding. Skip kelp, seaweed tablets and iodine drops unless your doctor advises them, because extra iodine can make thyroid problems worse. Do not eat or drink coffee, milk or breakfast at the same time as your tablet, and keep calcium and iron supplements at least 4 hours apart. Soy, fiber and cooked cruciferous vegetables are fine in normal, steady amounts.

Is broccoli bad for hypothyroidism?

No. Broccoli, cabbage, kale and cauliflower are healthy and fine in normal portions, especially cooked. Problems have only been linked to very large amounts of raw vegetables or juices in people who also lack iodine. These vegetables bring fiber and vitamins, which help with the constipation and heart health concerns common with an underactive thyroid, so there is no need to leave them off your plate.

How long after taking levothyroxine can I eat?

The usual advice is to wait 30 to 60 minutes after taking your tablet with water before breakfast, coffee, tea or milk. Calcium, iron, antacids and some other medicines are usually taken at least 4 hours apart. Some people take their tablet at bedtime instead, about 3 to 4 hours after the last meal. Follow your own doctor's or pharmacist's advice and keep the routine the same every day.

Should I go gluten-free for Hashimoto's?

Only if you have celiac disease. Celiac disease is more common in people with autoimmune thyroid disease, so ask your doctor about testing if you have ongoing diarrhea, bloating, unexplained weight loss or anemia. Keep eating gluten until you have been tested, or the result may be unreliable. For people without celiac disease, there is no good evidence that cutting out gluten helps Hashimoto's.

Do I need iodine supplements for my thyroid?

Most people do not. In the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, a normal varied diet with milk, yogurt, eggs and fish usually supplies enough iodine. Extra iodine from kelp, seaweed tablets or drops can make hypothyroidism and Hashimoto's worse. Pregnancy and breastfeeding raise iodine needs, so if that applies to you, ask your doctor or midwife rather than starting supplements on your own.

Can diet cure hypothyroidism?

No. No diet, food or supplement can replace thyroid hormone or cure Hashimoto's, and you should never stop your medicine because you have changed how you eat. A balanced, fiber-rich diet supports steady energy, regular digestion and heart health alongside treatment. If you still feel unwell even though your blood tests look normal, go back to your doctor, as another cause may be worth checking.

Your first step today is simple: write down when you take your tablet, eat breakfast and have your first hot drink, then ask your pharmacist to check the gaps. Once that routine is steady, build your meals around vegetables, protein and fiber-rich carbs, and keep a couple of freezer meals ready for tired days.
