How to Lower Your Energy Bills: Free Fixes First, Then Upgrades

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your last energy bill made you wince, you are far from alone, and you probably do not need to sit in a cold house to bring it down. The quickest way to lower your energy bills is to change free habits first (thermostat down one degree, heating timers matched to when you are home, standby switched off, full loads, shorter showers), then seal drafts and top up loft insulation, and only then think about bigger upgrades.

That order matters, because free and cheap fixes pay off at once and make every later upgrade work better. This guide takes you through each step for renters and homeowners, with checklists and safety rules so you save money without risking your health.

What uses the most energy in a home

In cold and hot climates, heating and cooling are usually the biggest share of home energy use, followed in many homes by heating water. Fridges and freezers run around the clock, tumble dryers and ovens use a lot while on, and lighting and gadgets are smaller but add up.

Start by reading your bill properly. Look for the units used (often kilowatt hours), the standing or fixed daily charge, and whether the reading was estimated or actual. Estimated bills can drift far from reality, so take your own meter reading and send it to your supplier.

Then read the meter at the same time each week for a month and jot it in a simple log. Comparing the same season year to year tells you far more than comparing January with June. Once you can see weekly use, you can measure whether each change below actually works.

Free ways to cut your energy bill this week

Before you spend anything, change the settings and habits that quietly drain money every day. Pick three from this list, make them stick, then add three more:

  • Turn the thermostat down about 1°F (or 1°C) and see if anyone notices.
  • Set the heating timer to warm the house shortly before you wake and come home, not hours ahead.
  • Lower the heating at night and when the house is empty.
  • Close curtains and blinds at dusk; open them in the morning for free sun.
  • Tuck long curtains behind radiators, not over them.
  • Turn radiators down to low in unused rooms and close the doors.
  • Move sofas and beds away from radiators and heating vents.
  • Time your hot water for when you need it.
  • Switch electronics off at the wall overnight.
  • Run the washing machine and dishwasher only when full.
  • Wash everyday laundry on cold or 30°C cycles.
  • Line dry or rack dry when you can.
  • Take shorter showers and boil only the water you need.

Turning the thermostat up high does not heat the house faster. Most systems heat at the same speed whatever the setting, so a higher number just means they run longer and overshoot.

How to save on heating without being cold

Never underheat your home to save money. Keep main living rooms at about 64 to 68°F (18 to 20°C), and warmer for babies, older people and anyone unwell; UK health guidance suggests at least 18°C. Cold, damp homes raise the risk of chest infections, heart problems and falls. If you cannot afford to heat your home, contact your energy supplier or utility and a local advice service early, and talk to your doctor if cold is affecting your health.

Within that healthy range, heat smarter. Thermostatic radiator valves let you keep bedrooms a little cooler than the living room. If you have a heat pump, steady settings or only small setbacks usually work best, so follow your installer's advice. With forced air heating, ask a professional before closing lots of vents.

Bleed radiators that are cold at the top, book an annual service for boilers, furnaces and other fuel-burning systems, and keep a working carbon monoxide alarm near any of them. Stay warm cheaply with layers, warm bedding, hot drinks and a blanket on the sofa.

Portable space heaters need care: keep them at least 3 feet (1 meter) from anything that can burn, plug them straight into a wall socket rather than an extension lead or power strip, and never leave them on while you sleep or go out. Never heat your home with a gas oven, stove, barbecue or generator.

Find drafts with a simple test

Drafts let warm air out and cold air in, and they are often easy to fix. Skip the old candle trick, which is a fire risk near curtains and dusty corners. Instead, on a cold or windy day:

  1. Close all outside doors and windows.
  2. Hold a hand-length strip of tissue loosely between two fingers, or dampen the back of your hand.
  3. Move slowly along the edges of windows, doors, the letterbox, keyholes, skirting boards (baseboards), floorboards, the loft hatch, outlets and pipe holes.
  4. If the tissue flutters or your hand feels cool, write it down and mark how strong it is.

Switching on kitchen and bathroom extractor fans during the test pulls air through gaps and makes leaks easier to feel; turn them off afterward. You will end up with a room-by-room draft map that tells you exactly what to buy.

Cheap draft-proofing that pays off fast

Most draft-proofing needs only a few low-cost supplies and an afternoon:

  • Self-adhesive weatherstripping around doors and opening windows.
  • A brush or flap cover for the letterbox and a cover for keyholes.
  • A draft excluder (door snake) along the bottom of doors.
  • Flexible filler or sealant for gaps in floorboards and along skirting boards.
  • Foam strips around the loft hatch, plus insulation on top of it.
  • A removable chimney balloon or cover for fireplaces you never use.
  • Foam pipe lagging on hot water pipes you can reach.
  • Window film as temporary secondary glazing on single-glazed windows.
Never block intentional ventilation. Leave wall vents, air bricks, trickle vents, extractor fans, vents for fuel-burning appliances and chimneys in use clear. Every home with a fuel-burning appliance or attached garage needs working carbon monoxide alarms, and you should test smoke and CO alarms monthly. After sealing, watch for condensation and open a window briefly or use extractor fans if it appears.

Loft and attic insulation explained

Attic (loft) insulation is often one of the most cost-effective jobs in a home. UK guidance commonly recommends about 270 mm (about 11 in) of mineral wool. US guidance suggests roughly R-30 to R-60 depending on your climate zone, and Australia and New Zealand set their own R-value recommendations by climate.

To check yours, look into the loft with a torch (flashlight) and measure the depth in a few places. If you can see the tops of the joists, there is probably room for more.

Topping up between and across joists is a job many people do themselves if they take care. Wear gloves, long sleeves, goggles and a dust mask; board a walkway and never step between joists; keep insulation clear of recessed lights not rated for contact, flues and eaves vents so the roof can still breathe.

If you find loose grey-brown or gold pebbly material, or anything you suspect is asbestos or vermiculite, do not disturb it. Get it tested by a professional. Wall, floor and solid wall insulation are usually jobs for professionals, and grants or rebates for them exist in many places but change often.

How to lower your electric bill

  • Switch to LEDs. They use roughly 75 to 90 percent less energy than old incandescent bulbs and last much longer. Start with the lights you use most.
  • Do a standby walk-around: TVs, consoles, speakers, set-top boxes, printers and chargers often draw power all day. Switch them off at the wall, or group them on a switched power strip. Leave fridges, freezers, alarms, medical equipment and anything else that needs constant power alone.
  • Keep the fridge at or below 40°F (4°C) and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C). Colder wastes energy. Clean the coils and test door seals by closing the door on a sheet of paper; if it slides out easily, the seal needs attention.
  • Cook efficiently with lids on pans and pans matched to the ring. Microwaves, slow cookers and small countertop ovens often use less than heating a full oven for a small meal.
  • Dry laundry outside or on a rack in a ventilated room. If you use a dryer, clean the lint filter every load, which also cuts fire risk.

For cooling, close curtains on sunny windows during the hottest hours, use fans where they keep you comfortable, open windows at night when it is cooler outside, set air conditioning a few degrees higher and clean its filters regularly. Ceiling fans should usually spin counterclockwise in summer; check the switch on yours.

Hot water savings that are safe

Water heating is a big cost in many homes. Shorter showers, a low-flow showerhead, fixing dripping hot taps (faucets) and lagging the tank and pipes all help.

Temperature needs balance. A storage water heater or hot water cylinder must be hot enough to control bacteria such as Legionella, and guidance commonly suggests storing water at about 140°F (60°C), with a mixing valve to reduce scald risk at the taps. US guidance often suggests 120°F (49°C) for scald safety where the system allows. Follow local guidance and your manufacturer rather than simply turning the dial down.

Tips for renters and bigger upgrades

Renters can usually do plenty without asking: thermostat and timer habits, curtains, draft excluders, removable weatherstripping, LED bulbs, standby switching and a low-flow showerhead you keep the old one for. For anything permanent, ask in writing. A calm request might be: "I've noticed a strong draft around the back door and the bedroom window. Could we arrange for them to be sealed before winter?" Some places have minimum energy standards for rentals, so check local rules.

Homeowners who have done the basics can then look at heat pumps, new windows, solar panels and batteries, wall insulation, efficient appliances, smart controls and time-of-use tariffs. None comes with a guaranteed payback time. Get several written quotes, ask installers how they sized the job, and check your government's current schemes, your utility or supplier and a local energy advice service for grants. Frequently asked questions

What uses the most electricity in a house?

In most homes, anything that heats or cools uses the most: electric heating, air conditioning, water heaters, tumble dryers and ovens. Fridges and freezers use steady power all day and night. Lighting and electronics are usually smaller but add up, especially old bulbs and devices left on standby. Weekly meter readings help you see which changes make the biggest difference in your home.

Is it cheaper to leave the heating on low all day?

For most boilers and furnaces, no. Lowering the heat at night and when everyone is out usually uses less energy than holding a constant temperature. Heat pumps are the main exception, because they generally work best with steady settings or small setbacks, so follow your installer's advice. Whatever system you have, keep living rooms at a healthy temperature when you are home.

Does turning off appliances at the wall save money?

Yes, a little per device and more across a household. Televisions, consoles, speakers, set-top boxes, printers and chargers can draw standby power around the clock. Switching them off at the wall overnight and when you go away is free. Leave fridges, freezers, alarms, medical equipment and anything that needs constant power switched on.

What temperature should I set my thermostat to save money?

Aim for the lowest comfortable setting within a healthy range, usually about 64 to 68°F (18 to 20°C) in main living rooms, and warmer for babies, older people or anyone unwell. Try lowering it by one degree for a week and see if anyone notices. Each small step down is a commonly cited easy saving, but never let your home get cold to save money.

How can renters lower their energy bills?

Renters can change heating and hot water timers, close curtains at dusk, use draft excluders and removable weatherstripping, swap to LED bulbs, cut standby power and take shorter showers without asking anyone. For repairs, insulation or new heating, write to your landlord with specific problems and dates. Some areas set minimum energy standards for rental homes, so check what applies locally.

Is draft-proofing worth it?

Usually, yes. Draft-proofing is one of the cheapest home improvements, often needs only basic supplies and an afternoon, and makes rooms feel warmer at the same thermostat setting. The key rule is never to block intentional ventilation such as wall vents, air bricks, trickle vents or extractor fans, and to keep working carbon monoxide alarms if you have fuel-burning appliances.

Your first step today is to take a meter reading, turn the thermostat down by one degree while staying in a healthy range, and pick two more free fixes from the list above. Read the meter again at the same time next week, and you will know exactly what your changes are worth before you spend anything on bigger jobs.
