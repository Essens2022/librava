How to Play Guitar for Beginners: Your First 30 Days, Step by Step

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have a guitar leaning in a corner, or you tried as a teenager and quietly gave up, you can still learn, and faster than you might expect. The short answer: tune the guitar, hold it relaxed, learn Em and Am first, then C, G, D and A, strum steady downstrokes while counting out loud, and practice 15 to 20 minutes a day with a timer. In about 30 days most adults can play simple songs from start to finish.

This guide walks you through each step: the setup that makes playing easier, tuning, your first chords, fixing buzzes, strumming patterns, changing chords in time, and a day-by-day plan. You do not need to read music or have a musical family.

What you need to start playing guitar

You need very little: a guitar, two or three thin or medium picks (plectrums, in the UK) and a tuner, either a small clip-on tuner or a tuner app on your phone. A strap helps if you want to stand. Electric players also need a small amp or headphones; start with a clean sound at low volume.

Any type of guitar works. Steel-string acoustics are the most common, nylon-string classical guitars are gentler on the fingertips, and electrics have lighter strings that are easy to press. Smaller adults may be more comfortable on a 3/4 size.

The single biggest upgrade for a cheap or secondhand guitar is a setup at a music shop. Lowering the action (the height of the strings above the neck) and fitting light strings can turn a guitar that hurts to play into one that feels easy.

If you are left-handed, left-handed guitars exist. In this guide, "fretting hand" means the hand on the neck and "strumming hand" means the hand over the soundhole or pickups, so it works either way.

How to hold a guitar and a pick

Sit on a chair without arms, with the guitar's body resting on your strumming-side leg and the neck angled slightly up. Your back is straight but not stiff, and your shoulders are loose. If you stand, adjust the strap so the guitar sits at about the same height as when you sit.

  • Your fretting-hand thumb rests behind the neck, roughly in the middle, without squeezing.
  • Arch your fingers so the knuckles curve up, as if you were holding a tennis ball.
  • Press with the very tips of your fingers, just behind the fret wire, not in the middle of the space.
  • Keep your fretting-hand fingernails short.
  • Hold the pick between the pad of your thumb and the side of your index finger, with only a small tip showing, and grip it loosely.

Tension is the enemy. If your wrist, shoulder or thumb feels tight, stop, shake out your hands and start again more gently.

How to tune a guitar for beginners

Standard tuning, from the thickest string to the thinnest, is E A D G B E. The thickest string is the 6th string and the thinnest is the 1st. A memory phrase helps: "Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie."

With a tuner, pluck one string, watch the display and turn its tuning peg slowly until the tuner shows the right note centered. Tune up to the note rather than down to it, because the string holds its pitch better that way. New strings stretch and drift for the first few days, so expect to retune often at first.

No tuner? Use the 5th-fret method once the 6th string is right. Press the 6th string at the 5th fret and match the open 5th string to it. Repeat for each pair of strings, with one exception: for the open 2nd string, press the 3rd string at the 4th fret, not the 5th. If you hear a wobble between the two notes, adjust the open string until the wobble slows and disappears.

Easy guitar chords for beginners to learn first

Start with chords that use few fingers, then build up. The code reads from the 6th string to the 1st: a number is the fret to press, 0 is an open string and x means do not play that string.

Em is the friendliest first chord. Put your middle finger on the 5th string and ring finger on the 4th string, both at the 2nd fret, and strum everything. G, C and D together cover a huge number of traditional songs, such as Amazing Grace.

How to fix buzzing strings and muted notes

Strumming hides problems. After you place any new chord, strum once, then pick each string slowly from thickest to thinnest and listen. It takes ten seconds and is the fastest way to clean chords.

What you hearLikely causeFix
Buzz on a pressed stringFinger too far from the fret or too lightSlide the tip toward the fret, press a little more
Thud on an open stringAnother finger leaning on itArch that finger and land on the tip
Everything sounds slightly offOut of tuneRetune
Constant buzz on one stringSetup problemAsk a music shop to check it

Do not fix every buzz by squeezing harder. If a finger is muting a neighbor, extra force flattens it even more. Check the position and angle first. To find how much pressure you really need, press so lightly that the strings buzz, then add pressure until the buzz just stops.

Beginner guitar strumming patterns

Counting is the secret skill. Most songs count in groups of four: 1, 2, 3, 4. Start with one downstroke on each count, called quarter notes (crotchets in the UK), at about one count per second, saying the numbers out loud.

Next, add upstrokes between the counts: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and", down on the numbers, up on the "and". These are eighth notes (quavers). Keep your strumming hand moving down and up all the time, like a pendulum, even when it misses the strings. That steady motion is what keeps you in time.

Then try the folk pattern, down, down up, up down up (D D U U D U):

  • Count 1: down, hit
  • And: up, miss
  • Count 2 and: down, up, both hit
  • Count 3: down, miss (the step that feels odd)
  • And, 4, and: up, down, up, all hit

For songs in 3/4 time, like Amazing Grace, count 1, 2, 3 and strum down on 1 with a little more weight, then lighter strums on 2 and 3. Practice any new pattern on a single chord until you can play it eight times in a row without thinking.

How to change chords smoothly and in time

Playing a chord and moving between chords are separate skills, and changes are what make songs possible. The one-minute changes drill builds speed quickly:

  1. Pick two chords, for example Em and Am.
  2. Set a timer for one minute.
  3. Play the first chord, strum once, switch, strum once, and keep going.
  4. Count every switch and write the number down.
  5. Try to beat it tomorrow.

Eight to ten changes on day one is normal. Many beginners reach 30 or more within a week. A few tricks help: move shared fingers as a unit, keep any finger that stays on the same string in place as an anchor, look ahead to the next chord, and lift your fingers slightly early on the last beat so you land on time. If you are late, strum the open strings rather than stopping the count. Good pairs to drill next are G to C, C to D, Em to G and A to D.

Do guitar fingers stop hurting?

Yes. For the first 2 to 3 weeks your fingertips will probably feel tender. The skin is adapting and forming calluses, harder patches that make pressing strings much more comfortable. After that, the soreness fades.

  • Keep sessions short: two 10-minute sessions are kinder than one 40-minute session.
  • Stop before tenderness turns into real soreness, and take a day off if your hands ask for it.
  • Use light strings and make sure the action is not too high.
  • Press only as hard as you need to.
Tender fingertips are expected, but sharp pain, numbness or tingling in your fingers, hand or wrist is not. Stop playing and rest, and if it continues or comes back each time you play, see a doctor before you carry on.

A 30-day guitar practice plan for beginners

Fifteen to 20 minutes a day with a timer is enough. Each session can follow the same shape: 2 minutes tuning, 2 minutes warm-up, 5 minutes on one new skill, 4 minutes of chord changes, 5 minutes playing a song through without stopping, and 2 minutes of free play.

WeekFocus
1Setup, posture, tuning, Em, Am, E, then C and A
2G, D and Dm, counting, down-up strumming, the folk pattern
3Smooth changes and your first songs, such as Amazing Grace and When the Saints Go Marching In
4Simple melodies from tab, basic fingerpicking, seventh chords and the 12-bar blues

Keep the guitar on a stand where you can see it, because you will pick it up far more often. Record yourself once a week, and you will hear progress you cannot feel day to day. Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest guitar chord to learn first?

E minor (Em) is usually the easiest. It uses two fingers, middle and ring, on the 5th and 4th strings at the 2nd fret, and you strum all six strings, so you do not have to aim. A minor and E major use almost the same finger shape, which makes them good second and third chords.

How long does it take to learn guitar as a beginner?

With 15 to 20 minutes of practice a day, most adults can tune up, play the main open chords, strum steady rhythms and play simple songs in about 30 days. Changing chords quickly and playing barre chords take longer, often a few months. Short daily practice matters much more than long occasional sessions.

Can I teach myself guitar at home?

Yes, many people do. Follow a clear order: tuning, posture, first chords, strumming, chord changes, then songs. Practice daily with a timer, check every chord string by string and record yourself weekly. A few lessons or a local group can help later, but you can play your first songs at home on your own.

Is it better to learn on an acoustic or electric guitar?

Both work for beginners. Electric guitars usually have lighter strings and lower action, so they are easier on your fingers, but you need an amp or headphones. Steel-string acoustics are louder and simpler to pick up and play anywhere. Nylon-string guitars are the gentlest on fingertips. Choose the one that suits the music you want to play.

Why does my guitar buzz when I play a chord?

Most buzzes come from finger position. Your fingertip may be too far from the fret wire, pressing too lightly or touching a neighboring string. Slide the tip close to the fret, arch your fingers and land on the very tips. If one string buzzes even when played open, the guitar may need a setup at a music shop.

How many minutes a day should a beginner practice guitar?

Fifteen to 20 minutes a day is ideal for beginners. It builds skill steadily without making sore fingertips worse. If you have more time, split it into two short sessions, such as morning and evening, rather than one long block. Stop while you still want to play, which makes it easier to come back tomorrow.

Start today with one small step: tune your guitar, play E minor, check each string one at a time, then do a single round of one-minute changes between Em and Am and write down the number. Leave the guitar out where you can see it, and beat that number tomorrow.
