Camping for Beginners: A Simple Plan for Your First Trip

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have never camped before, the whole thing can feel like a test you did not study for. Here is the short answer: book one or two nights at a campground (campsite in the UK) within two hours of home, borrow or rent a tent one size bigger than your group, sort out warm sleeping with a pad under every person, and plan three simple meals.

That is genuinely most of it. The rest of this guide covers each step in the order you will plan it, with the details that separate a soggy night from a weekend your family asks to repeat.

What a first camping trip really looks like

The easiest kind is car camping at an established campground. You park beside your pitch, there is a toilet block a short walk away, and drinking water comes from a tap. You do not need to be sporty or handy with knots, just willing to be slightly less comfortable than at home for a night or two.

What it costs

A basic tent site at a public US campground often costs around tabindex="-1"5 to $40 a night, and a family pitch in the UK is often in the range of £15 to £40, with busy holidays costing more. Food costs about what it would at home, plus ice and snacks. The big expense is gear, and you can avoid most of it on a first trip.

  • Borrow a tent first. Many people have one in the attic they used twice.
  • Use your own kitchen pans, plates and mugs.
  • Rent or buy used, and pitch any secondhand tent at home to check poles, pegs and zippers.

When you do spend money, put it into the things that keep you warm and dry: a decent tent and a good sleeping pad.

How to choose a campground for your first trip

Pick somewhere within about two hours of home, with toilets and drinking water. If the weather turns or a child gets sick, you can be home by dinner. Late spring, summer and early autumn (fall) give you the kindest temperatures for a first try.

Once you arrive, take two minutes to walk your site before unpacking anything. Where you put the tent matters more than most beginners expect.

  • Flat, slightly raised ground. Avoid dips and the bottom of slopes, because rain runs downhill and collects there. If there is a slope, sleep with your head uphill.
  • Look up. Never pitch under a dead tree or a large dead branch, which can fall without warning in wind.
  • Morning shade keeps the tent from turning into an oven at sunrise in summer.
  • Stay well back from rivers and lakes, and never camp in a dry stream bed, which can flood suddenly after rain upstream.
  • Notice where the fire ring sits so your tent door does not face straight into smoke.

Wild camping rules vary: England and Wales generally need the landowner's permission, Scotland allows responsible wild camping, and US national parks usually require designated sites. Always check with the land manager.

Which tent to get and how to pitch it

Tent sizes assume adults lying shoulder to shoulder with no room for bags or shoes. Choose one size larger than your group.

GroupTent size to look at
One adult2-person
Two adults3-person
Two adults, one or two kids5 or 6-person
Larger family6 to 8-person, or two smaller tents

Pitch it once in the backyard or garden before your trip. Most dome and tunnel tents go up in this order:

  1. Clear stones, sticks and pine cones from the ground.
  2. Lay down your footprint or groundsheet, tucked so no edge sticks out beyond the tent floor.
  3. Spread the tent on top with the door facing away from the wind.
  4. Push the shock-corded pole sections fully together and attach them through sleeves or clips.
  5. Raise the tent and peg the corners, pushing pegs in at about 45 degrees, leaning away from the tent.
  6. Put on the rainfly, lining its door up with the inner door.
  7. Peg out and tighten every guyline so the fly is smooth and does not touch the inner tent.
Do not skip the guylines because the tent looks fine without them. They keep the fly away from the inner tent, which stops rain soaking through where the fabrics touch, and they hold everything steady in wind.

How to stay warm sleeping in a tent

Cold nights are nearly always fixable. Think of your bed as a system: a sleeping bag above you, insulation below you and dry clothes in between.

Sleeping bag ratings

Many bags list a comfort rating and a limit rating. The comfort figure is where a person who sleeps cold should feel fine. The limit figure is closer to "you will get through the night," and when a bag shows one big number on the label, it is often the limit. Choose a bag whose comfort rating is at or below the lowest temperature you expect, plus about 10°F (5 to 6°C) of margin if you feel the cold.

What goes under you

The ground pulls heat out of you faster than the air does. A sleeping pad's R-value tells you how well it stops that: higher is warmer. Around 2 suits mild summer nights, and 3 to 4 is a good choice from spring through autumn. You can stack a foam pad under an inflatable one to add their values together.

Bedtime habits that help

  • Eat a proper dinner and a small snack before bed, since your body burns food to make heat.
  • Change into dry base layers of wool or synthetic fabric, clean socks and a hat. Skip cotton on cold or damp nights.
  • Put a hard bottle of hot (not boiling) water, lid tight, inside a sock at your feet.
Never use a stove, grill, charcoal or fuel-burning heater inside a tent, vestibule, camper or closed car, even for a few minutes. They can produce carbon monoxide, which you cannot see or smell and which can kill sleeping people, and tents catch fire quickly.

Camping checklist for beginners

Most of this list is already in your home.

  • Tent with poles, stakes (pegs), rainfly and footprint, plus a mallet and a few spare stakes
  • Sleeping bag, pad or air bed (and a pump) and a pillow for each person
  • Tarp and cord for a rain shelter
  • Clothes in layers: base layer, fleece or sweater, waterproof jacket and rain pants, warm hat, sun hat, plenty of socks, sleepwear
  • Two pairs of shoes per person
  • Stove, fuel, lighter, pots, pan, utensils, plates, cups, knife, cutting board, can opener
  • Cooler with ice, water containers and drinking water
  • Two or three washing-up basins, biodegradable soap, sponge, dish towel, trash bags
  • Headlamp (head torch) for each person, spare batteries and a lantern
  • Camp chairs, first aid kit and personal medicines
  • Sunscreen, insect repellent, hand sanitizer, toiletries, quick-dry towels, toilet paper
  • Phone, charger, power bank and a printed booking confirmation
  • Duct tape and a small repair kit

One habit worth copying: seal a full set of dry clothes for each person in a plastic bag and leave it in the car. If everything in the tent gets damp, you still have something dry to sleep in.

Setting up camp in your first hour

Arrive with daylight to spare and work in this order, so shelter comes first.

  1. Put headlamps in pockets, even in daylight.
  2. Pitch the tent, door away from the wind.
  3. Put up a tarp if rain is likely, with one side lower so water runs off.
  4. Inflate pads and lay out sleeping bags so they can fluff up.
  5. Set up the kitchen: stove on the table, cooler in the shade, water and a trash bag nearby.
  6. Set out chairs and lights.
  7. Buy or gather firewood if fires are allowed.

Once those seven jobs are done, everything else is a bonus. Keep a wet zone and a dry zone: wet jackets and boots stay in the vestibule or car, and nothing wet goes past the inner tent door. Be a good neighbor too. Respect quiet hours and keep pets on a leash.

Easy food and keeping your cooler cold

Plan three simple meals a day and cook most of them on a camp stove. A two-burner stove is easy for families, and you should only ever use it in the open air. Two easy ideas: breakfast burritos with skillet-scrambled eggs, and foil packets of chicken and vegetables cooked over coals rather than flames.

A well-managed cooler keeps food safe for 2 to 4 days. The goal is to keep cold food at 40°F (4°C) or below.

  • Pre-chill the cooler the night before.
  • Use block ice or frozen water bottles, which melt much more slowly than cubes.
  • Pack in reverse: day three food at the bottom, today's lunch on top.
  • Keep it shut, in the shade and off hot ground. Use a second cooler for drinks if you can.

Cook meat to safe internal temperatures, checked with a food thermometer: 165°F (74°C) for chicken and 160°F (71°C) for burgers. Color is not a reliable guide, especially in evening light. If perishable food has been above 40°F for more than about two hours (one hour in very hot weather), throw it out.

Never keep food, trash or scented items like toothpaste in your tent. Lock coolers in the car or use the campground's food storage, and in bear country follow the posted rules exactly. Bear boxes and approved canisters are required in many areas.

Campfire safety and putting a fire out

Check the fire rules before you light anything. Use the existing fire ring, obey any fire ban, keep a bucket of water nearby and never leave a fire alone. Buy firewood locally rather than bringing it from home, because moving firewood spreads tree pests and diseases.

Most wildfires from campfires start with fires people thought were out. Before bed and before you leave, use the drown, stir and feel method:

  1. Drown the whole fire with water until the hissing stops.
  2. Stir the ashes and scrape embers off half-burned logs.
  3. Drown it again and stir again.
  4. Hold the back of your hand near the ashes. If you feel any heat, repeat.

It is out when everything is cold to the touch. Never bury a fire, because coals can smolder underground.

Staying safe outdoors

Before you leave, tell someone where you are going, your site number and when you will be back. Agree on a time when they should call for help if they have not heard from you. Check the forecast for wind, overnight lows and storms.

  • Lightning. If you can hear thunder, a tent gives no protection. Get into a hard-topped car or a proper building and wait 30 minutes after the last rumble.
  • Cold. Hypothermia can happen at 40 to 50°F (4 to 10°C) when people are wet. Watch for strong shivering, clumsiness, confusion and drowsiness. Get them dry and wrapped up, and call for help if they are confused.
  • Water. Treat stream or lake water by boiling for one minute (three above about 6,500 feet or 2,000 meters), filtering, or using an approved chemical treatment.
  • Ticks. Check everyone each evening. Remove ticks with fine tweezers close to the skin, pulling straight up, and see a doctor if a spreading rash or fever follows.
In an emergency call 911 in the US, or 999 or 112 in the UK. Pack a first aid kit and make sure everyone knows where it is.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to start camping as a beginner?

Start with car camping at an established campground or campsite within about two hours of home, for one or two nights in mild weather. Choose a site with toilets and drinking water, borrow or rent the big gear, and practice pitching your tent in the backyard first. A short, easy trip builds confidence much faster than an ambitious one that goes wrong.

What do I need for camping for the first time?

The essentials are a tent one size bigger than your group, a sleeping bag and a pad for each person, warm layers and rain gear, a stove with fuel, a cooler, water containers, headlamps, a first aid kit and trash bags. Most kitchen items, towels and clothes can come straight from home, so the shopping list is usually much shorter than it looks.

How do you stay warm when camping at night?

Put good insulation under you, because the ground steals more heat than the air. Use a pad with a higher R-value or stack two pads, pick a sleeping bag by its comfort rating rather than its limit, and change into dry wool or synthetic base layers, socks and a hat. Eat a snack before bed and add a hot water bottle at your feet.

Is it safe to sleep in a tent?

Yes, at an established campground it is generally very safe when you follow a few rules. Keep all food and scented items out of the tent, choose a pitch away from dead branches and flood-prone ground, and never use a stove or fuel heater inside. Leave a trip plan with someone at home, and head for a car or building if you hear thunder.

What should you not do when camping?

Do not pitch in a dip, under dead branches or right beside a river. Do not keep food in your tent, feed wildlife, or move firewood from home. Never bring a stove, grill or heater inside a tent or closed car. Do not leave a campfire until it is cold to the touch, and do not ignore quiet hours or the campground's posted rules.

Your first step today is simple: pick a weekend in the next two months, find a campground with toilets and drinking water within two hours of home, and ask friends whether anyone has a tent you could borrow. Pitch it once in the backyard, and you are already halfway to a trip you will want to repeat.
