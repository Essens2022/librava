How to Start a Cleaning Business: A Step by Step Launch Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe you already clean for a neighbor or two, or you simply know you are good at making a home shine and want work that fits around your life. To start a cleaning business, pick one or two services, register the business as your area requires, get liability insurance, work out prices from your real costs, and win your first clients through people who already trust you.

This guide takes you through each step in launch order, from choosing a niche to building a weekly schedule. It also covers the safety rules every cleaner must know, because a professional cleaning business is built on trust as much as on spotless bathrooms.

Is a cleaning business right for you

The upside is real. Startup needs are modest, demand comes back week after week, and you can begin part time around school runs or another job. Many owners also feel a quiet pride in leaving a home calm and fresh.

The downside is real too. Cleaning is physical work, with bending, kneeling, carrying and repetitive movements every day. Clients cancel, cars break down and bookkeeping does not do itself. If you hire later, you also manage people.

The skills that matter most

  • Reliability. Turning up when you said you would is the main thing clients pay for.
  • Attention to detail, so the same corners are clean every visit
  • Clear, friendly communication about times, prices and changes
  • Discretion. You will see people's homes at their most private.

Decide early whether you want to stay solo or build a team one day. Both are valid, but the answer affects how you price, as later sections explain.

Choose your cleaning services and niche

Focus beats offering everything. A short menu is easier to price, easier to train for and easier for clients to understand. Here are the main options and how they compare.

ServiceWhat it involvesGood for beginners?
Regular residential cleaningWeekly or fortnightly maintenance visitsYes, steady repeat work
Deep cleansDetailed one-off or occasional cleansYes, priced separately
Move-in and move-out cleansEmpty homes, often with deadlinesYes, once you have a system
Vacation rental (holiday let) turnoversFast, reliable resets between guestsLater, needs tight scheduling
Small officesEarly morning or evening contract workLater, often needs written contracts
Post-renovation cleansHeavy dust and debrisLater, needs extra care

Avoid specialist work at first, such as heavy mold, biohazards, hoarding cleanups or anything involving hazardous waste. These need training, equipment and insurance most new businesses do not have.

Then define your service area. Draw a tight zone around home so travel time does not quietly eat your day, and say no to jobs far outside it.

Set up your cleaning business legally

Getting legal is less scary than it sounds, and doing it before your first client saves trouble later. Rules vary by country, state, province and city, so treat this as a checklist of questions to ask locally, not legal advice.

  1. Choose a business structure. Many start as a sole proprietor (sole trader); others form a limited liability company or limited company. An accountant can explain the trade-offs.
  2. Register your business name where required.
  3. Ask your city, county or council whether you need a business license or permit.
  4. Register for taxes as your country requires.
  5. Open a separate bank account for business money.
  6. Set up simple record keeping for every invoice, receipt and mile or kilometer driven.

A local small business advice service can often answer these questions for free. Keep everything you learn in one starter file, with dates, so you can check it again next year.

Insurance, bonding and protecting client trust

Insurance comes before your first client. A knocked-over vase or a client who slips on a wet floor can cost far more than you earn in a month.

  • General liability insurance (public liability in the UK) for damage or injury
  • Bonding, available in some countries, which protects clients if an employee steals
  • Employer's insurance once you hire, where the law requires it
  • Vehicle insurance that covers business use

Talk to an insurance broker and ask exactly what is and is not covered, including breakages and lost keys. Then write your own short breakage policy so clients know what happens if something gets damaged.

Keys and alarm codes

Give each client's key a code, never their name or address. Store keys and alarm codes securely, log when they leave and return, and never share codes by unsecured messages. Do not take photos inside clients' homes without permission. Small habits like these are what make people comfortable handing you their keys.

Cleaning supplies and chemical safety

Build your kit around your services. A core kit usually includes a caddy, microfiber cloths, a few multi-purpose and bathroom products, scrubbing brushes, a mop and bucket, gloves and a reliable vacuum (hoover). Color code your cloths so the cloth used on a toilet never touches a kitchen counter.

Never mix cleaning products. Bleach mixed with ammonia releases toxic gas, and bleach mixed with acids such as vinegar or some toilet and limescale cleaners releases chlorine gas. Read every label and safety data sheet, keep products in their original containers or clearly labeled refill bottles, ventilate rooms, and wear gloves and eye protection when the label says so. If someone swallows or inhales a product, call your local emergency number or poison information line straight away.

Other safety basics matter just as much:

  • Use step stools and ladders correctly instead of standing on chairs
  • Put out wet floor warnings and lift with bent knees, loads close to your body
  • Keep electrical items away from water
  • Tell someone your schedule when you work alone
  • Store products away from children and pets, and secure them in your vehicle

If you choose eco-friendly products, describe them honestly. Homemade cleaners do not disinfect the way registered disinfectants do.

How to price cleaning jobs

Most new cleaners underprice, usually by guessing a figure on the doorstep. Prices vary hugely from town to town, so build yours from methods rather than copying someone else.

The four main pricing methods

  • Hourly rate per cleaner, useful for deep cleans and homes you have not seen
  • Flat rate per visit for a written list of tasks
  • Pricing by home size or room count, handy for phone enquiries
  • Add-ons priced separately, such as inside ovens, fridges, cabinets and interior windows

Many owners blend them: estimate the hours behind the scenes, then quote a flat rate. The first visit is usually priced higher, because a home needs a deep clean before regular maintenance cleans can keep it up. Any discount for weekly or fortnightly clients should stay modest.

Know your true costs

List supplies, equipment, travel, insurance, phone, software, taxes and the unpaid hours spent on quotes, admin, laundry and driving. Your price must cover all of that, plus profit. Then research what local cleaning businesses offer and compare like with like, since one may include bed changes while another charges extra.

A useful test is to price as though you might one day pay someone else to do the cleaning. If your prices could not cover a fair wage, you will struggle to grow. And never compete only on being the cheapest; those clients leave as soon as someone cheaper appears.

Quotes, agreements and getting paid

See the home before you quote, either with a short walk-through or a video call. Note the number of rooms, floor types, clutter, the state of kitchens and bathrooms, pets, access, parking and how often the client wants you. Send a written quote within a day.

Then put the relationship in writing with a simple service agreement. It should cover:

  • The services included and the visit schedule
  • Price, payment method and when payment is due
  • Your cancellation and lockout policy, for when you arrive and cannot get in
  • Breakage, keys, alarm codes and pets
  • Your satisfaction guarantee terms and how complaints are handled
  • Privacy, including no photos without permission

If a client pays late, send a polite reminder a few days after the due date. If it keeps happening, ask for payment before each visit. Consider asking a lawyer to check your agreement template once. How to get your first cleaning clients

Your first ten clients usually come from people who already trust you. Tell friends, family, neighbors and coworkers, and ask them to pass your details on. Make referrals easy and thank people who send work your way.

  • Post in local online groups and neighborhood forums, following their rules
  • Create a free online business listing with your services and service area
  • Deliver flyers and door hangers where allowed
  • Set up a simple one-page website with services, area and contact details
  • Introduce yourself to real estate agents (estate agents) and property managers who need move-out cleans

First impressions win jobs. Answer the phone warmly, reply to messages the same day and turn up to walk-throughs on time and tidy. After the first few visits go well, ask happy clients for a short review; reviews are how strangers learn to trust you.

Scheduling and growing at your own pace

Build your week around groups of homes in the same area, and plan real travel time between them. Recurring weekly and fortnightly visits give you steady work, while one-off deep cleans fill the gaps. Keep notes on every home, such as where the vacuum is stored, which surfaces need special care and the pet's name.

Set a weekly admin hour for invoices, records, restocking and next week's schedule. Review your prices at least once a year and give existing clients written notice of any rise.

When you are fully booked, you have choices: stay solo with a full book, add services, take on small offices, or hire. Hiring brings legal duties, and the rules on employees versus contractors are strict, so get local advice first. Whatever you choose, protect your body with long-handled tools, knee pads, good shoes and real breaks.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a license to start a cleaning business?

It depends on where you live. Many places require a general business license or registration rather than a cleaning-specific license, and you will usually need to register for taxes. Check with your city, county or local council and a small business advice service before you start. Rules differ between countries, states and provinces, so always confirm locally.

How do I price a house cleaning job?

See the home first through a walk-through or video, then estimate how long each room will take. Turn that time into a price that covers supplies, travel, insurance, taxes, admin time and profit. Price the first deep clean higher than regular visits, list add-ons separately, and compare your result with what local cleaning businesses include.

What insurance does a cleaning business need?

Most cleaning businesses start with general liability insurance, called public liability in the UK, which covers accidental damage or injury. In some countries, bonding protects clients against theft by staff. Once you hire, employer's insurance may be required by law, and your vehicle policy should cover business use. A broker can match cover to your services.

Can I start a cleaning business with no money?

You can start with very little, because many people begin with their own basic supplies and vacuum. You still need to budget for insurance, any registration or license fees and decent products before taking paying clients. Starting small with a few clients near home keeps costs and travel low while you learn your timings.

Is it better to clean homes or offices?

Homes are usually easier to start with, since jobs are smaller and referrals spread quickly between neighbors. Offices often bring steady contracts and early or late hours that fit around residential work, but clients may expect written contracts, proof of insurance and invoicing with longer payment times. Many businesses start with homes and add small offices later.

What cleaning products should never be mixed?

Never mix bleach with ammonia, because it releases toxic gas, and never mix bleach with acids such as vinegar or some toilet and limescale cleaners, because that releases chlorine gas. The safest rule is never to mix different products at all. Read labels, ventilate rooms and get emergency help if someone breathes in fumes.

Today, write down the one or two services you want to start with and draw your service area on a map. Then make two calls: one to your local council or city office about registration, and one to an insurance broker. Those two answers turn an idea into a real cleaning business you can open with confidence.
