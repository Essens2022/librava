Foods to Avoid During Pregnancy: A Clear List and What to Eat Instead

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have just found out you are pregnant, the advice about food can feel endless and contradictory. The short answer: avoid unpasteurized dairy, soft mold-ripened and blue cheeses unless cooked, raw or undercooked meat, eggs and fish, pâté and liver, high-mercury fish such as shark and swordfish, and alcohol, and keep caffeine under about 200 mg a day.

This guide gives you the full list in one place, the reasons behind it in plain words, safer ways to enjoy many of the foods you love and what to do if you eat something on the list by mistake. Guidance differs a little between countries and changes over time, so if your midwife or doctor tells you something different, follow your care team.

Why some foods are off the menu

The list is not random. Almost every item comes down to five things.

  • Listeria is a bacterium that, unlike most food bugs, can grow in the fridge. It is rare, but pregnancy makes you more likely to catch it, and it can harm your baby even if you only feel mildly unwell. It is linked with unpasteurized dairy, soft mold-ripened and blue cheeses, chilled ready-to-eat meats and fish, and pâté. Thorough heating kills it.
  • Salmonella causes food poisoning and comes from raw or undercooked eggs, poultry and meat. Severe vomiting and fever are miserable and can be risky in pregnancy.
  • Toxoplasmosis is an infection from a parasite found in raw meat, soil on unwashed fruit and vegetables, and cat feces. It often causes no symptoms but can harm a developing baby.
  • Mercury builds up in some large, long-lived fish and can affect a baby's developing nervous system.
  • Vitamin A in large amounts, especially the retinol form in liver and some supplements, can harm a baby's development.

Once you know the reason, most of the rules make sense, and you can spot safe options on your own.

Can you eat cheese when pregnant

Yes, most cheese is fine. Hard cheeses such as cheddar and parmesan are generally fine, and so are pasteurized soft cheeses without a white rind: mozzarella, ricotta, cottage cheese, cream cheese, paneer, halloumi and pasteurized feta.

The ones to avoid cold are soft mold-ripened cheeses with a white rind, such as brie, camembert and rinded goat's cheese, and soft blue-veined cheeses. Once they are cooked until steaming hot, for example baked into a dish, the heat kills listeria and they are generally considered safe.

Always read the label for the word pasteurized. Some countries advise avoiding all unpasteurized cheese, even hard ones, so when in doubt, choose pasteurized. Soft Latin-style fresh cheeses are only an option if the label clearly says they are pasteurized.

Meat, deli meats and eggs in pregnancy

Cook all meat, poultry and sausages until steaming hot with no pink or blood, and that includes steak. A meat thermometer takes the guesswork out:

  • Chicken and turkey, whole or ground (minced): 165°F (74°C)
  • Ground beef, pork and lamb: 160°F (71°C), or steaming hot with no pink (UK guidance gives 70°C for 2 minutes as an example)
  • Whole cuts of beef, pork and lamb: at least 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest and no pink; many cooks take them further to 160°F (71°C) in pregnancy to be cautious
  • Egg dishes: 160°F (71°C), yolks and whites firm

Deli meats, cold cuts and hot dogs can carry listeria. Some countries say pre-packed cold meats are fine, but the cautious route is to heat them until steaming hot, 165°F (74°C). Ham is a better pizza topping than a cold sandwich filling for now. Raw cured meats such as salami, chorizo and prosciutto are fine cooked into a hot dish. Avoid all pâté and liver, and game shot with lead pellets.

Egg advice varies by country. Some advise cooking all eggs until firm; others say eggs produced under a recognized national safety scheme may be eaten runny. If you are unsure, cook them firm. Store-bought mayonnaise and dressings use pasteurized egg and are fine.

Which fish to avoid and which to eat

Fish is a good food in pregnancy, bringing protein, iodine and, in oily fish, omega-3 fats. The aim is about two portions a week, including some oily fish, chosen with mercury in mind.

  • Avoid: shark, swordfish and marlin. Some countries also list king mackerel, orange roughy, tilefish and bigeye tuna, so check local advice.
  • Limit tuna. Some countries advise no more than about 4 medium cans or 2 fresh tuna steaks a week; others suggest choosing canned light tuna over albacore.
  • Limit oily fish such as salmon, trout, mackerel, herring and sardines to about 2 portions a week.
  • Good everyday choices: cod, haddock, pollock, tilapia and cooked shrimp (prawns).

Overall, 8 to 12 oz (225 to 340 g) a week of a variety of low-mercury fish is a common guide. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), or until it is opaque and flakes easily, and cook shrimp until pink, opaque and steaming. Skip sushi with raw fish and raw oysters. Smoked salmon is fine once cooked until steaming hot, for example in a pasta bake or fish pie.

A simple safe-fish dinner is salmon baked with lemon, herbs and a drizzle of olive oil at 400°F (200°C) for 12 to 15 minutes, until it flakes, served with potatoes and green vegetables.

How much caffeine is safe in pregnancy

You do not have to give up coffee or tea. The general advice is to keep caffeine below about 200 mg a day. The amount in a drink varies a lot with cup size and strength, so treat these as rough figures.

DrinkApproximate caffeine
Mug of instant coffeeAbout 100 mg
Mug of filter coffeeAbout 140 mg
Mug of teaAbout 75 mg
Can of colaAbout 40 mg
Small 8.5 fl oz (250 ml) energy drinkAbout 80 mg

Coffee shop drinks, especially large sizes and extra shots, can contain much more. Chocolate has small amounts too. A simple approach is one mug of coffee and one of tea, or two mugs of tea, then switch to decaf or water. Herbal teas are fine for most people in moderation; check unfamiliar ones with your midwife or pharmacist.

On alcohol, the advice is simple: no amount is known to be safe in pregnancy, so the safest choice is none. If a recipe uses wine, use broth (stock) instead.

Supplements to take and to avoid

Food does a lot, but two supplements are commonly advised. A daily folic acid supplement of 400 micrograms is generally advised, ideally from before you conceive until about 12 weeks of pregnancy. Some people are prescribed a higher dose, for example with diabetes, certain epilepsy medicines or a family history of neural tube problems, so ask your doctor. Many countries also advise a daily vitamin D supplement in pregnancy.

Avoid vitamin A (retinol) supplements, high-dose multivitamins not made for pregnancy, and fish liver oil. A multivitamin made specifically for pregnancy is different; ask your pharmacist if you are unsure. Check any other supplement or herbal remedy with your midwife, doctor or pharmacist first, because natural does not always mean safe in pregnancy.

Kitchen habits that keep you safe

  • Wash your hands before cooking and after handling raw meat, raw eggs or soil-covered vegetables.
  • Wash fruit, vegetables and salad well, even bagged salad.
  • Use a separate board for raw meat, and do not wash raw chicken.
  • Keep your fridge at or below 40°F (4°C), or 41°F (5°C) in the UK, and your freezer at 0°F (-18°C).
  • Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours, eat cooked dishes ideally within 2 days, and cooked rice within 24 hours.
  • Reheat leftovers and ready meals until steaming hot, 165°F (74°C), and reheat only once.

When eating out, choose dishes cooked hot and served hot, ask for burgers and steak well done and ask whether sauces contain raw egg. At parties, hard cheese is fine; leave the brie and salami. The book Pregnancy Cookbook Made Simple includes a printable fridge-door list and a safe fish guide.

If you ate something on the list

It happens to almost everyone. You eat half a sandwich before realizing the cheese was blue, or a dish arrives with a runny egg. Try not to panic, because the risk from a single occasion is usually low. Mention it to your midwife or doctor, especially if you develop a fever, aches, flu-like symptoms or a stomach upset in the days or weeks afterward, since some infections take time to show.

This guide is general information, not medical advice, and does not replace your antenatal (prenatal) care. Contact your maternity unit, midwife, doctor or emergency services straight away for vaginal bleeding, severe or persistent tummy pain, a severe headache, vision problems or sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet, fever or feeling very unwell, fluid leaking from the vagina, your baby moving less than usual, vomiting so severe you cannot keep fluids down, intense itching, chest pain or severe breathlessness. Emergency numbers: 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK (111 for urgent advice), 000 in Australia, 111 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

What foods should you avoid in the first trimester?

The same list applies right through pregnancy: unpasteurized dairy, soft mold-ripened and blue cheeses unless cooked, raw or undercooked meat, eggs and fish, pâté, liver, high-mercury fish and alcohol, with caffeine kept under about 200 mg a day. The first trimester is also when a daily folic acid supplement is generally advised, so ask your doctor or pharmacist if you have not started one.

Can I eat deli meat while pregnant?

The cautious approach is to heat deli meats, cold cuts and hot dogs until steaming hot, 165°F (74°C), before eating, because they can carry listeria. Some countries say pre-packed cold meats are fine as they are, so local advice varies. Raw cured meats such as salami and prosciutto are fine when cooked into a hot dish like a pizza or pasta bake.

Can I eat tuna when pregnant?

Yes, within limits. Some countries advise no more than about 4 medium cans or 2 fresh tuna steaks a week, while others suggest choosing canned light tuna over albacore because it contains less mercury. Avoid bigeye tuna where local advice lists it. Cook fresh tuna through, and count it as part of your weekly fish, aiming for a variety of low-mercury choices.

Can pregnant women eat runny eggs?

It depends on where you live. Some countries advise cooking all eggs until the yolk and white are firm, while others say eggs produced under a recognized national safety scheme can be eaten runny. If you are unsure, cook eggs until firm, to 160°F (71°C). Avoid homemade mayonnaise and raw egg sauces; store-bought mayonnaise is made with pasteurized egg.

Is it safe to eat sushi when pregnant?

Avoid sushi made with raw fish or raw shellfish. Vegetarian sushi and rolls with cooked fillings, such as cooked shrimp (prawns) or cooked crab, are generally fine if they are freshly made and kept cold. Smoked salmon in sushi counts as cold-smoked fish, so leave it for now unless it is cooked until steaming hot in a dish.

Do I need to eat for two when pregnant?

No. Energy needs rise only modestly during pregnancy, mostly in the later months, and in the early weeks many people need no extra food at all. What matters more is variety: starchy foods, plenty of vegetables and fruit, a protein food at meals and pasteurized dairy or a fortified alternative. Talk to your care team if you have any concerns about weight.

Your first step today: put a thermometer in your fridge, copy the table above onto a sheet for the fridge door and check the label on any supplement you take for folic acid, vitamin D and vitamin A. Bring any questions to your next appointment, because your midwife or doctor would always rather hear them than have you worry.
