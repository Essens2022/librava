Breathing Exercises for Anxiety: 4 Simple Ways to Calm Down Fast

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

When your chest feels tight and your thoughts start racing, your breath is the quickest tool you carry everywhere. The simplest breathing exercise for anxious moments is the longer exhale: breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 4, then out slowly for a count of 6, and repeat for 1 to 3 minutes.

Below you will find four gentle techniques, step by step, with a table showing which one to use when. You will also learn how to pair breathing with grounding, how to use it at bedtime and how to make it a habit, so it is there for you when you need it most.

Why breathing calms anxiety so quickly

Breathing runs on autopilot all day, yet you can take over the controls whenever you like. That makes it a direct line into your stress response.

When you feel stressed or anxious, breathing tends to get faster and shallower, moving mostly in the upper chest. When you are calm, it slows and moves lower, into the belly. The link works both ways. If you deliberately breathe slowly and low, with a longer breath out, your body picks up the signal that it is safe enough to settle.

The breath out is the part that matters most. As you exhale slowly, your heart rate naturally eases a little, and lengthening the exhale leans into that effect. Studies suggest slow, relaxed breathing can help lower heart rate and ease feelings of tension within a few minutes.

Calming breathing is slow and soft. Big, fast, deep gulps of air can leave you more lightheaded and jittery. If people nearby can hear you breathing, you are probably working too hard.

Before you start, a few safety tips

These techniques are gentle by design. You should never feel strained, and comfort always comes first.

  • Sit or stand with your chest open rather than hunched.
  • Breathe through your nose if that is comfortable. If it is blocked, gentle mouth breathing is fine.
  • Keep the counts loose. If a number feels too long, shorten it, because the pattern matters more than the exact count.
  • Keep your eyes open when driving or doing anything that needs your full attention.
Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy, lightheaded, tingly or short of breath, and never hold your breath until it feels uncomfortable. If you have asthma, a lung or heart condition, or you are pregnant, follow your doctor's advice about breathing exercises. This guide is general wellbeing information, not treatment for any condition. If worry, stress or low mood is severe, lasts more than a couple of weeks or affects daily life, talk to your doctor (GP) or a qualified mental health professional.

Belly breathing, the foundation

Belly breathing, sometimes called diaphragm breathing, is the base for every other technique. Many stressed adults breathe almost entirely with the upper chest, so it can feel strange at first.

  1. Sit comfortably with one hand on your chest and the other on your belly, just above your navel.
  2. Breathe in slowly through your nose and let the air gently push your belly hand outward, while your chest hand stays fairly still.
  3. Breathe out slowly and feel your belly fall back.
  4. Keep it quiet and easy for about ten breaths.

There is no need to push your belly out hard. Think of letting the breath drop lower, like water settling in a glass. You are doing it right when your belly hand moves more than your chest hand.

If your belly will not budge, lie on your back with your knees bent and rest a light book on your stomach. Watch it rise and fall. Many people find this position easier, and it is a lovely way to start winding down in bed.

The 4-6 longer exhale technique

This is the easiest technique to use anywhere, because nobody can tell you are doing it. You simply make your breath out longer than your breath in.

  1. Relax your shoulders.
  2. Breathe in gently through your nose for a slow count of 4.
  3. Breathe out slowly through your nose, or through lips shaped as if cooling hot soup, for a count of 6.
  4. Let a tiny natural pause happen, then repeat for about ten rounds, or 1 to 3 minutes.

Adjust it to suit you. If 4 and 6 feels too long, try in for 3 and out for 4 or 5. If it feels easy, stretch to out for 7 or 8, as long as it stays comfortable. Do not squeeze out the very last bit of air.

Use it at red lights with your eyes open, in a waiting room, before a meeting or in a line at the store (a queue, in the UK). Notice small changes afterward, such as warmer hands, lower shoulders or slower thoughts.

Box breathing and the physiological sigh

Box breathing

Box breathing has four equal sides, like a square: breathe in for 4, pause gently for 4, breathe out for 4, pause gently for 4, then repeat for 2 to 4 minutes. Counting keeps a busy mind occupied, so it suits racing thoughts. You can trace a square with your finger on your leg as you go.

The pauses should feel soft, never like a strain. If pausing for 4 is uncomfortable, shorten the pauses to 2 or leave them out. Some people find the counting itself makes them tense. If that is you, stick with the longer exhale.

The physiological sigh

This one is very quick and helps with a sudden spike of stress. Your body often does it on its own after a good cry.

  1. Breathe in through your nose.
  2. Without breathing out, take a second short sniff in on top of the first.
  3. Let out one long, slow, relaxed breath through your mouth.

Do 1 to 3 sighs, then let your breathing return to normal. Keep the double inhale gentle, more of a top-up than a gulp, and do not repeat it for minutes on end.

Which breathing exercise to use when

You do not need all four. Most people end up with one go-to technique and one backup. Use this as a starting point and let your body tell you what works over a week or two.

Everyday tension in publicLonger exhale (4 in, 6 out)1 to 3 minutes

Some days two minutes of breathing will leave you noticeably calmer. Other days it will only take the edge off. Both count, and taking the edge off can be the difference between snapping at someone and staying steady.

Add grounding when your mind races

When worry pulls you into the future, grounding brings your attention back to the room you are in. The best-known version is the 5-4-3-2-1 senses exercise. Silently name:

  • 5 things you can see
  • 4 things you can feel, such as your back against the chair
  • 3 things you can hear
  • 2 things you can smell
  • 1 thing you can taste

Get specific. "A wooden chair with a wobbly back leg" pulls your attention in more firmly than "a chair." If you cannot find a smell or taste, name one you enjoy instead, like fresh bread. If your mind wanders halfway, just pick up where you left off.

A one-minute combined reset works well: three longer exhales, then press your feet into the floor, then name five things you can see. Grounding is not about pushing feelings away. It steadies you enough that the feeling becomes manageable.

For your toughest moments, write a short plan on a card or in your phone notes and practice it on a calm day: pause, name the feeling, feel your feet, do five longer exhales, run through 5-4-3-2-1, then choose one small next step.

What to do in a wave of panic

Some people get sudden waves of intense fear with a pounding heart, tight chest, shaking, sweating or tingling. These are frightening, even when the cause turns out to be stress.

Chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting or sudden weakness can have many causes, some serious. If your symptoms are new, severe, different from usual or you are unsure what is happening, get urgent medical help: call 911 in the US, or 999 in an emergency or NHS 111 for advice in the UK. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in the US, or call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK.

If a doctor has already told you these waves are linked with stress or anxiety, these steps may help you ride them out:

  • Tell yourself, "This is a wave of fear. It is very uncomfortable, and it will pass." Such waves usually peak and ease within minutes.
  • Do not fight the feelings. Let them be there while you focus on something steady.
  • Breathe out slowly. If you are breathing fast, slow down gently rather than gulping air, or try two or three physiological sighs.
  • Hold something with a clear texture, like your keys, and describe it in your head.
  • Stay where you are if it is safe, so your body learns it can cope.

If these waves happen often, or you start avoiding places because of them, talk to your doctor. They are common and very treatable with the right support.

Make breathing a daily habit

A breathing technique works best when you have practiced it at calm times. If you only try it mid-crisis, it can feel awkward. Link it to things you already do:

  • Three slow breaths each time you sit down at your desk.
  • One minute of longer exhales while the kettle boils or the coffee brews.
  • A few belly breaths before you open your email.
  • Bedtime breathing: lying down, breathe in for about 4 and out for about 6 for ten breaths, then stop counting and let your body sink into the bed.

A small sticker on your screen or mirror can act as a cue. Your mind will wander while you breathe, and that is normal. Each time you notice, come back to the next breath out. If you like structure, the book Calm in Minutes includes a 21-day plan that builds these habits one small step at a time.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best breathing exercise for anxiety?

For most people, the longer exhale is the easiest place to start: breathe in through your nose for 4 and out slowly for 6, for 1 to 3 minutes. It is quiet, works anywhere and leans on the calming effect of a slow breath out. Try others too, and keep whichever feels most comfortable for you.

How do you do box breathing?

Breathe in gently for a count of 4, pause softly for 4, breathe out for 4 and pause for 4, then repeat for 2 to 4 minutes. Keep the pauses relaxed, never strained, and shorten them to 2 or skip them if they feel uncomfortable. Stop and breathe normally if you feel lightheaded.

How quickly do breathing exercises work?

Many people notice a small change within one to three minutes, such as lower shoulders, warmer hands or slower thoughts. A physiological sigh can take the edge off in under a minute. Results vary from day to day, and techniques work best when you have practiced them at calm times, so they feel familiar when you really need them.

Why do I feel dizzy when doing breathing exercises?

Dizziness usually means you are breathing too deeply or too fast, or straining the counts. Stop, breathe normally and try again later with softer, quieter breaths and shorter counts, such as in for 3 and out for 4. If dizziness continues, or you have a breathing or heart condition or are pregnant, ask your doctor first.

Can breathing exercises help you fall asleep?

They can help your body shift toward rest. Lie comfortably, rest a hand on your belly and breathe in for about 4 and out for about 6, letting your body sink into the bed with each exhale. After ten breaths, stop counting and just notice the breath. If sleep problems last for weeks, talk to your doctor.

Start today: pick one daily cue, such as boiling the kettle or sitting down at your desk, and every time it happens this week do one minute of 4 in, 6 out breathing. A few calm practice runs now make the technique easy to reach for when your next stressful moment arrives.
