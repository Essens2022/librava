What to Eat After Gallbladder Removal: A Gentle Week by Week Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have just had your gallbladder out, or you are waiting for surgery, you may be nervous about every meal. The short answer: after gallbladder removal, start with small, plain, low-fat meals and plenty of fluids, keep meals small and lower in fat for the first few weeks, then bring fat back slowly, one food at a time. Most people return to a normal, varied diet within weeks or months.

Lower fat does not mean no fat, and light food can still be proper, comforting food. Below you will find what changes without a gallbladder, what to eat at each stage, how to reintroduce fat with a simple diary, how to handle bloating and loose stools, and the signs that need a doctor quickly.

What changes when your gallbladder is removed

Your gallbladder is a small pouch under the liver that stores bile, a fluid your liver makes to help digest fat. When you eat, especially a fatty meal, the gallbladder squeezes a burst of bile into your small intestine.

Without a gallbladder, your liver still makes bile. It just trickles into the gut steadily instead of arriving in one big dose. That is why people live normally without a gallbladder, and also why a large or very fatty meal can sometimes cause bloating, wind or loose stools for a while after surgery.

For most people, these effects settle over weeks or months as the body adjusts. A few people notice them for longer, and that is worth discussing with your doctor because treatment can help.

Your surgical team comes first. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Follow your hospital's instructions on eating and drinking after surgery, and talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before big diet changes, especially if you have diabetes, celiac (coeliac) disease, pancreatitis, liver disease or a history of disordered eating.

The first days after surgery

In the first few days, your body is healing from the operation as well as adjusting. Many people do not feel very hungry, which is normal. Hospitals usually advise fluids first, then small, plain meals.

Gentle first foods often include:

  • Clear soups and broths
  • Plain toast or crackers
  • Rice and mashed potato made with a little milk
  • Plain oatmeal (porridge) made with skim (skimmed) milk
  • Bananas and plain lower-fat yogurt
  • Small portions of poached or steamed chicken or fish

Keep portions small, eat slowly and sip fluids through the day. Gas pain in the shoulder and a sore tummy are common for a day or two after keyhole surgery, and gentle walking around the house helps both recovery and digestion.

The first few weeks, small and lower fat

Over the next few weeks most people move gradually back to ordinary food. Aim for steady progress rather than perfection.

  • Eat three light meals and one or two small snacks, so there are no big gaps or huge plates.
  • Cook with less fat: bake, roast with a little oil, poach, steam, grill (broil) or sauté lightly.
  • Choose lean meat, skinless poultry, fish, beans, lentils, eggs and lower-fat dairy.
  • Add fiber slowly over a week or two, with plenty of fluids.
  • Try one new food at a time so you can tell what suits you.
  • Go easy on very spicy food, large portions and, for some people, caffeine and alcohol.

Measure your oil rather than pouring it. About 1 to 2 teaspoons per serving is plenty for most dishes, and a splash of stock keeps food from sticking. Chilling a stew or homemade stock overnight lets you lift off the fat that sets on top.

Foods to limit after gallbladder removal

Common triggers vary from person to person, but these are the usual suspects in the early weeks. Limiting them is not forever. Many people bring most of them back later in small amounts.

  • Fried and greasy food, including fries (chips) and fried breakfasts
  • Fatty meats, sausages and other fatty processed meats
  • Pastry and pies with pastry crusts
  • Rich creamy sauces and large amounts of butter, cream and cheese
  • Very large meals of any kind
  • Very spicy dishes, and for some people strong coffee or alcohol

How to bring fat back step by step

After a few weeks, if things are settling, you can start reintroducing fat slowly and deliberately. The aim is not a fat-free life. Your body needs some fat, and healthy fats are part of a balanced diet.

  1. Pick one food with a little more fat, such as a small salmon fillet, a quarter of an avocado or a teaspoon of pesto.
  2. Eat a small portion with a familiar lower-fat meal, ideally at lunchtime so you are not lying down soon after.
  3. Write down the food, portion and time, then note how you feel that afternoon, evening and next morning.
  4. If it went well, try a slightly larger portion a few days later. If not, wait a week or two, or try a different food.
  5. Leave 2 to 3 days between new foods so you only test one thing at a time.

Healthy fats are the best place to start: olive oil and rapeseed (canola) oil, oily fish, avocado, nuts and seeds. Salmon with herb potatoes and green beans, or avocado and white bean toast, are easy first tests. Leave fried food, pastry and rich creamy dishes until last, if you bring them back at all.

Keep a simple food diary

A food diary sounds like homework, but it is the most useful tool you have. Without one, you will forget whether the bloating followed Tuesday's cheese sandwich or Wednesday's curry. With one, patterns show up within two or three weeks.

Note bloating, wind, loose stools, pain or nausea and when they started, plus anything else that might matter, like stress or a late meal. Take the diary to appointments. It turns my tummy is sometimes funny into something your doctor can actually work with.

Help for bloating, wind and loose stools

Some people notice bloating, wind or loose stools (diarrhea) after surgery, especially after fatty or large meals. For most, it improves over weeks or months. These habits may help:

  • Smaller meals spread evenly through the day
  • Lower-fat cooking with small portions of fat
  • Eating slowly and chewing well
  • Increasing fiber gradually rather than all at once
  • Drinking enough fluids, especially if stools are loose
  • A gentle walk after meals

Soluble fiber may help some people by making stools firmer. It absorbs water and forms a soft gel in the gut. Good sources include oats, barley, beans, lentils, carrots, potatoes, apples and pears. A bowl of oatmeal (porridge) in the morning, a lentil soup at lunch and potatoes or carrots with dinner is a gentle way to include it.

If diarrhea has not settled after a few weeks, or it is frequent or urgent enough to affect daily life, see your doctor. It is a well-known problem after gallbladder removal, and there is no need to put up with it quietly.

Red flags that need urgent help

Get urgent medical help for any of these, before or after surgery:

  • Severe tummy pain, especially lasting more than a few hours or spreading to your back
  • Fever, chills or shivering
  • Yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes (jaundice), or itchy skin with jaundice
  • Dark urine or pale, clay-colored stools
  • Persistent vomiting
  • After surgery: worsening pain, a swollen belly, or redness, swelling or discharge at a wound

Chest pain or pressure spreading to the arm, neck, jaw or back, or with breathlessness, sweating or faintness, can be the heart. Call emergency services straight away: 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia or 111 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

How long after gallbladder removal can I eat normally?

Most people return to a normal, varied diet within a few weeks to a few months. Start with small, plain, low-fat meals in the first days, keep meals smaller and lighter for the first few weeks, then reintroduce fat one food at a time. Some people adjust quickly, others take longer. A food diary helps you see what suits you.

Why do I have diarrhea after gallbladder removal?

Without a gallbladder, bile flows steadily into the gut instead of being stored and released with meals. After large or fatty meals, this can cause loose stools for some people. It usually improves over weeks or months. Smaller lower-fat meals, soluble fiber such as oats and enough fluids may help. If it has not settled after a few weeks, see your doctor, because treatment can help.

Can I eat eggs after gallbladder surgery?

Usually, yes. Eggs are a common gentle food after surgery, especially poached or boiled, or scrambled with a little milk instead of butter. Start with one egg in a small meal and see how you feel. If you notice discomfort, note it in your food diary and try again a little later, perhaps cooked a different way.

Do I need to follow a low-fat diet forever without a gallbladder?

Usually not. Lower-fat meals help most in the first few weeks after surgery. After that, many people bring fat back step by step and end up eating a normal, varied diet. Some keep a few personal triggers limited, such as fried food or very large meals. Going completely fat-free is not recommended, because your body needs some fat.

Can a gallbladder flush or cleanse remove gallstones?

No. Flushes and cleanses using olive oil and juice, apple cider vinegar or herbal remedies do not remove gallstones and can be risky. The stones people think they pass are usually soap-like lumps formed from the oil itself. Diet cannot dissolve gallstones. Some medicines can dissolve certain stones slowly, but only a doctor can decide if that suits you.

What can I eat while waiting for gallbladder surgery?

Eat smaller meals at regular times, roughly every 3 to 4 hours, and keep fat lower and spread through the day. Choose lean meats, skinless poultry, fish, beans, eggs and lower-fat dairy, and limit fried food, pastry and rich sauces. Do not skip meals or crash diet, as long gaps and rapid weight loss can make stones more likely.

Your first step today: follow your hospital's eating advice, make your next meal small, plain and lower in fat, and start a simple food diary noting what you ate, how much and how you felt. In a few weeks, when things are settling, use that diary to test your first healthy fat, such as a small piece of salmon at lunchtime.
