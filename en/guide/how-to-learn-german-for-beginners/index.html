How to Learn German for Beginners: Where to Start and What to Skip

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

German has a scary reputation: long words, three genders, cases. If you are starting from zero, or from a few half-remembered school lessons, here is the short answer. Begin with pronunciation, greetings and the verbs sein (to be) and haben (to have), learn every noun with der, die or das attached, and practice 15 minutes a day out loud. Leave the harder grammar for later.

This guide shows you the order that works, the sound rules that let you read almost any word, the first phrases to learn, simple ways to handle genders, the one word order rule that matters most, and a 90-day plan. Each example has an easy respelling in brackets, with capitals on the stressed syllable.

Why German is easier than it looks

English and German are close relatives, so you already know hundreds of words: Haus (house), Hand, Wasser (water), Buch (book), trinken (to drink). German spelling is also far more regular than English. Once you learn how the letters sound, you can read nearly any word aloud correctly.

The famous long words are just short words glued together. Handschuh is Hand plus Schuh (shoe), and means glove. On a street sign, Bahnhofstraße is der Bahnhof (station) plus die Straße (street). When you see a monster word, look for the pieces inside it.

A sensible order for beginners:

  1. Sounds and spelling, so you can read and say words correctly from day one.
  2. Greetings, du and Sie, and introducing yourself.
  3. Nouns with der, die and das, plus the words for "a".
  4. Sein, haben and regular verbs; word order with the verb second.
  5. Numbers, time and your daily routine.
  6. Real situations: cafés, shops, trains and hotels, then plans and the past.

German pronunciation for beginners

Most German consonants sound as you expect. These are the ones that catch English speakers, and ten minutes on them pays off for months.

LetterSounds likeExample
wEnglish vWasser [VAH-ser], water
vEnglish f (usually)Vater [FAH-ter], father
zts as in catsZug [tsook], train
jEnglish yja [yah], yes
s before a vowelEnglish zSonne [ZON-nuh], sun
ßa sharp sStraße [SHTRAH-suh], street
sp, st at the startshp, shtStuhl [shtool], chair
final b, d, gp, t, kHund [hunt], dog

For the vowel pairs, say the name of the second letter. In ei the second letter is i, so you say [eye]: Wein [vine], wine. In ie it is e, said "ee" in English: Bier [beer]. The pairs eu and äu sound like "oy": heute [HOY-tuh], today. And au sounds like "ow" in how: Haus [hows].

The umlauts change the sound and sometimes the meaning: schon is already, schön is beautiful. For ü, say "ee" and round your lips as if to whistle; for ö, say "ay" and do the same. If you have no umlaut keys, write ue, oe and ae. The ch after a, o and u is a throaty sound like the end of Scottish "loch"; after e and i it is a soft hiss, as in ich. A soft "sh" there is understood everywhere.

First German phrases to learn

These cover greetings, thanks and getting help. Guten Tag is safe from late morning to early evening; Hallo is friendly and a little more casual.

Hello (good day)Guten Tag[GOO-ten tahk]
Good eveningGuten Abend[GOO-ten AH-bent]

Bitte is a busy word: please, you're welcome and here you are. German also has two ways to say "you". Use Sie (always capital S) with strangers, staff, officials and older people, with Herr or Frau and a surname. Use du with friends, family and children. When in doubt, start with Sie; people will offer du when they want it.

In southern Germany and Austria people greet with Grüß Gott [grues got], and in German-speaking Switzerland with Grüezi [GRUE-ets-ee]. Guten Tag is accepted everywhere, but answering in kind earns a warm smile.

How to learn der, die and das

Every German noun is masculine (der), feminine (die) or neuter (das), and all plurals take die. Grammar gender often has nothing to do with real life: der Löffel (spoon), die Gabel (fork), das Messer (knife). The single most useful habit is to learn every noun with its article. Do not learn Tisch, learn der Tisch.

Ending or groupUsuallyExamples
-ung, -heit, -keit, -iondiedie Zeitung, die Information
most nouns ending in -ediedie Tasche, die Straße
-chen, -leindasdas Mädchen, das Brötchen
-um, -mentdasdas Museum, das Dokument
days, months, seasonsderder Montag, der Sommer

In a compound word, the last part decides the gender: das Haus plus die Tür gives die Haustür (front door). The words for "a" follow the gender too: ein for masculine and neuter, eine for feminine.

Color-code your notes: blue for der, red for die, green for das. Some learners stick colored notes on the furniture at home. It feels childish, but memory loves color and place, and after a few weeks der Stuhl simply sounds right.

Sein, haben and regular German verbs

Learn these two irregular verbs by heart early, because they appear in almost every conversation.

Personsein (to be)haben (to have)
ich (I)binhabe
du (you, informal)bisthast
er, sie, esisthat
wir (we)sindhaben
ihr (you all, informal)seidhabt
sie, Sie (they, you polite)sindhaben

Regular verbs are friendly. Take the infinitive, such as wohnen (to live), drop -en and add -e, -st, -t, -en, -t, -en: ich wohne, du wohnst, er wohnt, wir wohnen. The same endings work for kommen, lernen, machen, trinken and hundreds more. German has only one present tense, so ich wohne means "I live", "I am living" and "I do live".

Questions are simple too. For yes or no, put the verb first: Wohnen Sie in Berlin? German has no helper word like English "do". For open questions, start with a question word: wo (where), wer (who), was (what), wann (when), wie (how). Watch out for wer, which means who, not where.

The German word order rule that matters most

In a normal German statement, the verb is the second element. Often the subject comes first: Ich lerne Deutsch. If you start with something else, such as a time word, the verb stays in second place and the subject moves after it: Heute lerne ich Deutsch (Today I am learning German). Get this one rule into your ear early and your German will sound right far sooner.

The other early idea is the object of a sentence, called the accusative. The good news is that only masculine words change: der becomes den, and ein becomes einen. Feminine, neuter and plural stay the same.

  • Der Koffer ist schwer. (The suitcase is heavy.)
  • Ich nehme den Koffer. (I'll take the suitcase.)
  • Ich brauche einen Regenschirm. (I need an umbrella.)
  • Es gibt einen Park. (There is a park.)

To say "no" or "not a" before a noun, use kein, which takes the same endings as ein: Das ist kein Problem (That's no problem). Later you will meet the dative, verbs at the end after weil and dass, and separable verbs like aufstehen. Learn them one at a time, after the basics feel easy.

How to practice German every day

Short and daily beats long and occasional. Fifteen focused minutes a day will take you further than a two-hour session every Sunday.

MinutesWhat to do
1 to 3Read yesterday's words aloud, with der, die or das
4 to 9Learn one small new point
10 to 12Say a dialogue twice, once slowly
13 to 15Write three true sentences about your life

Once the basics settle, add listening: slow news and podcasts made for learners first, then children's programs, songs with the lyrics and films with German subtitles. Aim for the gist, not every word. Read graded readers or children's books aloud for a few minutes, and repeat short audio sentences out loud, copying the rhythm.

Then find a person to talk to. A language exchange, a local club or a tutor for 30 minutes a week shows you what to fix. Phrases like "Können Sie das bitte wiederholen?" (Can you repeat that, please?) and "Langsamer, bitte" (Slower, please) keep a conversation going when you get stuck.

Frequently asked questions

Is German hard to learn for English speakers?

German is easier than its reputation. English and German share many words, such as Haus, Hand and Wasser, and German spelling is very regular, so you can read words aloud after a short time. The challenges are noun genders, cases and word order. Learning each noun with its article and the verb-second rule early makes these much more manageable.

How long does it take to learn basic German?

With about 15 minutes a day, most beginners can greet people, order food, buy tickets, ask for directions and talk simply about themselves within about three months. Comfortable, longer conversations take considerably more time and practice. Steady daily habits matter more than long sessions, and regular speaking practice speeds things up a lot.

What is the best way to learn der, die and das?

Learn every noun together with its article, as one unit, and say the article aloud each time. Use ending clues: -ung, -heit and -keit are die, -chen and -lein are das, and days, months and seasons are der. Color-coding your notes by gender helps memory. In compound words, the last part decides the gender.

Should I use du or Sie in German?

Use Sie with adults you do not know well: shop staff, hotel receptionists, officials, your doctor and older people. Pair it with Herr or Frau and a surname. Use du with friends, family and children. When in doubt, start with Sie. People who prefer du will offer it, often saying "Wir können uns duzen."

Can I learn German on my own?

Yes. Many adults learn the basics alone with a clear beginner book, learner audio and daily practice out loud. The important part is speaking from the first week, even just to yourself. After a month or two, add a language exchange partner, a club or a tutor, because real conversations show you what to fix.

Is Austrian and Swiss German different from German in Germany?

Standard German is understood everywhere, but accents and some words differ. In Austria and southern Germany people say Grüß Gott for hello, and in Switzerland Grüezi. Swiss spoken dialects can be hard to follow at first, but people switch to standard German with visitors. Swiss writing also uses ss instead of ß.

Your first step today: spend 15 minutes on the sound rules above, reading Wasser, Zug, Wein, Bier and heute aloud, then say Guten Tag, ich heiße... with your own name and record it. That recording is your day 1, and every short session from here moves you forward.
