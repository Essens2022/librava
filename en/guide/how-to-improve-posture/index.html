How to Improve Posture: Easy Daily Habits and Simple Exercises

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have caught yourself hunched over a laptop, spotted a rounder back in a holiday photo, or feel stiff every time you stand up from a chair, you are not doing anything wrong, and you are certainly not alone. The short answer is that better posture comes from moving more often and getting stronger, not from holding one perfect position: change position every 30 minutes or so, set up your desk and phone so you are not craning forward, open up your chest and upper back with a few stretches, and build back and hip strength two or three times a week.

This guide busts the common myths, then gives you a quick self check, a 2-minute reset routine, a simple workstation setup, phone neck fixes, step by step stretches and strength moves, and a 4-week plan.

Is there such a thing as perfect posture?

No. Bodies come in many natural shapes, and there is no single correct position everyone should hold. Slouching is not damage, and studies suggest the link between posture and pain is weaker than most people think.

What causes most stiffness is staying in any one position for too long, even a so-called good one. Sitting bolt upright all day is tiring and unnecessary. A helpful way to think about it is that your best posture is your next posture: change position often and you will usually feel better.

What really helps is a mix of regular movement, strong and flexible muscles, comfortable setups and variety. A relaxed, upright stance can also help you feel a bit more confident, which is a nice bonus rather than a rule to obey.

Check first. This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor (GP) or physical therapist (UK: physiotherapist) before starting new exercises, especially if you have pain, a health condition, osteoporosis or a history of spine fractures, or are pregnant. Get advice promptly if your symptoms are severe or new.

A quick posture check you can do at home

Try the standing tall wall check. It is not a test, just a way to feel what tall and relaxed is like for your body.

  1. Stand with your back to a wall, heels about a hand's width from it.
  2. Let your bottom, then your shoulder blades, rest near or against the wall without forcing.
  3. Let the back of your head come toward the wall only as far as is comfortable, keeping your chin level, not tipped up.
  4. Breathe normally for five slow breaths and notice how this compares with your usual stance.
  5. Step away and try to keep a little of that feeling as you walk.

If your head stays a few inches away from the wall, that is common, especially with a naturally rounder upper back or later in life. Never crank your head back to touch it.

Over a day, notice when you feel stiff: after a long call, a TV evening, a drive? Those moments are where small changes help most.

Move every 30 minutes with a 2-minute reset

Short, regular breaks do more for posture than one long workout. Aim to stand up and move every 30 minutes or so. Stand for phone calls, take the stairs, walk to the far printer or the kettle.

When you get up, try this 2-minute reset, breathing normally and keeping every movement slow and comfortable:

  1. Reach both arms overhead and stretch gently for two slow breaths.
  2. Roll your shoulders backward five times.
  3. Do three slow chin tucks, sliding your head straight back, then release.
  4. Place your hands on your lower back and lean back just a little for one breath.
  5. March on the spot for 20 steps.
  6. Do 10 calf raises, holding a desk if you like.

To make it easier, do it seated or skip any part that does not feel right. Reminders do not need technology: a glass of water that needs refilling, or a sticky note on the screen, works well.

Good sitting posture at a desk

A good setup makes comfortable positions the easy option. Work through it once and you will rarely need to think about it again.

  • Chair height: feet flat on the floor or a footrest, knees roughly level with your hips.
  • Lower back: supported by the chair, a cushion or a rolled towel.
  • Keyboard and mouse: close to you, elbows near 90 degrees, shoulders relaxed.
  • Screen: about arm's length away, top at or slightly below eye level, straight in front of you.
  • Laptop: raise it on a stand or books and use a separate keyboard and mouse for longer sessions.
  • Lighting: bright enough that you are not leaning in to read; get your eyesight checked if you squint.

Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away for about 20 seconds. If you have a standing desk, alternating between sitting and standing is better than standing all day. On the sofa, use cushions and keep spells short.

How to fix phone neck and forward head posture

Looking down at a phone or tablet for long stretches leaves the neck and upper back working hard to hold your head up. You do not need to give up your phone, just change how you hold it.

  • Hold devices higher, closer to eye level, and bring your eyes down rather than your whole head.
  • Use both hands; it naturally lifts the phone and keeps your shoulders level.
  • Prop up tablets and e-readers on a stand or cushion instead of holding them on your lap.
  • Use the speaker or a headset for calls rather than wedging the phone between ear and shoulder.
  • Keep scrolling sessions shorter and stand up between them.

Chin tucks are a simple counter move. Sit tall, draw your chin straight back as if making a double chin, keeping your eyes level, hold 3 to 5 seconds, then relax. Do 5 to 10 a few times a day.

Stretches for rounded shoulders and a stiff back

Move slowly, stay within comfort and hold stretches for 20 to 30 seconds, 2 or 3 times. Mild stretching discomfort is fine; stop for sharp pain, numbness, tingling, dizziness or weakness.

  • Doorway chest stretch. Rest your forearms on a door frame at shoulder height and step one foot forward until you feel a stretch across your chest.
  • Upper back extension. Sit in a chair with a backrest at about shoulder blade height, hands crossed on your chest, and lean back gently over it. Do 5. You can also lie on a rolled towel placed across your upper back for 30 to 60 seconds.
  • Cat and cow. On hands and knees, slowly round your back, then gently let it sag and lift your head, 8 to 10 rounds.
  • Hip flexor stretch. In a half kneeling or lunge position, tuck your tailbone under until you feel a stretch at the front of the hip.
  • Hamstring stretch. Sit on the edge of a chair with one leg out straight and lean forward from your hips.

These make a good 10-minute morning routine. If you have osteoporosis, check with your doctor or physical therapist before cat and cow, and avoid deep forward bending with loads, sit-ups, toe touches and fast twisting.

Posture exercises to build strength

Strength beats willpower. Strong back, shoulder, hip and core muscles make upright positions feel easy, so you do not have to think about them. Aim for two or three sessions a week.

  1. Shoulder blade squeezes. Sit or stand tall and draw your shoulder blades back and down. Hold 5 seconds, 10 times.
  2. Wall angels. Stand with your back to a wall, arms in a goalpost shape, and slide them up and down slowly, 8 to 10 times. Face the wall for an easier version.
  3. Rows. Pull a resistance band or a water bottle toward your ribs, squeezing your shoulder blades, 10 to 12 times.
  4. Bridges. Lying on your back with knees bent, lift your hips, then lower slowly, 10 to 12 times.
  5. Bird dog. On hands and knees, reach one arm and the opposite leg out, 6 to 8 each side. Use just an arm or a leg to make it easier.
  6. Modified plank. Lean on a wall or kitchen counter with your body in a straight line, holding 10 to 20 seconds, 3 times.
  7. Sit to stand. Lean forward, stand up from a chair, then sit down slowly, 8 to 10 times.

Start with one round and the easier options, breathe normally, then add a round or more repetitions as it gets easier.

A 4-week plan to improve your posture

Go at your own pace and repeat any week that feels like too much.

2Workstation check, back roll, phone held higherAdd doorway and upper back stretches; 2 sessions

Adults are generally advised to aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus strength work on two days, so walking and the strength routine fit together nicely. When posture problems need a doctor

See a doctor or physical therapist if you have:

  • Pain that lasts more than a few weeks or keeps coming back
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in your arms or legs
  • A stoop or curve that is changing quickly, or noticeable height loss
  • New or worsening uneven shoulders or hips, especially in teenagers
  • Back stiffness that is worse in the morning, lasts more than about 30 minutes and eases with movement
  • Posture changes with new symptoms such as a tremor or shuffling walk

Get urgent care for back or neck pain after a fall or injury, sudden back pain with osteoporosis, loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the genitals or buttocks, or new leg weakness. For chest pain, call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand).

Frequently asked questions

Can you really improve your posture?

Yes, most people can feel more comfortable and stand taller with less effort. The changes come from moving more often, setting up your desk and phone sensibly, and building strength and flexibility over a few weeks. Your natural shape will not change completely, and it does not need to. Comfort and ease of movement are the real goals.

How long does it take to improve posture?

Many people notice they feel looser within a week or two of regular movement breaks and stretches. Strength takes longer, often several weeks of two or three sessions a week. Treat it as a set of habits rather than a quick fix, and review how you feel after four weeks.

Do posture correctors and braces work?

Braces may remind you to sit up, but they do not build the strength that makes upright positions feel easy, and wearing one for long periods can leave muscles doing less work. Regular movement, a sensible desk setup and simple strength exercises are a better long-term approach. Ask a physical therapist if you are unsure.

How should I sit at a desk for good posture?

Sit with your feet flat, knees about level with your hips and your lower back supported. Keep the screen about arm's length away with the top at or slightly below eye level, and your keyboard close with elbows near 90 degrees. Most importantly, stand up and move every 30 minutes or so.

How do I fix rounded shoulders?

Combine stretches that open the front of your chest, such as the doorway chest stretch and upper back extension, with exercises that strengthen the muscles between your shoulder blades, such as rows, wall angels and shoulder blade squeezes. Hold your phone higher and break up long spells at a screen. Give it several weeks of steady practice.

Is it bad to sit cross-legged?

No, sitting cross-legged or curled up on the sofa is fine in moderation. The problem is staying in any one position for a long time, not the position itself. Change position often, get up every 30 minutes or so, and you can enjoy whichever comfortable positions you like.

Your first step today is the wall check: five slow breaths standing tall against a wall, then set a simple reminder to get up and do the 2-minute reset every 30 minutes or so this afternoon. Small, regular changes like these add up far faster than trying to sit perfectly straight all day.
