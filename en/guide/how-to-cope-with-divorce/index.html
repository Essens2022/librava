How to Cope With Divorce: A Kind, Practical Plan for the Hard Weeks

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If your relationship has just ended and you feel foggy, frightened or numb, nothing is wrong with you. To cope with divorce, focus first on getting through each day safely, keep the basics of food and sleep going, avoid big decisions or signatures until you have advice, and lean on one or two trusted people while the feelings come and go in waves.

You do not have to fix everything this month. This guide walks you through the first weeks, the big emotions, the first money and paperwork steps, telling the children, dealing with your ex and slowly building a life that feels like yours again. It applies to married and unmarried couples alike.

Why divorce hurts so much

The end of a long relationship is a shock to your whole system, even if you saw it coming. Your heart races, your stomach knots, sleep disappears and your thinking goes foggy.

This is survival mode, and it is normal. Your brain is trying to protect you while it catches up with a huge change. For most people the fog starts to thin within weeks, even if the sadness takes longer.

It also helps to see that you are grieving more than a person. You are grieving routines, shared jokes, family holidays and the future you had planned. Because your ex is still around, at school pickups or in messages about the car insurance, this grief can feel messier than other losses. That does not make it smaller.

What to do in the first weeks

Sort everything into today, this week and later. Very few things truly need doing today. These usually do:

  1. Safety first. If you feel unsafe in any way, get confidential help before anything else (see the note further down).
  2. Keep children fed, at school and with a calm adult. Their days should feel as normal as possible.
  3. Secure your own accounts. Change passwords on your email, phone and banking if your partner knows them, turn on extra login checks and check if your phone shares its location with anyone.
  4. Note where key documents are kept, such as bank statements, pay slips and mortgage or lease papers.
  5. Keep one notebook for dates, conversations, questions and anything you agree to. Your memory will be unreliable for a while.

Just as useful is a list of things not to do yet:

  • Do not sign anything about money, property or children without advice.
  • Do not move out of the family home or move children somewhere new before getting advice, unless you need to leave for your safety, because it can affect later arrangements.
  • Do not move, hide or spend shared money in unusual ways.
  • Do not post about the separation online or send long emotional messages to your ex. Write them if you need to, then keep them as drafts.

Basic self-care when you can barely function

In the first weeks, self-care means the bare minimum that keeps your body running. Aim low and count every small win.

  • Food: eat something small every few hours, even if it is not a meal. Toast, soup, yogurt, eggs, bananas and crackers often go down easiest. Accept the lasagna from a neighbor.
  • Sleep: keep a steady wake time, put your phone out of reach at night and keep a notepad by the bed for worries you can look at in the morning.
  • Movement: a ten-minute walk or some daylight in the morning calms your nervous system more than you would expect.
  • Breathing: when panic rises, breathe in through your nose for a count of four and out slowly for six, five times.

Alcohol can feel like it helps, but it tends to wake you in the night and make anxiety worse the next day. If you notice you are drinking more than usual, try gently to cut back.

This guide is general support, not medical, legal or financial advice. See your doctor (GP) or a counselor if low mood, anxiety, poor sleep or heavy drinking last more than a couple of weeks, or if you struggle with work, children or basic tasks. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, reach out now: in the US or Canada call or text 988, in the UK call 999 in an emergency, NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123, in Australia call Lifeline on 13 11 14, in New Zealand call or text 1737.

How to handle the emotional waves

Grief after a breakup does not move in neat stages. It comes in waves. A good week can be followed by sobbing on the floor because you found an old pair of their reading glasses. Over time the calm stretches usually get longer, but there is no fixed timeline, and comparing yourself to others rarely helps.

Naming the feeling takes away some of its power:

  • Anger often shows where your limits were crossed. Walk it off, write it out or vent to a friend, but keep it out of messages, social media and arguments in front of the children.
  • Guilt usually sounds like "if only." Taking responsibility for your part is healthy. Taking responsibility for all of it is neither accurate nor kind.
  • Relief is common if things were tense for a long time.
  • Fear tends to arrive at night. Ask, "What do I know for sure today?" and "What is one small thing I can do tomorrow?" Write the answers and put the paper away.
  • Loneliness hits hardest in quiet moments, like cooking for one or a weekend when the children are with their other parent.

When a wave hits, let it rise and pass instead of fighting it: cry, breathe, make a cup of tea, then do the next small thing.

Plan ahead for hard dates such as anniversaries, birthdays and the first holiday season. Book a walk, a film or a long call for those days, and consider one new tradition rather than copying the old one with an empty chair.

Telling the children and helping them cope

Where it is safe, plan the conversation together and tell the children at the same time, with the same simple message. Whatever their age, make sure they hear four things:

  • This is a grown-up decision, and it is not their fault.
  • Both parents still love them, and that will not change.
  • What will stay the same, such as school, friends and pets.
  • What happens next, in simple, practical terms.

Use words that suit each child's age and expect reactions to arrive over weeks, not just on the day. Keep routines steady in both homes, and keep children out of adult conflict. Never use them as messengers, ask them to choose or criticize the other parent in front of them.

Let the school know in general terms so teachers can keep an eye out. If worrying changes in sleep, eating, mood or behavior last more than a few weeks, talk to the child's doctor or school. If a child is at risk, contact child protection or emergency services.

First money and legal steps to take

Separation law differs between countries and within them, and rules for unmarried couples are often different from those for married ones. So the most useful step is to get local advice early: official government information for your area, a free legal advice service, legal aid where available, or a family lawyer (attorney in the US, solicitor in the UK).

Meanwhile, you can prepare:

  • Gather copies of documents: ID, marriage certificate, tax returns, pay slips, bank and credit card statements, mortgage or lease, retirement account (pension) statements, debts, insurance and wills.
  • List everything you own and owe, marking each item as joint or yours.
  • Open an account in your own name for your income.
  • Joint accounts and debts usually stay the responsibility of both people until they are changed or closed, so get advice before closing anything, and avoid taking on new joint debts.
  • Check your credit report through official free options.
  • Make a simple one-household budget, and contact a free debt or money advice service early if things look tight.

Mediation and other ways of agreeing outside court are often quicker, cheaper and less stressful, though they are not suitable where there is abuse.

How to deal with your ex calmly

Staying civil is mostly for your own benefit. It protects your energy, keeps costs down and shields the children. Think of your ex as a business colleague in the job of raising your children, if you have them.

Choose one way of contacting each other and two set times a day to check it, then turn off notifications in between. When you reply, keep messages brief, informative, friendly and firm: a few sentences, facts only, a polite tone and a clear answer. For example: "Thanks for letting me know. I can do pickup at 5 on Friday instead of 6. Please confirm by Wednesday."

If a message is full of bait or blame, answer only the practical part, or nothing at all if nothing practical is needed. Keep handovers short and predictable, at school or a neutral place if needed. When conflict stays high, parallel parenting with minimal written contact can protect the children.

Closure is something you build yourself. You do not need your ex to explain, apologize or agree with your version of events in order to move forward.

Controlling behavior, threats, stalking, monitoring, financial control and violence are abuse, and they are never your fault. Leaving can be a time of higher risk, so plan with a free, confidential domestic abuse helpline or local support service, use a safe device and do not use co-parenting or mediation advice in that situation. In immediate danger call 911 (US and Canada), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 111 (New Zealand).

How to move on and rebuild your life

Moving on rarely happens in one big leap. It grows from small anchors that give shape to days that suddenly feel empty: a regular walk, a weekly call, Sunday breakfast at the same café.

  • Plan child-free or quiet weekends in advance, with something outside, someone to see and something just for you.
  • Rediscover what you actually like. Small choices about food, music and how you spend a free evening slowly rebuild your sense of who you are.
  • Expect some friendships to change. It is common and not personal. New friends usually come from seeing the same people regularly, so try a weekly class, club or volunteering shift.
  • Write an "I did that" list of everything you have handled so far. It is usually longer than you think.
  • Set one or two goals for the next 3, 6 and 12 months, covering home, money, parenting and something just for you.

Dating can wait until you genuinely want it, and there is no rush. The book Life After Divorce Made Simple includes a 30-day rebuilding plan of small daily steps if you like structure. Setbacks along the way are normal and do not cancel your progress.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get over a divorce?

There is no fixed timeline, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. The early shock and fog often begin to thin within weeks, while the deeper grief can take months or longer. A long marriage, a sudden ending, money worries or a difficult ex can all slow recovery. Most people notice the calm stretches getting longer and the hard waves getting smaller over time.

What should I do first when separating?

Start with safety, then the children's routines. Change passwords on your own email, phone and banking, note where key documents are kept and write down every conversation and agreement. Hold off on signing anything, moving out or moving money until you have spoken to a free legal advice service or a family lawyer (solicitor) in your area.

Is it normal to feel relieved after a divorce?

Yes. If the relationship was tense, cold or exhausting, relief is very common, often mixed with sadness, guilt and fear in the same afternoon. Feeling lighter does not mean the relationship meant nothing. It usually means part of you had been carrying something heavy for a long time. You do not need to feel guilty about it.

How do I stop thinking about my ex all the time?

Reduce the triggers you control: limit contact to set times, mute their social media and put away reminders for now. When thoughts loop, write them down and choose one small action for tomorrow. Fill quiet times with plans made in advance. If the same painful thoughts fill every day for weeks, a counselor or doctor (GP) can help.

How do I co-parent with a difficult ex?

Keep contact in writing and businesslike: short, factual, polite and clear. Answer only the practical part of a provocative message. Keep handovers brief and predictable, ideally at school or a neutral place. When conflict stays high, parallel parenting, where each home runs separately with minimal contact, can protect the children. If there is abuse, get specialist advice instead.

Should I move out of the family home?

Get advice before you decide, unless you need to leave for your safety, in which case leave and get help. Moving out, or moving children, can affect later arrangements about the home and the children, and rules vary by country and region. Keep paying the mortgage or rent if you can, and talk to the lender or landlord early if you are struggling.

Take one small step today: on a single sheet of paper, write one task for each of the next seven days, such as changing a password, telling one trusted person or finding your local free legal advice service, and add one kind thing for yourself each day. Put it on the fridge and cross off today's task.
