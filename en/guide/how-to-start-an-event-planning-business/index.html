How to Start an Event Planning Business From Home, Step by Step

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If friends already hand you the clipboard for every birthday, shower and office party, you may be wondering whether you could turn that talent into paid work. To start an event planning business, you test the work on a few small events, choose a niche and clear services, set up the business legally with insurance and a lawyer-reviewed contract, work out a price that covers your time, and then build a portfolio and vendor network to win your first clients.

It is a real business with long weekends and busy seasons, and building a client base takes time. This guide covers each step in order, with the checklists experienced planners rely on, so you can start small and professional rather than big and stressed.

What does an event planner actually do

An event planner works for the client. You help them decide what they want, set a budget, find and book the venue and vendors (suppliers in the UK), build the timeline, keep track of money and paperwork, and run the day so the client can enjoy it.

Other people at an event have different jobs. A venue coordinator works for the venue and looks after its rooms, staff and rules. A caterer handles food and drink. You connect everyone and keep them working to the same plan, and you never promise something on a vendor's behalf that they have not agreed to in writing.

Behind the pretty photos, most of the work is quiet admin: emails, spreadsheets, contracts, confirmations and problem solving. Event days often mean long hours on your feet, frequently on weekends and evenings, with peaks in the busiest seasons. If that sounds energizing rather than exhausting, read on.

Test the work before you launch

The safest way to find out whether you will enjoy this work is to do some of it before you call it a business. Offer to coordinate a relative's milestone birthday, a friend's baby shower or a colleague's leaving party. Volunteer for a charity fundraiser, school fair or community festival, or offer to assist an established planner on a few events.

Treat each trial like a real job:

  • Agree in writing what you will handle.
  • Build a simple budget, a planning timeline and a run sheet for the day.
  • Track every hour you spend, including messages and shopping.
  • Ask for honest feedback afterward, and permission to photograph the setup.

Keep trial events small, ideally between 15 and 50 guests. Do not take on a full wedding for a friend as your first project. High emotions, large budgets and many vendors make it a hard place to learn, and a disappointed friend is difficult to win back.

Choose a niche and the services you offer

A clear focus makes it easier for clients to understand you and for venues to recommend you. Common event types include weddings, private parties and milestones, children's parties, corporate meetings and team days, nonprofit fundraisers, and cultural and religious celebrations. Look at what your area needs, what you already know and which events you enjoy.

Then decide how much of each event you will handle:

ServiceWhat it includes
Full planningEverything from budget and venue search to running the day, often starting many months ahead
Partial planningTaking over once the client has booked some parts, with a clear written split of tasks
Day-of coordinationSeveral weeks of handover, confirmations and a run sheet, then running the day
Design onlyConcept, color palette, décor and layout, with or without styling on the day
A la carteSingle services such as a venue search or budget setup session

Day-of coordination is a popular starting point because it gives you event-day experience without months of planning. Write down exactly what it includes, including the number of hours, meetings and calls, so it does not quietly grow into partial planning.

Set up your event planning business legally

Most small planners start as a sole proprietor (sole trader in the UK), a partnership or a limited company or LLC. The simplest structures mean less paperwork but leave you personally liable, while a company can limit personal liability with more admin. Names and rules differ between countries, so speak to an accountant or a free small business advice service before you choose.

  • Register with your government or tax office as required where you live. Some places also need a general business license.
  • Check that your business name is available and not too close to an existing business.
  • Check whether your lease, zoning or planning rules allow a home-based business.
  • Open a separate business bank account, and never mix client money with your own.
  • Keep records of every invoice, receipt and payment from the first day.
  • Set aside money for taxes. Income tax and sales tax, VAT or GST rules vary by country, state and province.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice, so confirm the details for your own area.

Insurance and contracts that protect you

Events involve crowds, cables, candles, alcohol and weather, and many venues will not let you work without proof of insurance. Ask an insurance broker who works with small businesses about general liability insurance (public liability in the UK, Australia and New Zealand), professional liability or indemnity insurance, and employer's liability or workers' compensation if you hire helpers. Venues often ask for a certificate of insurance. You can also suggest that clients consider event cancellation insurance for weddings and large events.

Every client needs a written contract, and your template should be reviewed by a lawyer where you work, because consumer protection law varies. A good contract covers:

  • the parties, event details and a clear scope of services, including what is not included
  • fees, payment schedule and whether deposits are refundable or non-refundable
  • cancellation, postponement and events beyond anyone's control
  • how changes and extra hours are agreed and charged
  • liability limits and client responsibilities, such as dietary information by a deadline
  • how vendors are booked and paid, and any referral fees you receive
  • photo permission and how guest data is kept private
  • signatures and dates from both sides
Never work an event without insurance in place, and never accept your first paid booking until your contract has been reviewed. One accident or dispute can cost far more than years of premiums and a lawyer's fee.

How to price event planning services

Start by working out your floor, the lowest price at which a job still pays you fairly. Add up three things: a share of your yearly business costs (insurance, phone, software, website, training, your emergency kit), the direct costs of the event (travel, parking, printing, assistant wages), and every hour you will spend, from the first enquiry call to the follow-up email, at a fair hourly wage. Then add a profit margin so the business can survive quiet months.

MethodWorks well forWatch out for
Flat packageDay-of, small parties, set servicesHours creeping beyond plan
Hourly rateConsultations, corporate work, extrasClients worrying about every call
Percentage of event budgetLarge weddings and eventsLooks as if you gain from higher spending
A la carteClients who need light helpToo many tiny jobs

If you charge a percentage, set a minimum fee and define what counts as the total budget. Add minimums for busy-season weekends and travel fees beyond a set area. Avoid quoting on the first phone call before you know what the event involves, and review your prices at least once a year. Rates vary hugely by location, so research what established planners near you charge.

How to find your first event planning clients

Clients want to see what you can do before they trust you with a big day. Build a small, honest portfolio from friends' events (with permission), volunteer events and styled shoots, where a group of vendors creates a staged setup for photos. Always describe your role accurately, label a styled shoot as a styled shoot, credit the photographer and never use images from other planners.

Then put yourself where clients look:

  • A simple website with your services, area, portfolio and an enquiry form.
  • One or two social media accounts you can keep up with.
  • Visits to local venues, where coordinators often recommend reliable planners.
  • Friendly relationships with caterers, florists, photographers and rental (hire) companies.
  • Kind words from every happy client, used with permission.

Reply to enquiries within one working day. On the first call, which usually takes 15 to 20 minutes, ask about the event type, date, venue, guest numbers, what is already booked, what help they want and a rough budget range. Follow with a short proposal in the client's own words, listing what is included, what is not, the fee, the payment schedule and how to book.

Work with vendors and keep events safe

Build a trusted vendor list and check each vendor's insurance and any licenses, such as food hygiene registration for caterers. Confirm every booking in writing, with arrival times, setup needs and payment terms. If a vendor offers you a referral fee or commission, tell your client in writing, and never add hidden markups.

Safety is part of the job. For every event, run through a plain risk assessment:

  • Venue capacity, clear fire exits and an emergency plan with first aid.
  • A weather plan for anything outdoors, with tents or marquees installed and secured by professionals.
  • Taped or covered cables, no overloaded extension leads, and candles only where the venue allows.
  • Guest allergy and dietary information passed to licensed caterers, with allergens labeled at buffets.
  • Alcohol served only under the right license by trained staff, with water, transport options and no service to minors.
  • Step-free access, accessible restrooms (toilets), seating and a quiet space, with guests asked about needs in the invitation.
  • A lost child and vulnerable guest plan, and an incident log.

Permits may be needed for public spaces, amplified music, temporary structures or food stalls, so check with your local council or city early. Fireworks belong only with licensed professionals.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need a certification to be an event planner?

In most places, no specific certification is legally required to plan events. Clients care about your experience, organization, reviews and portfolio. Some planners choose a course to build confidence, but practical experience from trial and volunteer events matters more at the start. You do still need to follow local business registration, insurance, licensing and safety rules, which vary by area.

Can I start an event planning business with no experience?

You can start with no paid experience, but not with none at all. Coordinate a few small events for friends, family or community groups, volunteer at larger events, or assist an established planner. Track your hours, gather feedback and photos with permission, and you will have a small portfolio and a realistic sense of the work before your first paying client.

What insurance does an event planner need?

Most planners need general liability insurance (public liability in the UK, Australia and New Zealand) for injuries or property damage, and professional liability or indemnity insurance for claims about your advice or service. If you employ helpers, employer's liability or workers' compensation may be required by law. Speak to a broker and check what your venues ask for.

How do event planners charge for their services?

Common methods are a flat package fee, an hourly rate, a percentage of the total event budget, a set fee for day-of coordination, and a la carte pricing for single services. Many planners combine them. Whatever you choose, base it on your real costs, hours and a profit margin, write it down and apply it consistently to similar clients.

Is event planning a good business to start from home?

It can suit organized people who enjoy problem solving and can handle weekend work, seasonal peaks and long days on their feet. Startup costs can be modest, since much of the work is done from a home office. However, building a steady client base takes time, and you will need insurance, contracts and safety know-how from the very first paid event.

What is the difference between an event planner and a day-of coordinator?

A full event planner is involved from early on, helping set the budget, find the venue, book vendors and design the event. A day-of coordinator steps in for clients who planned the event themselves, usually starting several weeks before to confirm vendors, build the run sheet and visit the venue, then runs the day so the client can relax.

Your first step today is to choose one small gathering, such as a friend's birthday or a club social, and offer to coordinate it properly: a written list of what you will handle, a budget, a timeline and a run sheet. Track your hours as you go. By the end, you will know whether you would happily do it again for a paying client, and you will have your first portfolio example.
