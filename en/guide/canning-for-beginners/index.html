Canning for Beginners: How to Can Food Safely at Home, Step by Step

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Maybe your garden has gone wild with tomatoes, or you found flats of strawberries at the market and want summer in a jar. The short answer to safe home canning is this: high-acid foods such as jams, pickles and acidified tomatoes go in a boiling water bath, low-acid foods such as plain vegetables, meat and stock must go in a pressure canner, and you always follow a tested recipe exactly.

Get those three ideas right and the rest is a calm, repeatable routine. This guide explains why the rules exist, walks you through a water bath canning day from start to finish, and shows you how to check seals, store jars and spot spoilage.

How canning keeps food safe

Food spoils because of molds, yeasts, bacteria and the food's own enzymes. Canning works by heating filled jars long enough to destroy the organisms that could grow inside, then driving out air so the lid seals as the jar cools. A sealed, properly processed jar keeps out anything new.

The number that decides everything is pH 4.6. Foods at or below it are high-acid. Foods above it are low-acid, and that line decides which method a food needs.

The reason is botulism. It is rare and preventable, but serious. The spores of the bacterium behind it live in soil and survive boiling water. In a sealed jar of low-acid food at room temperature, any surviving spores can grow and make toxin, and a jar with toxin can look, smell and taste normal. Destroying the spores takes about 240°F (116°C), which you can only reach at home under pressure. In acid foods the spores cannot grow, so a boiling water bath is enough.

Botulism symptoms can include double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble swallowing and muscle weakness. Anyone with these symptoms after eating home-canned food needs emergency medical care straight away. Never taste food from a jar you are unsure about.

Water bath vs pressure canning

Water bath canning means processing jars fully covered in boiling water, 212°F (100°C) at sea level. Any large, deep pot with a lid and a rack works. Pressure canning uses a heavy pot with a locking lid and a gauge to reach about 240°F (116°C). It must be a pressure canner designed for canning, not an electric multi-cooker or a small pressure cooker.

Tomatoes sit close to the line, so tested recipes always add acid: 2 tablespoons bottled lemon juice or 1/2 teaspoon citric acid per quart, and 1 tablespoon lemon juice or 1/4 teaspoon citric acid per pint. Use bottled lemon juice, since fresh lemons vary, and vinegar labeled at least 5 percent acidity. A mixture such as a vegetable pasta sauce counts as low-acid unless a tested recipe has acidified it.

Why you should only use tested canning recipes

A tested recipe was processed in a lab with temperature probes in the slowest-heating spot of the jar. The processing time comes from that test, plus a safety margin, and it only applies to that exact recipe and jar size.

Add extra onions, cut chunks bigger, thicken a sauce or use quarts instead of pints, and heat moves through the jar differently. Extra low-acid vegetables can also push the pH above the safe line without you knowing. So the firm rule is do not change the proportions of vegetables, vinegar or acid. You can usually change dried herbs and spices, and leave salt out of most canned vegetables.

A jar that seals is not proof it was processed safely. If you love an old family recipe, find the closest tested version, or make yours as a refrigerator or freezer preserve instead.

Canning equipment for beginners

You need less than you might think for water bath canning:

  • A large, deep pot with a lid and a rack that keeps jars off the bottom
  • Canning jars in half-pint (about 250 ml), pint (about 500 ml) or quart (about 1 liter) sizes
  • New flat lids for every batch, plus screw bands that are not rusted or bent
  • A jar lifter, a wide-mouth funnel and a plastic bubble remover with headspace notches
  • Stainless steel or enamel pans, since acid foods react with aluminum and copper
  • Clean towels, a timer, labels and a waterproof marker

Jars can be reused for years, but run a finger round each rim and discard any with a chip. Do not reuse jars from shop-bought foods, which are often thinner and may not seal. In the UK, Australia and New Zealand you will also see clip-top jars and glass-lid jars. If you use those, choose ones designed for heat processing and follow the jar maker's instructions, though two-piece lid jars match how most tested recipes were developed.

How to water bath can step by step

Read the whole recipe first and set everything out before you start.

  1. Check jars for chips, then wash jars, lids and bands in hot, soapy water.
  2. Stand the jars on the rack in the canner, cover with water and keep at a simmer, about 180°F (82°C), until needed.
  3. Prepare the food exactly as the recipe says.
  4. Lift out one hot jar at a time and fill it through the funnel, leaving the recipe's headspace.
  5. Run the bubble remover round the inside, then recheck headspace.
  6. Wipe the rim and threads with a clean, damp cloth.
  7. Center a new lid and screw the band on fingertip tight.
  8. Lower the jar onto the rack and repeat, keeping jars about 1 inch apart.
  9. Make sure water covers the jars by at least 1 inch (2.5 cm).
  10. Cover the pot and bring it to a full rolling boil. Start timing only now, and if the boil stops, restart the timing from the beginning.
  11. When time is up, turn off the heat, remove the lid and leave the jars in for 5 minutes.
  12. Lift them straight out onto a towel, away from drafts, and leave them alone for 12 to 24 hours.

A classic first project is strawberry jam made with regular powdered pectin: crushed berries are boiled with the pectin, all the sugar goes in at once, it boils hard for exactly 1 minute, and half-pint jars are processed for 10 minutes at low altitude. Do not cut the sugar in regular pectin recipes, because it helps the jam set and keep. Use low or no sugar pectin and its own instructions if you want less.

Headspace and altitude adjustments

Headspace is the gap between the food and the jar rim. Too little and food bubbles out and spoils the seal. Too much and air may not be driven out. Unless a recipe says otherwise, leave:

  • 1/4 inch (6 mm) for jams and jellies
  • 1/2 inch (12 mm) for fruits, tomatoes and pickles
  • 1 inch (2.5 cm) for low-acid vegetables in a pressure canner

Water boils at a lower temperature the higher you live, so you need extra time in a water bath or more pressure in a pressure canner. Pressure canning times stay the same, only the pressure changes.

Pressure canning basics for low-acid foods

Jars are filled the same way, usually with 1 inch of headspace. The difference is how you run the canner, so follow your manual alongside these general steps.

  1. Add the amount of hot water your manual specifies, often 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7.5 cm), and set the filled jars on the rack.
  2. Fasten the lid with the weight off or the petcock open.
  3. Heat until steam flows steadily, then let it vent for a full 10 minutes to drive out air.
  4. Put on the weight or close the petcock and let pressure build.
  5. Start timing when you reach the pressure for your altitude. If it drops below, bring it back up and restart the timing.
  6. Turn off the heat and let the canner lose pressure naturally. Never cool it with water.
  7. Once it is fully depressurized, remove the weight, wait 10 minutes and open the lid away from your face.

Green beans, carrots and chicken stock are popular first pressure canning projects once you are comfortable with the water bath.

Checking seals and storing your jars

After 12 to 24 hours, press the center of each lid. A sealed lid curves down slightly and does not move. Remove the bands and gently lift each jar by the edge of its lid. If it holds firm, it is sealed.

If a jar has not sealed within 24 hours, you can reprocess it with a new lid and the full time, or simply refrigerate it and use it within a few days. Label each sealed jar with its contents and date, and store without bands somewhere cool, dark and dry, ideally 50 to 70°F (10 to 21°C). Use within about 1 year for best quality.

Signs of spoilage

Do not taste a jar, and throw it away safely, if you see any of these:

  • A bulging, swollen or unsealed lid
  • Leaks or dried streaks down the outside
  • Liquid spurting out when opened
  • Mold, a bad smell, rising bubbles or unexpected cloudiness

Wear disposable gloves, bag a sealed suspect jar tightly and put it out of reach of people and pets. For leaking jars, follow current official guidance on handling them first. Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest thing to can for a beginner?

Jam is the classic first project because it is high-acid, uses a simple water bath and needs only a short processing time. Strawberry jam with regular pectin, applesauce and dill pickle spears from a tested recipe are all good starting points. Leave low-acid foods like green beans until you have a pressure canner and a few water bath batches behind you.

What foods can be water bath canned?

High-acid foods can be water bath canned. That includes most fruits, jams, jellies, marmalades, fruit sauces and butters, pickles and relishes made with tested amounts of vinegar, tested salsas, and tomatoes with added lemon juice or citric acid. Plain vegetables, meat, poultry, stock and dried beans are low-acid and must be pressure canned instead.

Can you can vegetables without a pressure canner?

Not plain vegetables. Green beans, corn, carrots and potatoes are low-acid, and boiling water never gets hot enough to destroy botulinum spores, however long you boil. Your options without a pressure canner are to pickle them using a tested recipe with enough vinegar, or to freeze them after blanching. Electric multi-cookers are not a safe substitute.

How do you know if a canned jar sealed?

After 12 to 24 hours, press the middle of the lid. A sealed lid is slightly curved down and does not flex or pop. Then remove the band and lift the jar gently by the lid edge. If the lid holds, the seal is good. Unsealed jars can be reprocessed within 24 hours with a new lid or kept in the fridge and used soon.

How long do home canned foods last?

Properly processed and sealed jars are best used within about one year. After that they may still be safe if the seal holds, but color, flavor and texture slowly decline. Store jars without bands in a cool, dark, dry place, ideally 50 to 70°F (10 to 21°C), and always check for bulging lids, leaks, mold or a bad smell before using.

Do you need to sterilize jars for canning?

Only when a recipe processes jars for less than 10 minutes. In that case, boil clean jars for 10 minutes, plus 1 minute for every 1,000 feet of altitude, before filling. For longer processing times, jars that have been washed in hot, soapy water and kept hot until filling are enough, because the processing itself does the work.

Your first step today is to look up your altitude, then choose one tested high-acid recipe such as strawberry jam or applesauce and gather a deep pot with a rack, a few new lids and a jar lifter. One small, careful batch will show you how calm and satisfying canning day can be.
