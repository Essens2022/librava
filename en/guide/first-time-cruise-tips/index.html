First Time Cruise Tips: What to Know Before You Book and Sail

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

Planning your first cruise can feel like learning a new language, with talk of tender ports, guarantee cabins and all aboard times. The most useful first time cruise tips are simple: choose a 7-night sailing with a mix of sea and port days, budget for the extras the fare leaves out, pick a midship cabin if you worry about motion, pack your essentials in a carry-on, and always be back on board well before the all aboard time.

This guide walks you through each stage in order, from choosing a ship and booking a fair fare to boarding day, life at sea and port days, with checklists you can use straight away.

Choose the right type of cruise for you

Cruises are not all alike, and picking the wrong style is the most common reason a first cruise disappoints.

TypeWhat it feels likeSuits
Big resort shipFloating resort with many restaurants, shows, pools and kids clubsFamilies, groups, people who like lots to do
Mid-size shipGood choice of dining, calmer, easier to find your wayCouples, retirees, first timers wanting balance
Small shipQuiet, personal, smaller portsTravelers who prefer destinations over onboard activities
River cruiseCalm water, a new town almost every day, often more inclusiveCulture lovers, anyone nervous about seasickness
ExpeditionRemote places, wildlife, lecturesAdventurous travelers with a bigger budget

Read descriptions carefully. A ship described as "lively" may be noisy late at night, and one called "traditional" may have more formal dress codes. Ask yourself whether the trip is about the ship or about the places, and choose accordingly.

How long should a first cruise be

Seven nights is the classic first cruise. It gives you time to settle in, see a few ports and enjoy one or two relaxing sea days. Short sailings of 3 to 5 nights are a good taster if you are unsure about motion or crowds, though they are often busy with weekend groups and celebrations. If you are flying a long way, such as from the UK to the Caribbean, 10 to 14 nights makes the travel time worthwhile.

For a first trip, many people enjoy roughly one sea day for every two or three port days. Choose more ports if you get restless and more sea days if you want a proper rest.

  • Check hurricane, cyclone or monsoon seasons for your region. The Atlantic hurricane season, for example, runs roughly June to November.
  • Look for tender ports, where small boats carry you ashore. They are fine for most people but can be hard for wheelchair users and may be cancelled in rough seas.
  • Round trips from a port you can reach easily are simpler and often cheaper than one-way sailings.

What a cruise fare includes and leaves out

On most ocean cruises the basic fare covers your cabin, meals in the main dining room and buffet, some casual eateries, water, tea, coffee and breakfast juice, most entertainment, pools, the gym and kids clubs. Getting from port to port is included too, which is the real value of a cruise.

Budgets go wrong on the extras. Depending on the line and fare, you may pay for:

  • Port fees, taxes and government charges
  • Gratuities or daily service charges per guest
  • Alcohol, sodas (soft drinks), specialty coffee and bottled water
  • Specialty restaurants and internet access
  • Shore excursions, spa treatments and photos
  • Flights, hotels and transfers to the port
  • Travel insurance

More inclusive fares, common on small luxury ships and many river cruises, may cover drinks, tips, internet and some tours. They look pricier at first but can be better value once you add up the extras. Fares move constantly, so check the current fare and what it includes before you decide.

Popular sailings, school vacation weeks and specialty cabins such as accessible or connecting cabins are often booked 9 to 18 months ahead. Last-minute deals exist, but choice is limited and late flights can be expensive.

Location matters as much as type. Look at the deck plan above, below and beside any cabin. Common noise sources include theaters, bars and nightclubs, the pool deck where loungers scrape early in the morning, galleys (kitchens), elevators (lifts) and the very front or back of the ship where anchors and thrusters work in port. A cabin with other cabins all around it is usually the quietest.

If you worry about seasickness, choose a lower deck toward the middle of the ship (midship), where movement is smallest. A window or balcony can help, because seeing the horizon helps your brain match what your body feels.

Documents, insurance and health before you sail

  • Bring a valid passport. Some round-trip cruises from a US port may accept other proof of citizenship for US citizens, but a passport is the safest choice in case you must fly home from a foreign port. Many countries require it to be valid at least 6 months beyond your travel dates.
  • Check visa and entry rules, including electronic travel authorizations, for every country on the itinerary using official government travel advice. Rules vary by nationality.
  • Children traveling with one parent may need a signed consent letter from the other parent.
  • Buy travel insurance when you book. Check that it covers cruise travel, medical treatment abroad, medical evacuation, missed port departures and cancellation, and declare existing medical conditions. Onboard medical centers charge fees, and your home health plan may not cover care at sea.
  • Pack all prescription medicines in your carry-on, with a few spare days' supply.
  • If you are pregnant or traveling with a baby, check the line's policy, since many set limits such as no travel beyond about 24 weeks of pregnancy and minimum infant ages.
If you are prone to motion sickness, talk to your doctor or pharmacist before you travel about medicines or patches. Some cause drowsiness and are not suitable for everyone, especially if you have a health condition, take other medicines or are pregnant. Fresh air and looking at the horizon help many people too.

What to pack for a cruise

Most people overpack. For a 7-night cruise, a sensible base list looks like this:

  • 5 to 7 daytime outfits and 4 to 5 smart casual evening outfits
  • 1 or 2 dressier outfits if your sailing has formal nights
  • 2 swimsuits (swimming costumes), so one can dry, plus a cover-up for walking through the ship
  • A light sweater (jumper) for cold air conditioning and a waterproof jacket for cooler regions
  • Comfortable walking shoes for ports and sandals for the pool
  • Sunscreen, sunglasses, a hat and a refillable water bottle
  • A non-surge travel charger with several USB ports

Your suitcases are handed to porters at the terminal and may not reach your cabin until early evening. Pack a carry-on with passports and cruise documents, insurance details, all medicines, phone and charger, valuables, a swimsuit and a change of clothes for dinner.

Commonly banned or restricted items include clothes irons and steamers, candles and incense, surge protectors and some power strips (extension leads), and drones. Rules on bringing your own alcohol or soft drinks vary a lot, so check your line's list before you pack.

Boarding day and the safety drill

If you are flying, arrive in the port city the day before. A delayed flight on sailing day can mean missing the ship.

TimeWhat to do
MorningTravel to the terminal within your arrival window
MiddayCheck-in, security, board, lunch
Early afternoonSafety drill, find your cabin, a first lap of the ship
Mid-afternoonBook restaurants and shows, check your onboard account
EveningSail-away on deck, unpack, dinner, an early night

The safety drill (muster) is mandatory for every guest. Learn your assembly station and where your life jacket is, and follow crew instructions in any emergency. Never sit on, climb or lean over balcony or deck railings. If someone goes overboard, shout, throw a life ring if one is near, keep pointing at the person and alert crew immediately.

Onboard accounts are usually cashless and linked to a card. Check your balance every couple of days so the final bill holds no surprises. Put your phone in airplane mode (flight mode) with roaming off as soon as you board, because the ship's own mobile network can be very expensive.

Staying well and spending sensibly onboard

Wash your hands often with soap and water, especially before eating and after using the bathroom. Hand sanitizer alone is less effective against some stomach viruses such as norovirus. If you get a stomach bug, report it to the medical center and follow their isolation advice. Use sunscreen, drink water and pace any alcohol on hot days.

Before buying a drinks package, do the math (maths). Count how many drinks you would honestly have on a sea day and a port day, and compare that with the daily package price including service charges. Many lines require everyone in a cabin to buy the alcohol package if one person does.

Tipping varies by line. Many add a daily service charge per guest, some include it in the fare, and bar drinks and spa treatments often carry an automatic charge already. Read receipts before adding another tip so you do not pay twice.

Port day rules every first timer should know

You have three ways to explore a port. Ship excursions cost more, but the ship will usually wait if a ship tour runs late. Independent tours with local operators are often cheaper and smaller, but you are responsible for getting back. Exploring on foot costs little and is often the most relaxing choice.

  • Be back well before the all aboard time, which is usually 30 to 60 minutes before departure. Aim to reach the pier an hour or more before it in an unfamiliar port.
  • Set your watch to ship's time, since it may differ from local time.
  • Carry your cruise card, a photo ID, the ship's port agent details and the name of the next port. If you miss the ship, you must reach the next port at your own cost.
  • Keep valuables to a minimum, choose reputable tour operators and drink bottled or safe water where advised.
  • Respect local dress codes, dispose of litter properly and never chase or feed wildlife.

Frequently asked questions

What should I know before my first cruise?

Know what your fare includes and budget for extras like gratuities, drinks, internet and excursions. Bring a valid passport, buy travel insurance when you book, pack essentials in a carry-on for boarding day, attend the mandatory safety drill, and always be back on board well before the all aboard time, which is usually 30 to 60 minutes before departure.

Which cabin is best for seasickness on a cruise?

A cabin on a lower deck toward the middle of the ship moves the least, so it is the best choice if you worry about motion. Cabins high up and at the front feel the most movement. A window or balcony can help because seeing the horizon steadies many people. Ask a doctor or pharmacist about remedies before you go.

Do I need a passport for a cruise?

A valid passport is strongly recommended for every cruise. Some round-trip cruises from US ports accept other proof of citizenship for US citizens, but without a passport it can be very hard to fly home from a foreign port in an emergency. Many countries also require at least 6 months of validity, so check official advice for each stop.

How much extra money should I budget for a cruise?

It depends on the line, fare and your habits, so add up the likely extras before booking: port fees and taxes if shown separately, daily gratuities, drinks, specialty dining, internet, excursions, photos, spa and transport to the port. Inclusive fares cover more of these. Check the current fare and what it includes, then track your onboard account during the trip.

What happens if you miss the ship in port?

The ship will not wait for guests on independent tours or exploring alone. You are responsible for reaching the next port at your own expense, so carry the ship's port agent details and the next port's name. Ship excursions usually have a guarantee that the ship waits or helps you rejoin. Check whether your insurance covers missed port departures.

What items are not allowed on a cruise ship?

Commonly banned or restricted items include clothes irons and steamers, candles and incense, surge protectors and some power strips, drones, and weapons or anything that looks like one. Rules on bringing alcohol, soft drinks and water vary widely. Check your cruise line's prohibited items list before packing, because confiscated items may be held until the end of the trip.

Your first step today is to decide what kind of trip you want: a lively resort ship, a calmer mid-size ship or a river cruise, and roughly 7 nights with a mix of sea and port days. Then make a short list of the extras that matter to you, check the current fare and what it includes for two or three sailings, and you will be booking with your eyes open.
