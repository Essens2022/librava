How to Start a Home Bakery Business: 9 Legal, Practical Steps

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If friends keep telling you to sell your cakes, you are probably wondering what it takes to do it properly. To start a home bakery business, you check your local home food rules, register or get a permit, take a food hygiene course, set up a safe kitchen with clear allergen records, then price every bake to cover ingredients, packaging, overheads and your time before you sell a single item.

None of this is difficult, but the order matters. This guide walks you through each step in a sensible sequence, with checklists and a worked pricing example you can copy today. It is general guidance, not legal advice, so always confirm the details with the office that enforces food rules where you live.

Decide what kind of home bakery you want

Baking for friends is fun because you choose the date. Customers choose for you, and they nearly always choose Saturdays, holiday weekends and the week before a big celebration. Before you spend money on boxes and permits, decide whether this is a side business or your main income, because that choice shapes how much you invest and how many hours you commit.

Answer these questions on paper:

  • How many hours a week can I give to baking, packing, selling and admin?
  • Which days and evenings stay protected for family?
  • Do I need this money for bills, or is it extra?
  • How much can I spend on setup without stress?
  • What would make this feel worth it after six months?

Then be honest about your kitchen. A home oven holds a limited number of trays and a home fridge (refrigerator) fills up fast. Those limits set a ceiling on what you can make in a day, so decide in advance how many orders you can handle each week and stick to it.

Talk to your household first. During production you may need the kitchen clear, pets out and children busy elsewhere. Their support matters as much as your baking skills.

Check your cottage food and home food rules

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that can sink a launch. Rules decide what you may make, where you may sell and what your label must say. They differ by country, state, province and city.

How the rules usually work

  • United States. Most states have cottage food laws allowing lower-risk baked goods from a home kitchen. States differ on permits, training, sales channels, yearly sales limits and label wording, often including a statement that the food was made in a home kitchen not inspected by the health department. Shipping across state lines is usually not allowed.
  • United Kingdom. You must register as a food business with your local council at least 28 days before trading. It is free, and it applies even if you sell only occasionally.
  • Canada sets rules by province and often by municipality. Australia requires you to notify or register with your council or state food authority. New Zealand home bakers generally register under a national programme through the council or national regulator.

Foods usually allowed and not allowed

Usually allowed from homeUsually not allowed from home
Breads and rollsCheesecake
Cookies (biscuits), brownies and barsCustard or cream fillings
Plain cakes and cupcakesCream cheese frosting (icing)

The pattern is simple: foods that stay safe at room temperature are lower risk. If something needs the fridge to stay safe, assume you cannot sell it from home until your rules say otherwise in writing.

Find and read your local rules in 7 steps

Information is scattered and sometimes out of date, so work through it methodically:

  1. Search your country or state name plus "cottage food", "home food business" or "register a food business", starting with official government pages.
  2. Identify who enforces the rules: your state or local health department, your council's environmental health team or your national food regulator.
  3. Save the official guidance, allowed food lists, application forms and label requirements.
  4. Read it twice, highlighting anything that applies to your products and selling channels.
  5. Call or email with your questions, and note the date and the name of the person who answered.
  6. Check zoning or planning permission, your lease or mortgage, building rules and your home insurance.
  7. Keep everything in one folder and set a yearly reminder to check for changes.

Watch for the words "must", "may" and "only". A rule saying you may sell at farmers markets might mean markets are the only place you may sell. Check every channel separately, because permission for market sales may not cover online orders or delivery.

Register, get trained and prepare for inspection

Once you know what applies, get legally set up. Depending on where you live, that means a council food business registration, a cottage food permit, a provincial or municipal permit, or no permit but rules you still follow. Forms usually ask what you will make, where you will sell, your water supply and whether you have pets. Never start selling while an application is still being processed.

Next, book food hygiene or food handler training. In the UK a level 2 food hygiene course is the usual standard, and some US states require a food handler card or a cottage food course. Many courses take a few hours online and cost little.

What inspectors actually look at

  • A handwashing setup with soap, warm water and paper towels
  • Clean, undamaged surfaces, floors and equipment
  • Ingredients sealed, labeled and stored off the floor
  • A fridge thermometer and a written temperature log
  • Business and family food kept apart
  • Cleaning chemicals away from food, no signs of pests
  • Allergen records, sample labels, your food safety plan and training certificate

Inspectors care more about your habits and records than a sparkling oven door. If they suggest an improvement, write it down, thank them and fix it promptly.

Write a simple food safety plan

A food safety plan based on HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) sounds technical but is really common sense written down. In the UK a written system like this is required; elsewhere it is good practice.

  1. List your steps, from buying ingredients to handing food to the customer.
  2. Spot the hazards at each step: bacteria, chemicals and allergens, or physical bits like hair and packaging.
  3. Decide how you prevent each hazard.
  4. Decide how you check that control is working.
  5. Plan what you do if a check fails.
  6. Keep records showing you followed the plan.

Aim for one to three pages that describe your real routine. Alongside it, keep a ring binder for fridge temperatures on production days, a cleaning schedule, purchase dates, allergen records, certificates and any problems or complaints with what you did about them.

Get allergens and labels exactly right

Allergens are where a guess can put someone in hospital. Know the legal list for every place you sell. The US lists nine major allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame. The UK and EU list 14, adding celery, cereals containing gluten, lupin, molluscs, mustard and sulphites. Canada, Australia and New Zealand have their own rules, so print your country's current list and keep it in the kitchen.

Read the full ingredient list on every pack you buy, every time you buy it, because suppliers change recipes. The sneaky ones for bakers include:

  • Chocolate chips and coatings (often milk and soy)
  • Sprinkles and sugar decorations
  • Ready-made fondant and modeling paste
  • Jams, curds, caramels and flavorings
  • Flour blends, margarine and spreads

Record every ingredient and sub-ingredient for each product. Use "may contain" statements honestly, only where there is a real risk of cross-contact, never as a blanket cover.

If you are not sure, say so. When a customer asks whether something contains nuts and you cannot check right now, tell them you cannot confirm it. Losing a sale is far better than a guess.

How to price baked goods so every sale pays

Almost every home baker undercharges at first, usually by copying supermarket prices and forgetting their own time. A full price covers seven things:

  1. Ingredients per unit
  2. Packaging per unit: boxes, boards, liners, labels, ribbon
  3. Your time for shopping, baking, decorating, packing, cleaning and admin
  4. Overheads: energy, equipment wear, insurance, permits, training
  5. Payment and platform fees
  6. Delivery, or charge it separately
  7. A check against local prices

Any price below 2.10 means paying to work. For custom cakes, set a base price per serving, add a charge for design complexity and list delivery separately. Take a non-refundable deposit to book the date, charge a rush fee for short-notice orders and apply your prices to friends too.

Where to sell baked goods from home

Start with the channels your rules allow, then pick one or two you can run well.

  • Farmers markets and pop-ups. Good for testing demand. Bring handwashing supplies, keep samples covered and served with tongs, display allergen information and plan for weather.
  • Custom orders for birthdays and celebrations, handled with a clear order form, written terms, design approval and deposit deadlines.
  • Online orders through a simple website or social media page with local collection or delivery, if your rules permit it.
  • Cafes and shops, which often need a licensed kitchen, so check before you offer wholesale.

For custom cakes, avoid copying famous characters, logos or brand names, which can break copyright and trademark law. Offer a design inspired by the theme instead, such as colors and shapes. Take photos of your bakes in good daylight, describe size, servings and allergens clearly, and ask happy customers for a short review.

Set up your money, tax and insurance

Open a separate bank account for the bakery from day one, and pay all income in and all costs out of it. Photograph receipts as soon as you get them. Record what you bought, what you sold and through which channel.

Find out early which taxes apply: income tax, self-employment or national insurance contributions, and sales tax, VAT or GST once you pass any threshold. Ask an accountant what to set aside and move that share into a savings account every time you are paid.

Review insurance before your first sale and once a year:

  • Product liability, if your food causes illness or injury
  • Public liability, for market stalls and events
  • Home insurance updated for business use
  • Car insurance covering business deliveries

If you outgrow your home rules, perhaps by wanting to sell cheesecakes, supply cafes or exceed a yearly sales limit, a shared commercial kitchen rented by the hour is the usual next step. Frequently asked questions

Can I sell baked goods from home without a license?

It depends on where you live. Some US states let you sell certain low-risk baked goods with no permit, but you must still follow label and sales rules. In the UK you must register with your council at least 28 days before trading, even for occasional sales. Check the official home food or cottage food page for your area and confirm with the office before your first sale.

What baked goods can I sell from home?

Usually foods that stay safe at room temperature, such as breads, cookies, brownies, plain cakes, cupcakes and buttercream without perishable dairy. Items that need refrigeration, like cheesecake, custard or cream fillings, cream cheese frosting and fresh fruit toppings, are usually not allowed from a home kitchen. Your local rules have the final word, so check their allowed food list.

How much does it cost to start a home bakery?

Costs vary widely, but many people start with equipment they already own and spend mainly on registration or permit fees where they apply, a food hygiene course, labels, food-safe packaging, a fridge thermometer and insurance. Add market stall fees if you sell at markets. Buy only what your first menu needs and upgrade once orders prove demand.

Do I need insurance for a home bakery?

It is strongly recommended and some markets require it. Product liability insurance covers claims if your food causes illness or injury, and public liability covers accidents at stalls or events. Tell your home insurer you run a business from home, and check that your car insurance covers deliveries. Review your cover every year and whenever your business changes.

Can I ship baked goods from my home bakery?

Often not, or only within limits. Many US cottage food laws ban shipping across state lines, and some only allow direct sales handed over in person. Other places allow posting within the country if food is packed safely and labeled correctly. Check what your registration or permit covers before offering shipping, because permission for local sales does not automatically include mail order.

How do I price cakes from home?

Cost a simple cake using ingredients, packaging, your time, overheads and fees, then divide by the number of servings to get a base price per serving. Add a charge for design complexity such as extra tiers or detailed piping, and add delivery separately. Compare the result with local home bakers of similar quality, then take a deposit to secure the date.

Your first step today is simple: find the official home food business or cottage food page for where you live, save a copy in a folder named for your bakery and write down any questions to ask the office. Everything else, from training to pricing, builds on knowing exactly what your rules allow.
