How to Potty Train a Puppy: A Calm Schedule That Prevents Accidents

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you are mopping up puddles several times a day and wondering if your puppy will ever get it, take heart: potty training follows a predictable pattern. To potty train a puppy, take them to the same outdoor spot after every nap, meal and play session and every 1 to 2 hours in between, reward them with a treat the second they finish, supervise closely indoors, and never punish accidents.

Do that consistently and most puppies are reliable somewhere between 4 and 6 months old. This guide gives you the schedule, the signals to watch for, a plan for nights and the fixes for the problems almost every owner meets.

How puppy potty training works

Your puppy is not being naughty when they pee on the rug. Young puppies have tiny bladders and very little control, and they have no idea that your kitchen floor is different from the lawn. Both of those change with age and practice.

The whole method rests on two ideas. First, prevent accidents by getting your puppy outside before they need to go. Second, reward every success on the right spot. Each outdoor pee builds the habit you want, and each unnoticed indoor accident builds the wrong one a little. Your job is to tip the score heavily toward success.

A rough guide to how long a puppy can hold it in the daytime is their age in months plus one hour. A 2-month-old might manage about 3 hours, a 3-month-old about 4. Treat that as the absolute limit on a calm day, never a target.

When to take your puppy out

Puppies need to go at fairly predictable moments. Take yours out:

  • As soon as they wake up, from a nap or in the morning
  • About 10 to 20 minutes after eating or a big drink
  • During and after play, because excitement speeds everything up
  • After chewing, training sessions or visitors
  • Last thing before bed
  • Every 1 to 2 hours in between, even if nothing else has happened

At 8 to 12 weeks, many puppies need a trip out roughly every hour while awake and active. It feels relentless for a few weeks. A timer on your phone keeps you consistent when life gets busy.

Here is how part of a morning might look for a 10-week-old puppy. Adjust the times to your own household, but keep the pattern.

Puppies need a lot of sleep, around 18 to 20 hours a day, so much of the day is a loop of nap, potty, play, potty, nap. Once you see it that way, the schedule starts to feel manageable.

Use one potty spot and reward on the spot

Choose one place in your yard (garden), or one patch of grass near your door if you live in an apartment (flat). The smell tells your puppy this is the place, so returning to it every time speeds things up.

  1. Carry a very young puppy to the spot, or walk them on a leash (lead) so they do not get distracted on the way.
  2. Stand still and be a little boring. Let them sniff a small area.
  3. Say a calm cue such as "Go potty" while they sniff. After a couple of weeks the words start to prompt the behavior.
  4. Reward within a second or two. The moment they finish, say "Yes!" and give a tasty treat right there, then some quiet praise.
  5. Give a few minutes of sniffing or play before you head in, so going potty does not always mean the fun ends.

Waiting until you are back inside to give the treat is too late. Your puppy will think it was for walking through the door.

If nothing happens after about 5 minutes, go back in, keep your puppy close or in their crate or pen for 10 to 15 minutes, and try again. This short loop prevents the classic scene where a puppy comes in from a long, fruitless trip and pees straight on the floor.

Signs your puppy needs to pee

Puppies do tell you, just quietly at first. Watch for:

  • Sudden sniffing with the nose low to the floor
  • Circling, especially in a corner or behind furniture
  • Wandering away from play with a purposeful look
  • Whining, pacing or heading toward the door
  • Stopping mid-chew and drifting off

When you spot any of these, calmly scoop up your puppy or clip on the leash and go. If you always use the same door, many puppies start heading there by themselves.

Supervision makes the biggest difference

Most accidents happen when nobody is watching. When your puppy is out of the crate or pen, keep them in the same room as you, using closed doors, baby gates or a light leash clipped to your belt. When you cannot watch, use a properly sized crate or puppy pen for short periods. Puppies usually avoid soiling where they sleep. After a week or two with no accidents in one room, add one more room at a time.

What to do when your puppy has an accident

Accidents will happen, and your reaction matters more than the puddle. If you catch your puppy mid-pee, make a light, cheerful sound such as "Oops, outside!" and carry them out. If they finish there, mark and reward as usual. If you find a puddle later, simply clean it up. Your puppy cannot link your reaction with something they did even a minute ago.

Never scold, shout or rub a puppy's nose in it. That old advice does not teach where to go. It teaches your puppy to fear peeing in front of you, so they sneak behind the sofa and may even hold it outside while you wait, then go the moment they are back indoors.

Clean with an enzyme cleaner made for pet urine from a pet store (pet shop). Ordinary sprays may hide the smell from you but not from your puppy's nose, and ammonia-based cleaners can smell like urine and draw them back. Blot first, soak the spot as the label directs, and treat carpet padding too if needed.

Potty training at night

Young puppies cannot hold it all night. Most 8 to 10-week-old puppies need one, sometimes two, trips out overnight. Plan a late break right before you go to bed and an early one as soon as you wake.

  • Keep night trips boring: carry your puppy out, wait quietly, give a calm "Yes!" and a treat, then go straight back to bed.
  • No play, no chatting and minimal lights.
  • Many owners set one planned alarm rather than waiting for crying, then push it 15 to 30 minutes later every few nights.

Most puppies sleep through somewhere around 4 to 6 months, though every puppy is different. A crate near your bed in the early weeks helps you hear when they genuinely need to go.

Pee pads, apartments and common problems

Pee pads (puppy pads) can help in a high-rise apartment, for owners with mobility challenges, or when your vet has advised keeping your puppy indoors for now. They can also slow training, because your puppy learns that going indoors on something soft is fine. If you have outdoor access, skip them. If you need them, keep one pad in a fixed spot away from bed and bowls, and later move it gradually toward the door and then outside.

In apartments, keep shoes, keys, bags and treats by the door, carry young puppies through hallways and elevators (lifts), and pick a potty spot close to the entrance. Before vaccinations are complete, ask your vet which outdoor areas are safe, and avoid busy spots used by many unknown dogs.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to potty train a puppy?

Most puppies are reliably potty trained somewhere between 4 and 6 months old, and some, especially small breeds, take a little longer. You will usually notice fewer accidents and longer gaps first, then a puppy who heads for the door on their own. Teething, a busy week or an upset tummy can bring a few accidents back, which is normal.

How often should I take my puppy out to pee?

Take a young puppy out after waking, eating, drinking and playing, and every 1 to 2 hours in between. At 8 to 12 weeks, many need to go about every hour while awake. A rough daytime maximum is their age in months plus one hour, so a 3-month-old might hold it for about 4 hours when calm and resting.

Why does my puppy pee right after coming inside?

Usually the trip outside was too short or too exciting, so your puppy sniffed and played instead of going. Next time, take them to the same spot on a leash, stand still for about 5 minutes, and reward the moment they finish. If nothing happens, keep them close or in their crate for 10 to 15 minutes, then try again.

Should I use pee pads for my puppy?

Only if you need them, for example in a high-rise apartment or when your vet has advised keeping your puppy indoors. Pads can make training take longer because they teach that going indoors is acceptable, and some puppies then use rugs too. If you use them, keep one pad in a fixed spot and move it gradually toward the door and outside.

Can I potty train a puppy before vaccinations are finished?

Yes, you can start on day one. Use your own yard (garden) or a quiet spot your vet agrees is low risk, and avoid busy areas used by many unknown dogs until your vet says your puppy is protected. Carrying your puppy to and from the spot also reduces contact with places other dogs have used.

Should I punish my puppy for peeing in the house?

No. Punishment, including scolding or rubbing their nose in it, teaches your puppy to hide when they pee rather than where to go, which makes training slower. If you catch them in the act, calmly interrupt and carry them outside, then reward if they finish there. If you find a puddle later, just clean it with an enzyme cleaner.

Today, set a phone timer for every hour your puppy is awake, take them to the same spot each time, and give a treat the second they finish outside. Keep a quick log of times for a week and you will soon be beating the puddles to the punch.
