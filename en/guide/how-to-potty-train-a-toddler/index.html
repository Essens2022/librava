How to Potty Train a Toddler: A Calm, Child-Led Plan Without Tears

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you are feeling the pressure from family, daycare (nursery) or a feed full of toddlers proudly sitting on potties, take a breath. To potty train a toddler, wait for the signs of readiness, prepare for a week or two with books and practice sits, then offer the potty at natural moments such as after waking and after meals, respond to accidents calmly and never punish or force your child to sit.

Readiness often shows somewhere between about 18 months and 3 years, and there is no single right age or guaranteed number of days. This guide walks you through the signs, the main approaches, a sample first day, the words to use and the problems that trip most families up.

Signs your toddler is ready for potty training

Readiness rarely arrives all at once. It tends to show up as a handful of small changes over weeks or months. Your child does not need every sign, just a general picture of a body that can hold on and a child who notices what is happening.

  • Their diaper (nappy) stays dry for an hour or two, or they sometimes wake dry from a nap.
  • Poos come at fairly regular times and are soft and easy to pass.
  • They can walk to the potty and sit down and get up without help.
  • They can push loose trousers (pants) down, or are trying.
  • They tell you, with words, signs or gestures, that they are weeing or pooing, or just have.
  • They hide to poo, pull at a wet diaper or ask to be changed.
  • They are curious about the toilet and want to copy others.

A child who tells you afterward is often close to telling you before. That shift from "I did a wee" to "wee coming" is one of the most useful signs of all. Signs, gestures and pictures count fully for children who are not talking much yet.

When it is better to wait

Even with the signs in place, timing matters. Potty training goes best during a fairly steady patch. Consider waiting a few weeks if:

  • A new baby is due or has just arrived
  • You are moving house
  • Your child is starting or changing daycare, or a new childminder
  • Your child is unwell or recovering from an illness
  • A big trip or vacation (holiday) is coming up soon
  • Your child is in a phase of refusing absolutely everything

Waiting is not giving up. A few weeks is nothing in the long run. If your child often has hard or painful poos, sort that out first with your health professional, because constipation makes training much harder.

If family members push back, you do not need to win the argument. A kind line works: "We are watching for the signs and will start when she is ready. Could you keep a potty at your house for when we do?" Giving grandparents a role often turns a critic into a helper.

Get ready before you start

A week or two of gentle preparation makes the first days much smoother.

  • Choose a stable potty, or a toilet seat reducer with a sturdy step stool so your child's feet are supported.
  • Let your child pick some underwear they are proud of.
  • Read simple potty books and let them watch you or a sibling use the toilet.
  • Agree on matter-of-fact words for pee and poo, and use correct body part names.
  • Practice the routine: sit, wipe front to back, flush, wash hands with soap.
  • Let a teddy or doll practice on the potty too.

Short practice sits at natural moments, such as before a bath, help the potty feel like part of everyday life rather than a big event.

Choose a potty training approach

There are three broad ways to do this, and children get there with all of them.

ApproachBest forProsCons
GradualCautious or easily upset childrenLow pressure, fits busy livesCan take longer, mixed signals
Intensive few daysClearly ready childrenClear start, quick early progressHard work, stressful if not ready
BlendedMost familiesFlexible, builds confidence firstNeeds some planning

A blended plan often works well: a few weeks of relaxed practice, then a focused long weekend at home once your child starts telling you before they go. Think about temperament too. A cautious watcher usually does better with a slow build, while a busy explorer may respond well to a short, focused stretch with clear routines.

Intensive does not mean strict. The intensity is about your attention, not about pushing your child, and a few focused days usually start the learning rather than finish it.

Your first potty training day

Clear your schedule, stay home and plan easy activities you can pause, like puzzles, drawing and building blocks. Keep the potty in the room where you spend most of the day. Many families go bare-bottomed or in just underwear at first, then move to loose, elastic-waisted bottoms. Skip overalls (dungarees) and bodysuits.

On wakingStraight to the potty, before milk or breakfast
20 to 30 minutes after mealsOffer a relaxed sit, often a good poo time
Every 30 to 45 minutesOffer the potty between activities
After napsStraight to the potty
Before bath and bedA last sit, then training pants or a diaper for sleep

Keep sits short, a few minutes at most. If nothing happens, say "That's okay, we'll try later" and let them go. Watch your child more than anything else: crossed legs, wriggling, going quiet or standing still all mean a wee may be coming. If they start on the way, praise whatever lands in the potty.

Make statements, not questions. "Do you need a wee?" invites a reflex "No!" Try "It's potty time, then we'll finish the puzzle," or offer a choice that still leads there: "Do you want to walk or hop to the potty?" A kitchen timer helps, because then it is the timer's idea, not yours.

What to say when accidents happen

Accidents are how your child learns what the feeling means. A toddler's warning time between "I feel something" and "it is happening" can be just a few seconds at first, and it stretches with practice. Many children also have a dip at the end of the first week, once the novelty wears off.

Keep your words short and neutral, the same tone you would use for spilled water: "Oops, the wee came out. Wees go in the potty. Let's get you dry." Let your child help in a small way, like fetching dry pants, framed as teamwork rather than a consequence.

  • Avoid "Why didn't you tell me?" Most toddlers genuinely do not know.
  • Avoid comparisons such as "Your cousin never does that."
  • Praise effort as well as success: "You felt the wee coming and got to the potty. Well done!"
  • If you use stickers or a chart, keep it low-key and phase it out over a few weeks.

If you snap, which every parent does at some point, repair it: "I sounded cross before. Accidents are okay. I'm sorry I was grumpy."

Poo worries, refusals and fear of flushing

Poo is often harder than wee. Some children hold it in, standing stiffly, crossing their legs or hiding in a corner. Held-in poo gets harder and more painful, which makes them hold on even more. Help comfortable poos with regular water, plenty of fruit, vegetables, beans and wholegrains, active play and a relaxed sit about 15 to 30 minutes after meals.

Position matters. Feet should be flat on the floor or a firm step, knees slightly higher than hips, leaning forward a little. Blowing games, like pretend bubbles or birthday candles, help the tummy relax without straining.

  • Refuses to sit: check the potty is stable and the room is warm, keep sits short, and try sitting clothed or with a book.
  • Scared of flushing: let them leave the room first, or wave goodbye from the door until they feel brave enough to press the button.
  • Hides to poo in a diaper: first ask them to do it in the bathroom, then sitting on the potty with the diaper on, then gradually without it.

If battles take over, pause for a few weeks with a cheerful "We'll have a little break and try again soon," then look at the readiness signs again.

This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your pediatrician, family doctor (GP) or health visitor if your child has pain or stinging when peeing, blood in their pee or poo, fever with accidents, very frequent peeing or a sudden increase in thirst, hard or painful poos or fewer than about three a week, or soiling. Ask too if they were dry for 6 months or more and start wetting again, are not dry in the day by about 4, or you have any worries about development. Never give laxatives or remedies without a doctor's advice.

Nighttime dryness and naps

Staying dry at night usually comes later than daytime dryness, sometimes not until 5 to 7, and bedwetting is common and usually not a concern under 5. Keep using training pants or diapers for sleep until your child wakes dry most mornings for a week or two. Then try nights with a waterproof mattress protector, a potty nearby and a spare set of sheets ready.

Offer drinks through the day rather than lots right before bed, and a last wee at bedtime. Treat wet nights the same calm way as daytime accidents. Naps often become dry before nights do.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best age to start potty training?

There is no single best age. Many children show readiness somewhere between about 18 months and 3 years, and many are dry in the daytime by about 3 to 4. Look for signs such as staying dry for an hour or two, noticing when they wee or poo and showing interest in the toilet, and choose a calm period in family life to begin.

How long does potty training take?

It varies widely, and no method can promise a set number of days. A focused few days at home often gets the learning started, but most children still have accidents and need reminders for weeks afterward. Children who start when clearly ready usually progress faster. Night dryness is separate and can take months or years longer.

Does 3-day potty training work?

An intensive few-days approach can work well for children who are clearly ready and families who can stay home and pay close attention. It usually starts the learning rather than finishing it, so expect accidents for a while afterward. If your child is only partly ready or becomes upset, a gradual or blended approach is often kinder and calmer.

Why is my toddler refusing to poop on the potty?

Common reasons include a past painful poo, fear of the feeling of letting go, a wish for privacy or simply being used to pooing standing up. Check for constipation, make sure their feet are supported, and use small steps, such as pooing in the bathroom in a diaper first. If poos are hard or painful, talk to your doctor or health visitor.

Should I use pull-ups when potty training?

Pull-ups or training pants are a sensible choice for naps and nights, and some families use them for long outings. In the daytime at home, underwear helps many children notice when they are wet. Using pull-ups does not ruin potty training. Choose what suits your child and keep your approach consistent between home, daycare and grandparents.

When should I stop and try again later?

Consider a break if accidents are not reducing after a week or two, your child is distressed or clingy around the potty, daily battles leave you both upset, or they start holding in poo. Return to diapers without any sense of disappointment, keep potty books and talk light, and try again in a few weeks to a couple of months.

Your first step today is simple: go through the readiness signs above, note which ones you see, and put a potty and a couple of picture books where your child plays. If most signs are there, pick a calm few days in the next couple of weeks and start; if not, keep the practice light and look again in a month.
