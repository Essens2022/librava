How to Crochet for Beginners: Your First Stitches Step by Step

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have bought a hook, watched someone crochet and thought your hands will never move like that, take heart: they will, usually within an evening or two. To crochet as a beginner, start with a 5 mm (H-8) hook and smooth weight 4 (worsted or aran) yarn, learn the slip knot and chain, then practice rows of single crochet until your stitches look even.

This guide takes you from that first loop to a finished dishcloth. You will learn what to buy without overspending, two comfortable ways to hold the hook, each basic stitch in plain steps, how to read a pattern, the US and UK terms trap and how to fix the mistakes every beginner makes.

What you need to start crocheting

You need far less than the craft aisle suggests. A simple, sensible starter kit looks like this:

  • One 5 mm (US H-8) hook. Aluminum is cheap, light and smooth, which makes it the friendliest first hook.
  • One or two balls of weight 4 yarn (worsted in the US, aran in the UK) in a light, plain color, so you can see each stitch. Smooth acrylic or an acrylic blend is ideal for practice.
  • A blunt yarn needle for weaving in ends.
  • Small scissors.
  • A few stitch markers. Safety pins or a scrap of contrasting yarn work too.

Skip the fluffy, sparkly or very dark yarns for now, because they hide your stitches. Hooks are labeled in two systems: a US letter and number, and the metric width of the shaft. When both are shown, trust the millimeters, since 5 mm is always 5 mm.

Cotton is best for dishcloths and pot holders because it absorbs water and handles heat. Never use acrylic for anything that touches hot pans, since it can melt.

How to hold a crochet hook and yarn

One hand holds the hook and does the moving. The other hand feeds the yarn and steadies the work. Right-handed crocheters usually hold the hook in the right hand; left-handed crocheters can simply mirror every instruction.

  • Pencil grip. Hold the hook like a pen, thumb on the flat thumb rest, handle pointing back over your hand. It gives fine finger control.
  • Knife grip. Hold it like a table knife, handle tucked in your palm and index finger along the top. It uses more wrist and suits long sessions and big hooks.

Neither grip is better, so choose the one that lets your hand relax. Keep the hooked head facing you or slightly down so it can pull yarn back toward you.

For the yarn hand, lay the working yarn (the strand leading to the ball) across your palm, wrap it loosely around your little finger, and lift it over your index finger. That raised index finger controls tension: lift it to tighten, lower it to loosen. Pinch the work just below the hook with your thumb and middle finger. It will feel awkward for the first few hours, and that is normal for everyone.

How to make a slip knot and chain

Almost every project starts with these two skills.

Slip knot

  1. Pull out about 6 inches (15 cm) of yarn. This loose end is the tail.
  2. Lay the working yarn over the tail to make a small circle.
  3. Push the hook through the circle, catch the working yarn behind it and pull a loop through.
  4. Pull both ends gently until the loop sits snugly on the hook but can still slide.

If the knot falls apart when you tug the tail, you caught the tail instead of the working yarn. The slip knot never counts as a stitch.

Chain (ch)

  1. Yarn over (yo): wrap the yarn over the hook from back to front.
  2. Turn the hook slightly downward and draw the yarn through the loop on the hook. One chain made.
  3. Repeat, moving your pinching fingers up every two or three chains.

To count, look for the little V shapes on the front. Count each V, but never the slip knot or the loop on your hook. Keep the loop on the wide part of the shaft, not the narrow throat, or your chains will come out tight.

Nearly every beginner makes the foundation chain too tight. If yours is a battle to work into, make the chain with a hook one or two sizes larger, then switch back to your 5 mm hook for the first row.

How to single crochet step by step

Single crochet (sc) is the stitch you will use most at first. It makes firm, dense fabric for dishcloths, coasters and toys. For single crochet, chain one more than the number of stitches you want, so chain 21 for a row of 20.

  1. Insert the hook into the 2nd chain from the hook. The chain next to the hook stays empty.
  2. Yarn over and pull up a loop. You have 2 loops on the hook.
  3. Yarn over again and draw through both loops. One single crochet made.
  4. Repeat into every chain to the end.

At the end of the row, chain 1 and turn the work around like a page in a book. That chain 1 is a turning chain and usually does not count as a stitch. Work your first single crochet into the very first stitch, right at the base of the chain, inserting the hook under both top loops of each stitch.

Count your stitches at the end of every row. A practice swatch of 15 stitches for 12 rows is a perfect first goal, and it teaches your hands the rhythm faster than anything else.

Half double, double and treble crochet

Taller stitches are just extra yarn overs. Once single crochet feels easy, these come quickly.

  • Half double crochet (hdc). Yarn over, insert the hook, yarn over and pull up a loop (3 loops), then yarn over and draw through all 3.
  • Double crochet (dc). Yarn over, insert, yarn over and pull up a loop (3 loops). Yarn over and draw through 2, then yarn over and draw through the last 2.
  • Treble crochet (tr). Yarn over twice, insert and pull up a loop (4 loops), then work off the loops two at a time three times.

Notice the rhythm: after pulling up the loop, you work off loops two at a time. Each taller stitch needs a taller turning chain at the start of the row.

These are conventions, and the pattern you are following always wins.

How to read a crochet pattern

Patterns look like code until you know a handful of abbreviations. Here is a typical beginner line:

Row 2: Ch 1, turn, sc in each st across. (28 sts)

That means chain 1, turn the work, work one single crochet into every stitch, and check you have 28 stitches. The number in brackets at the end is the stitch count, and it is your best friend.

  • ch = chain, sl st = slip stitch, st(s) = stitch(es), sk = skip
  • sc2tog = single crochet two together, a decrease that joins two stitches into one
  • An asterisk marks a repeat: "*2 sc, 1 ch; rep from * across" means keep doing that sequence to the end.
  • Brackets group stitches worked into one place, such as "(3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in corner space".

US and UK crochet terms

The same names mean different stitches. In UK terms each basic stitch is named one step taller: US single crochet is UK double crochet, US half double is UK half treble, US double is UK treble, and US treble is UK double treble. Chain and slip stitch are the same. UK patterns also say "miss" for skip and "tension" for gauge.

Before you start any pattern, check which terms it uses. A UK pattern full of "dc" worked as US double crochet comes out twice as tall and far too big. Pencil the US term above each UK stitch before you begin.

Fix common beginner crochet mistakes

  • Work getting wider. You are adding stitches, usually by working into the turning chain at the end of a row. With a chain 1 that does not count, work only into real stitches.
  • Work getting narrower. You are missing the first or last stitch. With double crochet and a chain 3 that counts as a stitch, skip the first stitch and work the last one into the top of the turning chain from the row below.
  • Stitches too tight to work into. Relax your yarn hand and keep loops on the wide part of the shaft.
  • Lost count on a long chain. Put a marker every 10 or 20 chains.

A stitch marker in the first and last stitch of each row makes edges easy to spot. When something goes wrong, do not be afraid to pull it out. Crocheters call this frogging, because you "rip it, rip it." Take the hook out, pull the working yarn gently until you reach the last good stitch, then put the hook back into the live loop. Frogged yarn crochets up perfectly well.

Finish a piece by cutting the yarn with a 6 inch (15 cm) tail, pulling it through the last loop, and weaving the tail back and forth through the stitches with a yarn needle.

Easy crochet projects for beginners

Choose first projects that are flat squares or rectangles, so you only need one or two stitches.

  1. Striped dishcloth. In weight 4 cotton with a 5 mm hook, chain 29 and work rows of single crochet (28 stitches), changing color every 4 rows, for about 8 inches (20 cm) square. It teaches straight edges and color changes in 2 to 3 hours.
  2. Ridged scarf in half double crochet.
  3. Chunky ear warmer in bulky yarn.
  4. Round coasters, your first taste of working in circles.
  5. A classic granny square, the start of countless blankets.

Crochet should never hurt. Put the hook down every 20 to 30 minutes, stretch your hands and roll your wrists and shoulders. If aches in your hands or wrists keep coming back, talk to a doctor or qualified health professional before carrying on.

Frequently asked questions

Is crochet easy to learn for beginners?

Yes. Most people make an even chain in their first evening and a small square of single crochet within a few days. Crochet only ever has one live loop on the hook, so mistakes are quick to undo. Practicing 15 to 30 minutes a day is enough to make your first dishcloth in about a week.

What size crochet hook should a beginner use?

A 5 mm hook, labeled H-8 in the US, is the best size to learn with. It pairs well with weight 4 worsted or aran yarn, which is thick enough to see every stitch clearly. An aluminum hook is cheap and smooth, so start with that and try a padded handle later if you need more comfort.

What is the best yarn for beginner crochet?

Choose a smooth, light-colored weight 4 yarn, called worsted in the US and aran in the UK. Acrylic or an acrylic blend is inexpensive and slides easily on the hook, so you will not mind pulling it out. Avoid fluffy, textured, sparkly or very dark yarns until you can recognize your stitches easily.

What is the difference between US and UK crochet terms?

UK terms name each basic stitch one step taller. US single crochet is UK double crochet, US half double is UK half treble, US double crochet is UK treble, and US treble is UK double treble. Chain and slip stitch are the same. Always check which terms a pattern uses before you start.

Why is my crochet getting wider or narrower?

The problem is almost always at the edges. Getting wider means you are adding stitches, often by working into the turning chain. Getting narrower means you are missing the first or last stitch of a row. Count stitches at the end of every row and put a marker in the first and last stitch to keep edges straight.

What should I crochet first?

A dishcloth in single crochet is the ideal first project. It is small, flat and square, uses cotton yarn that is easy to see, and teaches you to keep edges straight and count stitches. After that, try a scarf in half double crochet, then coasters or a granny square to learn working in rounds.

Your first step today is small: make a slip knot, chain 20, count the Vs, pull it out and do it again three times until every loop looks about the same size. Once your chains are even, work a little swatch of single crochet, and in a week you will be holding a finished dishcloth you made with your own hands.
