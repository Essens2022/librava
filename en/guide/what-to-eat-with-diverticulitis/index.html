What to Eat With Diverticulitis: A Stage by Stage Food Plan

By the Librava editors · Updated 2026-10-26

If you have just come home with diverticulitis and a leaflet that says clear liquids, then low fiber, you probably have no idea what to actually put on the plate. The short answer: during a flare you usually start with clear liquids for a few days, move to soft low-fiber foods as the pain eases, then build back up to a high-fiber diet over a few weeks, always following your doctor's plan.

Once you are well, the everyday goal flips. A high-fiber diet with plenty of fluids is what doctors generally recommend for living with diverticula. Below you will find each stage explained, sample days, easy fiber swaps, the current advice on nuts and popcorn and the warning signs that need urgent help.

Diverticulosis, diverticular disease and diverticulitis

These three words sound alike, but they mean different things, and that changes what you eat.

  • Diverticulosis means having diverticula, small pouches that bulge out through weak spots in the wall of the large bowel (colon). It is very common with age and usually causes no symptoms at all.
  • Diverticular disease means the pouches cause symptoms, such as lower tummy pain (often on the left), bloating or changes in bowel habit.
  • Diverticulitis means one or more pouches have become inflamed or infected. It usually brings constant, often severe pain, commonly low on the left, with fever, nausea and constipation or diarrhea.

A doctor makes the diagnosis, sometimes with a scan. Because symptoms can overlap with other bowel conditions, you may be offered further tests such as a colonoscopy after a flare. Take part in bowel screening when it is offered.

Your doctor's plan comes first

Mild diverticulitis is often treated at home. Your doctor may or may not prescribe antibiotics, since they are not always needed now. If you are given them, finish the course exactly as directed. Severe diverticulitis may need hospital care.

The stages below describe a common pattern, but your own plan may differ in timing or foods. Where your doctor's advice and this guide disagree, follow your doctor.

Talk to your doctor or pharmacist. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Check before taking any painkiller during a flare, because anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen and some stronger painkillers may raise the risk of complications or worsen constipation. Never stop a prescribed medicine, such as low-dose aspirin for your heart, without medical advice. If you have kidney disease, diabetes, IBS or IBD, or are on a fluid limit, ask before changing your diet.

Diverticulitis flare diet at a glance

Most flare plans follow the same broad shape. The bowel rests first, then takes on gentle food, then slowly returns to high fiber.

1. Clear liquidsA few days, as your doctor advisesClear broth, water, pulp-free juice, weak tea or coffee without milk, ice chips
2. Low fiberSeveral days to a couple of weeksWhite rice, bread and pasta, eggs, tender chicken and fish, peeled soft vegetables, peeled potatoes, smooth soups
3. Stepping back upA few weeksOne new fiber food at a time with fluids, building to beans, whole grains, nuts and seeds

The timings are a rough guide only. Move on when the pain is easing and your doctor agrees, not because a calendar says so.

Stage 1, clear liquids for a few days

Clear liquids rest the bowel while keeping you hydrated. A handy test: if you could read print through a glass of it, it counts as clear.

  • Clear broth or stock, strained of all bits
  • Water, still or sparkling
  • Clear juices without pulp, such as apple or white grape
  • Weak tea or coffee without milk or cream
  • Ice chips

Sip slowly and often, about a mug every hour or two, rather than drinking a lot at once. A sample day might be weak tea and a mug of clear chicken broth at breakfast, water and a small glass of apple juice mid-morning, a large mug of clear vegetable broth at lunch and a bowl of clear beef broth at dinner.

Expect to feel hungry and a little flat. That is normal and it does not last. A homemade broth tastes far better than a cube in hot water, so it is worth freezing a few portions while you are well, ready for the next time you need them.

Stage 2, low fiber foods as the pain eases

When the pain starts to settle, often after a few days, your doctor may suggest gentle low-fiber food. Small meals little and often tend to suit people better than three big plates.

The foods on the right are not banned. They are resting, and they are the very foods that will help you most later. The most common mistake is feeling better on day three and jumping straight to a big plate of your usual dinner. Moving up too fast can bring the pain back.

Stage 3, stepping back up to high fiber

Once you are comfortable on low-fiber food and your doctor agrees, bring fiber back over a few weeks. Add one new food every couple of days and drink plenty as you go. A gentle order that suits many people:

  1. Soft cooked vegetables such as carrots, peas and green beans
  2. Oats, polenta and potatoes with their skins
  3. Smooth lentil soups and small amounts of well-cooked lentils
  4. Brown rice, whole wheat (wholemeal) bread and whole wheat pasta
  5. Beans and chickpeas, starting with small portions
  6. Raw salads, fruit with skins, nuts, seeds and popcorn

Some extra wind is normal as fiber returns. If you get pain, stay on your current step for a few more days or drop back one, then try again. Severe or worsening pain is a red flag, so get help rather than waiting.

Everyday diverticulosis diet between flares

When you are well, a high-fiber diet is the everyday pattern doctors generally recommend. Adults are usually advised to aim for about 25 to 30 grams of fiber a day or a little more, and most people eat far less. You do not need to count. If most meals include whole grains, beans or lentils, vegetables and some fruit, you are on track.

A high-fiber day might look like savory oatmeal (porridge) with mushrooms and spinach, a bean soup with whole grain bread at lunch, a handful of nuts in the afternoon and lentil shepherd's pie with plenty of vegetables for dinner. Other helpful habits include regular activity, not smoking and eating less red and processed meat. Take fiber supplements only if your doctor or pharmacist suggests them.

Add fiber slowly and drink more

Adding lots of fiber at once gives your gut bacteria a sudden feast, which means wind, bloating and cramps. Going slowly over three or four weeks avoids most of that.

  • Week 1: switch one meal a day to a higher-fiber version, such as oatmeal instead of white toast.
  • Week 2: add beans or lentils most days and swap to whole wheat bread.
  • Week 3: move to brown rice and whole wheat pasta and add an extra portion of vegetables.
  • Week 4: add nuts, seeds and popcorn as snacks and keep skins on potatoes and fruit.

Fiber works by holding water, so without enough fluid it can make constipation worse. Have a glass of water with each meal and one between meals, unless your doctor has told you to limit fluids. Pale yellow urine is a fair sign you are drinking enough.

Red flags that need urgent help

See your doctor for new or changing tummy pain, changes in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks, bloating that will not settle or any blood in your stools. Get urgent medical help for:

  • Severe or worsening abdominal (tummy) pain
  • Fever or chills
  • Rectal bleeding that is more than a small streak, heavy bleeding or passing clots
  • Vomiting, or being unable to keep fluids down
  • A swollen, hard or very tender belly
  • Feeling very unwell, faint or confused

In an emergency call 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia or 111 in New Zealand.

Frequently asked questions

Can you eat nuts, seeds and popcorn with diverticulitis?

Usually, yes, once you are well. These foods used to be banned, but they are no longer thought to cause diverticulitis or flares, and most people with diverticula can enjoy them as part of a high-fiber diet. Small seeds in tomatoes and strawberries are fine too. Hold them back during a flare and early recovery, and if your own doctor has told you otherwise, follow that advice.

What foods should you avoid during a diverticulitis flare?

During a flare, high-fiber foods go on hold. That means whole grains, beans and lentils, raw vegetables, fruit and vegetable skins, nuts, seeds and popcorn, plus large, rich or very spicy meals. You usually start with clear liquids, then move to soft low-fiber foods like white rice, eggs and peeled potatoes. These limits are temporary, and high fiber comes back as you recover.

How long should you stay on clear liquids for diverticulitis?

Usually a few days, but your doctor decides. Clear liquids rest the bowel while keeping you hydrated. As the pain eases, you move to low-fiber food, then gradually back to high fiber over a few weeks. If you are not improving on clear liquids, or pain gets worse, or you develop fever or vomiting, contact your doctor or get urgent help.

Is a high-fiber or low-fiber diet better for diverticulitis?

Both, at different times. A low-fiber diet is used for a short while during and just after a flare to let the bowel settle. Once you have recovered, a high-fiber diet with plenty of fluids is the everyday pattern generally recommended for people with diverticula. The key is building fiber back up slowly over a few weeks rather than all at once.

What is a good breakfast for diverticulitis recovery?

It depends on your stage. On clear liquids, a mug of clear broth with weak tea works. On low fiber, try soft scrambled eggs with white toast. When stepping back up, soft polenta with cheese and a poached egg is gentle. Once fully recovered, savory oatmeal (porridge) with spinach or baked beans on whole grain toast adds plenty of fiber.

Do probiotics help diverticulitis?

The evidence is still unclear. Some people try them, but there is not yet strong proof that probiotics prevent or treat diverticulitis. If you are curious, ask your doctor or pharmacist before starting one, especially if you take other medicines. A high-fiber diet with plenty of fluids, regular activity and not smoking are the habits with the clearest everyday support.

Your first step today: if you are in a flare, follow your doctor's plan and stick to clear liquids or low-fiber food until they say otherwise. If you are well, make one fiber swap this week, such as savory oatmeal instead of white toast, add a glass of water with each meal, and freeze a few portions of clear broth so they are ready if you ever need them.
